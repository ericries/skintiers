This is the only modality that earned the top grade in the 2026 review of home dermatologic devices: "For facial rejuvenation, home-use fractional nonablative lasers demonstrated high-quality evidence (Level A) with consistent clinical improvement."[^grade] **Bottom line:** that grade is the best on the ladder at [[home-use-skincare-devices-by-evidence]], and two things stop a reader acting on it. The review names no device, and the OTC device that is actually cleared claims something much narrower than facial rejuvenation.

> **The grade covers a modality; the cleared device covers crow's feet.** The only consumer fractional laser whose FDA file this page could read is the Tria FANp, a "semiconductor diode laser system that delivers infrared light at a wavelength of 1450 nm ± 50 nm", and its cleared indication is one line: "The FANp is an over-the-counter device indicated for the treatment of periorbital wrinkles (crow's feet)."[^k141868] Not the face. The area around the eyes.

## The Evidence

**It is the top of the ladder, and it comes from a scoping review.** The review that assigned it describes its own method as "A scoping review of PubMed identified clinical studies evaluating home-use devices for acne vulgaris and facial rejuvenation", with "Levels of evidence and strength of recommendation" then assessed using GRADE.[^grade] A scoping review maps a literature rather than pooling it, so a Level A label produced this way is a weaker artifact than Level A from a meta-analysis with extractable effect sizes. The review is still the best overview available, and its own conclusion is that "Evidence supporting home-use dermatologic devices varies substantially by modality".[^grade]

**Where it sits relative to everything else in that review.** For rejuvenation, fractional nonablative lasers are Level A; "Light-emitting diodes and radiofrequency devices showed moderate evidence (Level B), while microneedling devices were supported by low-quality evidence (Level D) and associated with infectious complications".[^grade] That last clause is worth carrying: home microneedling is the one rejuvenation modality the review flags for infection, which is relevant to anyone reading [[microneedling]].

**No device is named, so the grade cannot be shopped.** The abstract identifies no manufacturer, model or wavelength behind the Level A rating. This page therefore cannot tell you that a specific purchasable device inherits it, and does not try to. That is the same problem documented across the device pages here: a modality grade is not a product grade.

## What is actually cleared for home use


**Tria Beauty holds the two OTC fractional clearances this page could find**, both filed as 510(k) Summaries: K130459 (Tria FAN System, 2013) and K141868 (Tria FANp, 2014), which names the earlier one as its predicate.[^openfda][^k141868] The FANp file states only that "Performance data was submitted with this 510(k) notification to support the determination of substantial equivalence", without characterising that data as clinical or nonclinical, which is vaguer than the reVive files discussed on [[revive-light-therapy-led-cleansing-system]].[^k141868]

**The product code does not isolate home devices.** Both Tria clearances sit under product code ONG, "Powered Laser Surgical Instrument With MicrobeamFractional Output", which holds 35 clearances in total, the great majority of them in-office platforms such as Fraxel, Clear and Brilliant, and CO2 systems.[^openfda] So you cannot use the code to find consumer devices, and a device being in this category tells you nothing about whether it is for home use.

**This is not the same thing as the in-office ablative option.** [[fractional-co2-laser-resurfacing]] is an ablative CO2 procedure performed by a clinician at settings no consumer device delivers. The Level A grade here belongs to nonablative home devices and does not transfer to it, nor the reverse.

**No tier list on this page, deliberately.** Grading entries would require either home laser product pages, which do not exist here yet, or treating the in-office CO2 page as though it were a home device, which would be wrong. The ladder across modalities is on [[home-use-skincare-devices-by-evidence]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. A device emitting infrared laser light near the eyes carries real risk; the cleared indication above is periorbital, and eye protection and manufacturer instructions matter more here than on any topical.
