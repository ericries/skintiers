Laser and light-based hair removal uses lasers (alexandrite, diode, neodymium:YAG, ruby) or intense pulsed light (IPL) to target pigment in the hair follicle and reduce unwanted hair. It is a professional device procedure, not a topical skincare step, and it is the option that reduces the hair itself, which is why it is the emerging long-term approach to [[pseudofolliculitis-barbae|razor bumps]].[^dalia]

> The honest headline from the best evidence is narrower than the marketing. A Cochrane review of the randomized trials found a short-term effect of roughly 50% hair reduction with alexandrite and diode lasers up to six months after treatment, little evidence for IPL, Nd:YAG, or ruby lasers, and no documented long-term hair removal from any of them in those trials.[^cochrane] So this reduces hair for months, but "permanent removal" overstates what the controlled evidence has actually shown.

## The Evidence

The strongest evidence synthesis is a Cochrane review of eleven randomized controlled trials involving 444 people, "none of which were of high methodological quality."[^cochrane] Its finding was specific: "There appeared to be a short-term effect of approximately 50% hair reduction with alexandrite and diode lasers up to six months after treatment, whereas little evidence was obtained for an effect of intense pulsed light, neodymium:YAG or ruby lasers," and "Long-term hair removal was not documented with any treatment."[^cochrane] In other words, the alexandrite and diode lasers have the best short-term evidence, and durable removal beyond six months was not established in the trials the review could pool.

Where it matters most is [[pseudofolliculitis-barbae|razor bumps]]: because it reduces the hair that re-enters the skin and drives the inflammation, a 2023 treatment review calls laser hair removal a "potential long-term treatment option," while noting that "additional studies are warranted to understand its long-term efficacy and permanency."[^dalia] That is the same caution the Cochrane review raises, from the other direction.

Reported adverse effects were infrequent in the trials: "Pain, skin redness, swelling, burned hairs and pigmentary changes."[^cochrane] Because these procedures use energy devices, they are carried out by trained professionals.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Device choice, the number of sessions, and safety for your skin type are decisions for a qualified practitioner; the evidence above describes in-office laser and light-based procedures.
