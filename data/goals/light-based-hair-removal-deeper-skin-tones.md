## Summary

If you have Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin and you are trying to work out whether a laser or an IPL device is safe and effective for you, the honest answer is that almost nobody has published the number you need, and the FDA clearance you might be relying on does not contain it either.

> **Bottom line.** The risk here is real and mechanistically well understood: in deeper skin, melanin in the epidermis competes with the hair follicle for the light.[^jaad] But the published efficacy and safety evidence specific to Fitzpatrick IV to VI is one prospective study of ten women, and the FDA-cleared indications for the two home IPL devices covered on this site mention no skin tone, no Fitzpatrick type and no hair colour at all.[^k243453][^k190354] The skin-tone boundary you see on a box is the manufacturer's own labelling, not a regulatory finding.

## Why deeper skin is treated differently

The mechanism is not disputed. A 2026 clinical review in the *Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology* states that in skin of colour, "increased epidermal melanin acts as a competing chromophore with a broad absorption spectrum, increasing the risk of adverse effects such as post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, hypopigmentation, blisters, and scarring."[^jaad] Light aimed at the pigment in a hair follicle is also absorbed by the pigment in the skin above it, and that absorbed energy has to go somewhere.

That is a statement about risk, not about efficacy, and it is the part of this topic that is genuinely settled. Everything downstream of it is thinner than the marketing suggests.

## The Evidence

**For Fitzpatrick IV to VI specifically, there is very little.** The most directly relevant study found for this page is a 2025 prospective clinical study in *Lasers in Medical Science* of **ten** Sudanese women with Fitzpatrick skin types IV to VI, treated three times at three-week intervals with an 808 nm diode laser.[^diode] After three sessions the authors report "the mean hair reduction was 57.9% (range 45.7-66.7%) in the underarm group and 56.7% (range 45.5-70.8%) in the chin group."[^diode] On safety they report that "one patient (skin type VI) reported pain during the first session, and one developed a mild, superficial blister at 9 J/cm², with no further complications after fluence adjustment", and that "No cases of keloid formation, scarring, wound infection, prolonged erythema, purpura, or pigmentary changes were observed."[^diode]

Ten people, three sessions, no control group and no long-term follow-up. That is encouraging and it is not a basis for a confident recommendation. The authors say so themselves: "Further research with larger cohorts and longer follow-up is recommended to optimize treatment protocols."[^diode]

**The reviews say the gap is the finding.** A 2024 systematic review in *JAMA Dermatology* of laser and light-based therapy for hirsutism concluded that its own certainty was limited in part by "few studies evaluating effectiveness and safety in those with skin of color".[^jama] The 2026 JAAD review reaches the same place from the device side: "Evidence gaps include a lack of large, prospective trials in darker skin types and standardized treatment parameters."[^jaad] When two independent reviews in the same two years both name the same absence, the absence is the result.

**The category evidence is weak before skin tone enters.** The Cochrane review of randomized trials in this area found a short-term effect of roughly 50% hair reduction with alexandrite and diode lasers up to six months, little evidence of an effect for intense pulsed light, Nd:YAG or ruby lasers, and no documented long-term hair removal from any of them: "There appeared to be a short-term effect of approximately 50% hair reduction with alexandrite and diode lasers up to six months after treatment, whereas little evidence was obtained for an effect of intense pulsed light, neodymium:YAG or ruby lasers. Long-term hair removal was not documented with any treatment."[^cochrane] The wider detail is on [[laser-hair-removal]]. Note what that means for this page: the diode laser used in the Sudanese study is one of the two with the better short-term evidence, and the 808 nm wavelength is in the family the Cochrane review had data on.

## Which wavelength, and the tension in the answer

The conventional clinical answer is the 1,064 nm Nd:YAG laser, on the physical argument that a longer wavelength is absorbed less by epidermal melanin on its way down. A 2013 review in *Dermatologic Surgery* that set out specifically to consider "Special treatment recommendations for darker skin types" states it plainly: "Because of its longer wavelength, the Nd:YAG is the best laser system to use for pigmented skin."[^gan]

Here is the tension, and this page is not going to paper over it. The Cochrane review is the stronger study design, and Nd:YAG is one of the three modalities it found little evidence for. The 2013 review is a narrative literature review, not a synthesis of randomized trials, and its own finding on long-term removal is the opposite of Cochrane's: "The literature supports the use of the alexandrite, diode, Nd:YAG and IPL devices for long-term hair removal."[^gan]

So the two best sources disagree, and they disagree in a specific way: the review that addresses skin tone is the weaker design, and the stronger design does not stratify by skin tone at all. The only prospective study found here in Fitzpatrick IV to VI used neither wavelength the Cochrane review favoured nor the Nd:YAG the 2013 review recommends for pigmented skin, but an 808 nm diode, and reported one blister.[^diode] Nobody has run the trial that would settle this.

## What the FDA clearance does and does not say

Home IPL units are cleared under product code **OHT**, "Light Based Over-The-Counter Hair Removal", regulation 21 CFR 878.4810, class II.[^oht] Two of them are covered on this site, and both clearance documents were read in full for this page.

The cleared indication for the **Philips Lumea IPL** (K243453, Philips Consumer Lifestyle B.V., 10 February 2025) reads in full: "Philips Lumea IPL is indicated for the removal of unwanted hair for a single user. It is also indicated for the permanent reduction in hair regrowth, defined as the long-term, stable reduction in the number of hairs regrowing when measured at 6, 9 and 12 months after the completion of a treatment regime."[^k243453]

The cleared indication for the **BRAUN Silk.expert Pro 5** (K190354, CyDen Limited, 18 March 2019) reads in full: "The BRAUN Silk.expert Pro 5 Hair Removal Device is indicated for the removal of unwanted hair. The BRAUN Silk.expert Pro 5 is also indicated for the permanent reduction in hair regrowth, defined as the long-term, stable reduction in the number of hairs regrowing when measured at 6, 9 and 12 months after the completion of a treatment regime."[^k190354]

Neither statement names a skin tone, a Fitzpatrick type or a hair colour. A case-insensitive search of the full extracted text of both documents for Fitzpatrick, skin type, skin tone and hair colour returned no matches in either.[^k243453][^k190354] Both documents consist of the clearance letter and the FDA Form 3881 Indications for Use enclosure; neither carries a 510(k) summary discussing patient selection.

This is the practical point. *FDA-cleared* on a home IPL box is not a statement that the device was evaluated for your skin tone. The skin-tone limit comes from the manufacturer.

## What the manufacturers actually say

Braun's own product page is the more specific of the two: "Braun IPL is suitable for a wide range of skin tones from light to medium dark skin tones, with hair ranging from natural blonde to dark brown or black. IPL technology is least suitable on very blonde, red, grey or white hair where there is less melanin to absorb the light."[^braun]

Two things about that sentence. First, it stops at "medium dark skin tones" without saying where medium dark ends, and the cached page contains no occurrence of the word Fitzpatrick, checked case-insensitively on 2026-11-02.[^braun] A reader who knows they are a V has been told nothing. Second, the boundary itself is drawn in a chart image rather than in text; the alt text accompanying it on the accessed page reads "Chart showing Skin i·expert IPL suitability for light to medium dark skin tones and hair ranging from blonde to dark brown or black", which names a sibling Braun device and repeats the same two vague words.[^braun]

Philips is less specific still. The Lumea product page accessed for [[philips-lumea-ipl-9000-series]] is a JavaScript single-page application that rendered only its title and meta description, with no suitability statement at all.

Both devices carry a skin-tone sensor that reads the skin and sets or withholds the flash. That is a safety interlock, and it is the thing actually enforcing the boundary on your arm. It is not an efficacy claim, and no independent trial of either device in any skin type was found for the product pages.

## What this means in practice

- **The in-office setting is where the only IV-to-VI data exists**, such as it is, and it is also where an operator can lower the fluence after a reaction, which is what the Sudanese study did after its one blister.[^diode] That is the basis for ranking [[laser-hair-removal]] above the home devices here, not a claim that it is well evidenced.
- **An FDA clearance tells you nothing about your skin tone.** Read the manufacturer's suitability chart, and notice that it is the manufacturer's.
- **A device's own sensor refusing to fire is information**, not a malfunction.
- **Expect reduction, not removal.** No published trial in any skin type has documented long-term hair removal; see [[laser-hair-removal]].

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted and **not** endorsed here.

- "Permanent reduction in hair regrowth". This is the regulatory phrase, and the cleared definition is narrower than it sounds: reduction "measured at 6, 9 and 12 months after the completion of a treatment regime."[^k243453] It is not permanent removal, and it was not evaluated by skin tone.
- "suitable for a wide range of skin tones from light to medium dark skin tones"[^braun] A range with an unstated upper bound, in a vocabulary that does not map onto the Fitzpatrick scale the question is usually asked in.
