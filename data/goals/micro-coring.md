An in-office procedure that removes skin rather than heating or injuring it in place: hollow needles punch out full-thickness cores, and the holes close without visible scarring. It is the one minimally invasive option that actually removes a small amount of excess skin, which is why it sits apart from [[microneedling]] and the energy devices at [[fractional-radiofrequency-microneedling]].

> **The published clinical evidence is 112 patients across five studies, and nothing compares it to anything else.** A 2025 PRISMA systematic review from Massachusetts General Hospital found eight studies in total: "three preclinical in vivo porcine studies and five clinical studies involving 112 patients."[^review] Its own closing recommendation asks for "larger patient cohorts, longer follow-up, comprehensive histological analyses, and comparisons with other minimally invasive techniques".[^review] Until those comparisons exist, nobody can say whether it beats the cheaper procedures it is sold against.

## What it is

The review describes it as "a novel, minimally invasive technology for skin rejuvenation that uses hollow needles to remove full-thickness skin cores."[^review] Most of the clinical work has been on facial wrinkles, "with emerging applications for enlarged facial pores and acne scars."[^review]

The FDA regulates it as a **powered microneedle device**, product code QAI, Class II, 21 CFR 878.4430, defined as "a device using one or more needles to mechanically puncture and injure skin tissue for aesthetic use."[^classification] Cytrellis Biosystems holds two clearances under that code: the Cytrellis Dermal Micro-Coring System, K202517, decided 2021-07-09, and the ellacor System with Micro-Coring Technology, K252752, decided 2025-10-30.[^k202517][^k252752]

Both are 510(k) clearances with the decision "Substantially Equivalent".[^k202517][^k252752] That means only that the device is enough like an already-marketed one to be sold. It says nothing about whether it works. The same route cleared the microneedling pens it is classified alongside.

## The Evidence

One systematic review, and it is candid about what it found. The eight included studies "evaluated various microcoring designs, differing in needle size, depth, number, and treatment density", which is a direct obstacle to pooling them: the studies are not testing one procedure.[^review]

On mechanism the review says current evidence indicates micro-coring "improves skin quality through mechanisms involving tissue removal, skin tightening, and epidermal and dermal regeneration/remodeling."[^review] On tolerability it reports "mild pain and trace-to-moderate bleeding during the procedure, and trace-to-moderate postprocedure adverse skin effects", and concludes the procedure "seems to be well-tolerated and safe."[^review]

No effect size is quoted here because the review's abstract reports none, and this page does not have the full text.

**No tier list on this page.** Ranking micro-coring against [[subcision]], [[microneedling]] or the laser options would require head-to-head data that the systematic review says does not exist.
