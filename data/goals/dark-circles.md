Under-eye dark circles are not one problem. A 2021 systematic review notes that "etiologies of the condition include pigmentary, structural, vascular, and mixed causes," and that the most effective treatments overall are procedures: "soft tissue fillers and autologous fat grafting are most effective" for the structural (hollowing) type, while lasers help vascular and pigment types.[^review] That matters because it decides whether skincare can help at all.

> Figure out which kind you have before spending on creams. Only the pigmentary (brown, melanin) type responds meaningfully to topicals, and even then modestly. Vascular circles (bluish, from thin skin showing blood vessels) and structural circles (shadowing from tear-trough hollowing) are better addressed by lasers or fillers, which sit outside topical skincare. For the pigmentary type, the useful topicals are retinoids plus brighteners, and, as everywhere with pigment, daily sunscreen.

## Which type do you have

A rough self-check: gently stretch the skin under the eye. If the darkness is brown and stays, it is likely pigmentary and worth treating topically. If it is bluish and fades when you stretch or press, it is vascular. If it is a shadow cast by a hollow that deepens when you look up, it is structural. Mixed cases are common, and a dermatologist can tell them apart properly.[^review]

## The Evidence

For pigmentary dark circles, the same logic as the rest of [[hyperpigmentation]] applies: brighteners and cell-turnover agents fade excess melanin slowly, and sun protection stops it coming back. Retinoids are the best-studied option and also thicken the thin under-eye skin; vitamin C, niacinamide, and azelaic acid are brightening adjuncts. None of this is dramatic, and none of it touches vascular or structural circles, where the review's most effective options are lasers and fillers.[^review] Caffeine eye products can temporarily reduce puffiness, but that is a transient depuffing effect, not a treatment for the darkness itself.
