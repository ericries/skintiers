Filler is the one acne-scar procedure that works by addition rather than by injury: it raises a depression instead of wounding the skin and waiting for collagen. How the procedures rank against each other is on [[acne-scar-procedures-by-evidence]], and what each costs in pain and recovery is on [[acne-scar-treatments-by-downtime-and-pain]]. This page covers filler specifically.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Injectable filler is a procedure performed by a medical professional, not a product, and the choice of material and depth belongs with someone examining your scars. Scarring acne that is still active is a dermatology appointment first.

## The Evidence

**The strongest finding is for filler plus a laser, not filler alone.** It comes from a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 68 randomised controlled trials and 4,480 patients, which reports that "laser + filler injection ranked best in reducing GBS (SUCRA: 72.1%)".[^nma] Note what that sentence does and does not say. It ranks a combination, so it is not evidence that filler on its own is the best choice, and its 72.1% ranking probability is visibly lower than the 98.4% that laser plus platelet-rich plasma achieved on a different scar scale in the same analysis.[^nma] The fuller reading of that ranking, including a third analysis that disputes whether any of these rankings is dependable, is on [[acne-scar-procedures-by-evidence]].

**The filler-specific review has no statistics in it.** A 2026 systematic review looked at fillers for atrophic acne scars specifically and gathered "Twenty-four studies met inclusion criteria, involving collagen-based fillers, hyaluronic acid fillers, collagen stimulator fillers, autologous fat, and decellularized matrix fillers".[^fillers] Its efficacy finding is a single sentence with no number attached: "All filler types demonstrated significant scar improvement."[^fillers] Checked by string search, the abstract contains no meta-analysis, no pooled estimate, no confidence interval, no p-value, and no mention of randomisation, controls, risk of bias or GRADE. So it tells you what has been tried and what happened, not how much better any of it is than an alternative.

**What it does give you is duration.** "Effects ranged from 3-6 months to >24 months", with "CaHA, PCL, and autologous fat/SVF demonstrated ≥6-month durability".[^fillers] That spread is the practical fact about filler for scars: it is a correction that wears off on a schedule that depends on the material, which is a different proposition from a resurfacing procedure that remodels tissue once.

**Safety reads reassuringly and should be read as uncontrolled.** "Transient erythema, edema, and ecchymosis were common. Infrequent events included nodules and hyperpigmentation. No severe complications were reported."[^fillers] Those are observations across 24 studies with no comparator and no pooled rate.

**The 95% figure is background, not a result.** The review's opening states that "More than 95% of patients develop persistent scarring" among people with acne.[^fillers] That is a framing sentence citing the wider literature rather than something these 24 studies measured, and it sits far above the scarring rates usually quoted for acne overall. It is reported here as what the review says, not adopted.

**The conclusion is written in the language of promise.** It calls fillers "a promising alternative" with "substantial clinical promise", and the authors are plastic surgeons.[^fillers] They declare no conflicts of interest. Promise is not an effect size, and a review that reports none should not be read as having found one.
