Marketing that looks like science is cheap to produce now, and the usual advice, look for clinical studies, no longer separates much. **Bottom line:** you do not need subject expertise to check a claim. You need a second browser window, and about four minutes.

> **The single highest-yield move is lateral reading.** Cosmetic chemist Michelle Wong, who holds a chemistry PhD, describes it plainly: "all it means is opening up a new browser window and searching the things in your first article or piece of content."[^wong] She notes there is research behind it in digital literacy and misinformation, and calls it "pretty much the next best thing to asking an expert or becoming an expert on that topic yourself."[^wong] Nothing below is harder than that.

## The checks, roughly in order of payoff

**Open the citations and see where they land.** A fabricated reference list usually looks correct and fails on contact. Working through one such site, Wong found that "none of the authors match up": the first reference, attributed to one author, "takes us to an article by Zhang and co-workers", and the next attributed to another "takes us to an article by Al-Atif."[^wong] You do not need to understand a paper to notice that it is not the paper that was cited.

**Search the institutions and the people, rather than only the claim.** Look up the body a quoted expert supposedly leads. If the only page that surfaces is another site in the same family, that is the finding. Wong describes the pattern as a hallucinated world: "multiple websites and experts and studies that only really exist in this little isolated microcosm where they're all linked with each other, but nothing outside."[^wong]

**Check when the websites were born.** Domain registration is public. Wong looked up a cluster of review sites on whois.com and found registrations in November 2025 and March 2026, all through the same registrar.[^wong] A publication presenting itself as an established authority, registered months ago, has told you something about itself.

**Ask whether the claim is one a cosmetic is allowed to make.** This is the check that needs a little background, and it is the one this site is built around. A product that genuinely demonstrated it changes the structure of skin would have a regulatory problem, not a marketing opportunity: as Wong puts it, brands "usually can't legally say this in their marketing unless they get it approved as a drug."[^wong] A cosmetic advertising a proven structural change is either overstating or describing a drug. [[reading-a-sunscreen-label]] is the same reasoning applied to one label format.

**Check the claims against each other.** The site Wong examined said five of 127 creams "measurably worked below the surface in lab testing" while also being "based on analysis of 857 clinical studies".[^wong] Those are different activities. Testing 127 creams yourself and reviewing 857 existing papers are not the same study, and a claim that quietly swaps between them is not describing real work.

## The Evidence

One source, read in full: a cosmetic chemist's recorded investigation of a specific cluster of marketing sites, used here for the method she demonstrates rather than for her conclusions about those sites.[^wong] The checks themselves are not her invention; lateral reading is an established technique in digital-literacy research, which she says so herself. This page cites no study of its own because it makes no claim about skin.

## What not to lean on

Tells that the text was machine-written, such as odd infographic fonts, garbled figure labels or relentlessly punchy sentences, are the weakest check and the one most likely to stop working. Wong is explicit that they are "just a red flag for investigating further" and expects them to be cleaned up as the people doing this get better at it.[^wong] Treat an AI smell as a reason to start checking, never as the verdict.

## The honest limit

Lateral reading finds fabrication. It does not grade real evidence, which is a separate and harder job: a genuine study can still be small, industry-funded, short, or measuring something that does not matter to you. That is what the rubric on each ingredient page is for.
