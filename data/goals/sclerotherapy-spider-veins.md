An injection that collapses a visible leg vein, and the standard treatment for leg spider veins rather than a fringe one. A 2026 long-term study calls sclerotherapy "the treatment of choice for telangiectasias and reticular veins of the lower limbs".[^longterm] This page is about what happens afterwards, which is the part the consultation tends to skip. Facial redness is a different problem and is at [[facial-redness-relief]].

> **At three years, almost everyone's spider veins were back.** A prospective blinded cohort followed patients for a mean of 36 months after a single session. Its result for leg telangiectasias is blunt: "In Study 1, almost all the patients had recurrence of telangiectasias, with no significant difference between treatments."[^longterm] The larger reticular veins behaved quite differently, with recurrence described as "minimal".[^longterm] If you are treating fine surface vessels, plan for maintenance rather than a cure.

## What it involves

A sclerosant is injected into the vessel. The study above compared two of them, "0.2% polidocanol in 70% hypertonic glucose (PDHG)" and "75% hypertonic glucose (HG) alone", in 43 patients for telangiectasias and 35 for reticular veins.[^longterm] For the fine vessels neither agent beat the other on recurrence; for reticular veins the polidocanol mixture did, at p = 0.016.[^longterm]

## The Evidence

**Recurrence depends on vessel size, not on the agent.** That is the single most useful finding here, and it comes from the three-year data above.[^longterm]

**The common side effects mostly settle.** The same study reports that "Pigmentation resolved mainly over time, with no significant intergroup or interstudy differences", while matting "was more persistent in telangiectasias but nearly disappeared in reticular veins".[^longterm] Matting, meaning a new blush of fine vessels where the old ones were, is the complication worth asking about.

**How often those side effects happen is measurable, and sclerotherapy is the comparator arm.** A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of six studies and 833 patients compared cryolaser cryosclerotherapy against isolated sclerotherapy, pooling three directly comparative studies covering "363 CLaCS vs 259 sclerotherapy".[^meta] Relative to the combined technique, plain sclerotherapy had significantly more matting, at a risk ratio of "0.07 [95% confidence interval [CI], 0.01-0.36]" in the combined arm's favour, more microthrombi at "0.18 [95% CI, 0.06-0.54]", and more bruising at "0.43 [95% CI, 0.32-0.58]".[^meta] For hyperpigmentation there was no significant difference and the heterogeneity was extreme, at "I2 = 92.1%".[^meta]

Those numbers compare adverse events. They say nothing about which technique clears more veins. The review's own clearance figure is single-arm and applies to the combined technique, not to sclerotherapy, and it flags "the need for trials with standardized outcome measures".[^meta]

**No tier list on this page.** The obvious comparator, cryolaser cryosclerotherapy, has no page here, and the one pooled comparison between them measured side effects rather than clearance. Ranking them on that would misrepresent what was measured.
