Home radiofrequency sits in the middle of the ladder at [[home-use-skincare-devices-by-evidence]]: "Light-emitting diodes and radiofrequency devices showed moderate evidence (Level B), while microneedling devices were supported by low-quality evidence (Level D) and associated with infectious complications."[^grade] **Bottom line:** that grade is better documented than the LED one, because a real clinical study sits behind it rather than bench testing. It is also thinner than twelve cleared devices makes it sound, because they trace back to the same single uncontrolled study, and because tightening is not what any of them is cleared to treat.

> **The category is named for tightening and cleared for wrinkles.** CurrentBody lists its device's Common/Usual Name with the FDA as an "OTC device for skin tightening based on RF", while the indication FDA actually cleared reads "an over-the-counter home use device intended for non-invasive treatment of mild to moderate facial wrinkles for adults with Fitzpatrick skin types I-IV".[^k232424] Wrinkles, in one population. The words tighten and laxity do not appear anywhere in the FDA decision summary for the device that created this whole category.[^denovo]

## The Evidence

**One clinical study holds up the category.** The [[newa-skin-therapy-system]] was granted in December 2015 as De Novo DEN150005, which means FDA reviewed it on its own evidence instead of by comparison to an older device, and wrote a new classification around it. That evidence was a "prospective, single center study" of 69 enrolled and 62 completing, all female, in which three independent dermatologists scored photographs for "at least one degree of wrinkle score reduction": 95.16% (59/62) passed at four weeks and 100% (62/62) at three months.[^denovo] There was no sham or control arm, and a 100% success rate against a one-grade threshold is what uncontrolled before-and-after scoring reliably produces. The device page has the full read.

**The other eleven devices did not repeat it, and say so plainly.** FDA has classified 12 devices under product code PAY, and eleven of them are ordinary 510(k) clearances that argued substantial equivalence to something already in the category.[^pay] The CurrentBody Skin RF (K232424, cleared 2024) is explicit about what that means: "No clinical testing was performed with the subject device, as it is substantially equivalent to the predicate device, and thus requires no additional clinical data to prove its safety and efficacy."[^k232424] What was submitted instead was bench work, including a porcine skin experiment whose result was that the new device heats tissue like the old one: "Measurements showed comparable (near identical) spatio-temporal tissue heating between the subject and predicate devices."[^k232424]

**The chain does not even run back to the study directly.** CurrentBody's predicate is the "Pollogen Stop U Model UXV device, cleared under 510(k) number K220322 on May 4th, 2023", not the NEWA.[^k232424] So a 2024 device inherits its place in the category from a 2023 device, which inherited it from an earlier one. Each link is a demonstration of equivalence, and none of the links adds clinical evidence about wrinkles. This is the general weakness of the pathway, covered at [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

**Two of the twelve publish nothing about their testing.** Ten of the files are filed as 510(k) Summaries, which publish a description of the data. The two exceptions are both FOREO's: FAQ 101 (K222012) and FAQ 102 (K240616) are filed as Statements, which certify only that the firm will supply information on request.[^pay] Those two devices are discussed at [[foreo-faq-led-masks]], where the same pattern runs through the rest of that line.

**Deeper skin tones are outside the cleared indication.** Both indications read here stop at Fitzpatrick type IV.[^denovo][^k232424] The two also differ on who counts as a user: the NEWA grant specifies "adult women", while CurrentBody's reads "adults".[^denovo][^k232424] Neither restriction is a safety finding about anyone, only a record of the population each submission studied and claimed.

**What FDA calls this category.** The classification record for product code PAY gives the device name as "Over-The-Counter Radiofrequency Coagulation Device For Wrinkle Reduction", regulation 21 CFR 878.4420, class 2.[^pay] Coagulation is a surgical word, and it is a useful corrective to the spa framing: these devices work by heating tissue, which is also why the NEWA label carries a warning about treating skin with facial hair still on it.[^denovo]

## What this means if you are shopping

**The modality grade is real, so this is not a category to dismiss.** The review's conclusion puts it among the better home options: "Fractional lasers, LED, and radiofrequency devices demonstrate the most favorable balance of efficacy and safety".[^grade] Expect roughly what the one study measured, which is about one grade of wrinkle change on a photographic scale, and expect it to stop when you stop treating.

**Buying a different device in the category does not buy the study.** Only the NEWA's evidence was reviewed as clinical evidence. A newer or cheaper PAY device is in the same regulatory class and may well heat skin the same way, which is the part that was tested.

No tier list sits on this page, because only one home radiofrequency device has a page here so far. The cross-modality ranking is at [[home-use-skincare-devices-by-evidence]], and the goals people buy these for are [[skin-firmness-elasticity]] and [[anti-aging]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. These devices deposit heat in skin, the cleared indications cover mild to moderate facial wrinkles rather than any medical condition, and manufacturer instructions on treatment areas and facial hair exist because of findings in the files cited below.
