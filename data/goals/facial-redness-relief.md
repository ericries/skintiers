"Facial redness" is not one condition, it's a symptom, and the cause determines what actually helps. In someone with recurring centrofacial flushing and visible blood vessels, the cause is likely [[rosacea]] and specifically its erythematotelangiectatic form ([[rosacea-erythematotelangiectatic]]). In someone with flaky, salmon-pink patches around the nose, brows, and hairline, it's more likely [[seborrheic-dermatitis]]. Itchy, dry, ill-defined redness points to [[atopic-dermatitis]] or a reaction to a specific product ([[contact-dermatitis]]). And redness that flares with new products, weather, or over-exfoliation without a clear rash pattern is often just a barrier problem: see [[sensitive-skin]]. A single sunburn is its own transient cause and resolves with time and sun avoidance, not with the treatments below.

## Working out the cause

Look for the pattern behind the color, not the color alone. Redness with visible thread-like vessels, flushing triggered by heat, alcohol, or spicy food, and a centrofacial (nose, cheeks, chin) distribution points to rosacea.[^dermnet] Redness with greasy scale concentrated in the eyebrows, nasolabial folds, and scalp margin points to seborrheic dermatitis. Redness that appeared after switching products, with stinging or burning on application, points to a barrier or contact reaction rather than a chronic inflammatory disease. If the redness doesn't fit any of these, or is spreading, painful, or accompanied by pustules or skin thickening, that's a reason to see a dermatologist rather than keep testing products, since these causes are treated differently and some (rosacea, contact dermatitis) benefit from a diagnosis before you spend money.

## What is happening in the skin

Redness itself is just increased blood flow to the skin, whether from actual vasodilation (temporary flushing) or from new, persistently widened vessels (telangiectasia) that don't fully constrict back down.[^dermnet] In rosacea, dysregulated inflammation, vasodilation, and new vessel growth work together, and triggers like UV, heat, exercise, spicy food, alcohol, and stress push the process further via mechanisms including a vasodilating peptide (CGRP).[^dermnet] In barrier-impaired or irritated skin, the redness instead comes from an inflammatory response to a compromised surface, allowing more irritants and allergens through, which is why fixing the barrier calms the redness even without treating a specific disease.

## What actually works

Two things are true at once: the biggest, fastest win for most non-rosacea redness is repairing the barrier and easing off irritation, via [[skin-barrier-repair]] and consistent [[moisturizing]] with a bland, fragrance-free product. And when the cause is rosacea, the best-evidenced topical redness treatment is [[azelaic-acid]], with [[niacinamide]] as a gentler adjunct. Daily broad-spectrum [[sunscreen-uv-filters|sunscreen]] matters for both paths, since UV is a documented rosacea trigger and also directly damages the barrier. What is genuinely out of scope here: persistent visible blood vessels and background erythema from rosacea often need topical [[brimonidine|brimonidine]] (a vasoconstrictor with same-day but rebound-prone effect) or vascular laser/IPL to actually reduce the vessels themselves; both are medical or in-office treatments, not something a skincare routine resolves.[^dermnet]

## What's overhyped

No serum "de-reddens" skin the way a laser or a diagnosis-matched prescription does. Cooling gels, "redness-correcting" green-tinted primers, and most redness-branded moisturizers are camouflage or mild soothing at best; they don't change the underlying vessels or inflammation. If a product claims to calm redness in days regardless of cause, treat that as marketing, not evidence.

## The Evidence

DermNet's rosacea reference describes the mechanism as dysregulated inflammation with "vasodilation," "lymphatic dilatation, and angiogenesis," lists trigger factors including "ultraviolet (UV) radiation, temperature change, exercise, spicy foods, alcohol, psychological stress, air pollution, and tobacco smoking," and separates "transient erythema (flushing)" from "persistent erythema," the latter treated with brimonidine/oxymetazoline or vascular laser rather than topicals.[^dermnet] For the strongest topical option when rosacea is the cause, an independent 251-patient RCT found azelaic acid 15% gel patients rated their erythema improved more often than metronidazole 0.75% gel patients (56% vs 42%, P=.02), detailed with its citation on the [[azelaic-acid]] page. The barrier-repair mechanism for non-rosacea redness rests on the Cochrane review cited on [[moisturizing]], which found moisturizing reduced eczema flares (risk ratio 0.40).

## Medical note

This is an educational summary, not medical advice. Most of the common causes of facial redness, including rosacea, seborrheic dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, and allergic or irritant contact dermatitis, are medical diagnoses that only a clinician can confirm and treat, and the interventions with the strongest evidence for persistent redness (topical brimonidine or oxymetazoline, and vascular laser or IPL) are prescription or in-office treatments that fall outside a skincare routine. Redness that is spreading, painful, weeping, accompanied by pustules or skin thickening, or that involves the eyes is worth having assessed rather than self-managed with more products. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.
