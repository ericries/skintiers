The honest answer is that it depends what you asked the product to do, and the gap between the fastest and slowest answers is enormous. **A hydrating serum shows you something today. A retinoid may need six months.** Judging the second by the first's clock is the commonest way people conclude that skincare does not work.

## The Evidence

**Where the six-month figure comes from.**

It sits on an FDA label, which is unusual for a number this widely repeated. The prescribing information for a 0.02 percent tretinoin cream states: "Up to 6 months of therapy may be required before the effects are seen."[^renova] The trials behind that label ran 24 weeks, and their own results explain the caution: at the end, 40 percent of patients on the drug were graded as no change, against 58 percent on the vehicle.[^renova]

Read the second number too. More than half the people using a cream with no active in it were also unchanged, which is the comparison that makes the first number meaningful, and the same label is blunt that the product "DOES NOT ELIMINATE WRINKLES, REPAIR SUN-DAMAGED SKIN, REVERSE PHOTOAGING, or RESTORE MORE YOUTHFUL or YOUNGER SKIN".[^renova] What tretinoin does and does not do is graded on [[tretinoin]].

**The thing nobody can source: purging.**

The standard advice is to push through an initial worsening because the product is bringing existing congestion to the surface. That advice is everywhere, and it is not in the literature under that name.

Searched on 2026-11-08: a PubMed query for acne purging returns zero records, and a query for retinoid or tretinoin with purging or an initial or paradoxical flare in acne also returns zero.[^pubmed] A looser search for skin purging returns 122 records, and the top results are about radiation dosimetry, chemical-warfare decontamination and eating disorders, so the term is matching homonyms rather than the skincare idea.[^pubmed]

**What does exist is adjacent and narrower.** Searching for an initial acne flare with retinoids returns a small number of records about flares following isotretinoin, which is an oral drug and a different situation from starting a topical.[^pubmed] So the honest position is that early worsening on a topical is widely reported by clinicians and users, and that the specific framing of purging as a distinct, self-limiting process is not something a search of the indexed literature supports or refutes. Absence of an indexed term is not absence of a phenomenon, and this page claims only what was searched.

**A rule that survives the uncertainty.**

If a product is irritating you, the question is not how long to endure it. Stopping tells you something immediately and costs nothing but time. The waiting game is for products that are comfortable and simply have not visibly done anything yet, which is where the timelines above apply.
