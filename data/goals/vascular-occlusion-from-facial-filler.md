The serious risk of any facial filler is not the filler but the needle: injecting it into an artery instead of beside one. This page is the safety counterpart to [[dermal-fillers-for-acne-scars]], which covers whether fillers work on scars and says nothing about this. It is written for someone deciding whether and with whom to be injected, not as instruction for performing injections.

> **What it is, in the reviewers' own words.** "Significant complications are uncommon with aesthetic hyaluronic acid (HA) dermal fillers, but inadvertent injection of dermal filler into an artery can result in tissue damage, scarring, visual loss, and even stroke."[^lowe] Uncommon is doing real work in that sentence, and so is can.

## The Evidence

**Where it happens, from 243 published cases.** A systematic review collected "243 cases" of filler-induced facial skin ischaemia with "738 digital clinical photographs retrieved". The "facial artery (58% of cases) and ophthalmic artery (48% of cases) angiosomes were most commonly affected", and, in its own words, "The frontonasal and angulonasal territories were the most common facial skin segments injured by filler-induced vascular occlusion".[^soares] In plain terms, the bridge and sides of the nose and the area between the brows account for most of the published damage.

**The number to read carefully.** In that review, "Cutaneous involvement of the ophthalmic angiosome was significantly associated with neuro-ophthalmologic complications [vision loss, 39% versus 0.8% ( P = 0.00001); stroke, 8% versus 0.8% ( P = 0.0085)]".[^soares] Those percentages are NOT the risk of filler. They are the rate of vision loss among published occlusion cases that already showed skin involvement of the ophthalmic territory, against cases that did not. Read as a population risk they would be wildly wrong; read correctly they say that where on the face the skin changes appear predicts how bad the rest of the injury is.

**What the evidence supports for reducing it.** A 2026 review using the BestBETs methodology, scoped to peripheral cutaneous occlusion and "explicitly excluding ocular and neurological complications", found the preventative measures with support to be "practitioner skill and knowledge; cannula usage; microboluses of small filler volumes, low plunger pressure, and constant needle tip movement".[^lowe] For acute management it backs "the liberal usage of hyaluronidase, heat, and massage, and supports the addition of antiplatelet agents".[^lowe]

**One widely used precaution does not work.** The same review states plainly that "Aspiration is controversial and cannot reliably exclude intravascular needle placement".[^lowe] Pulling back on the plunger to check for blood is a reassurance ritual, not a test, and a patient should not treat it as evidence of care.

**Why anatomy cannot be assumed.** Mapping the artery before injecting is advocated because its course varies: pooled Doppler data put the facial artery medial to the nasolabial fold in 46.2% of cases, crossing it in 22.5% and lateral in 12.0%, and ultrasound found it at visualisation rates of 100%, 99.9% and 99.8% across three levels. The full treatment, including the arithmetic problems in those pooled figures, is on [[pourani-2025-facial-artery-doppler-mapping-pmid-40874402]].[^pourani]

## What This Page Does Not Tell You

No incidence figure. None of the three sources here gives a rate of occlusion per procedure or per injector, and the 243-case review is a collection of published cases, which is a denominator-free sample biased toward the photogenic and the severe. Anyone quoting a filler occlusion rate needs a source that none of these three is.

## Common Marketing Claims

> No clinic or brand copy is quoted here, and none should be read into the above.

Two claims a reader is likely to meet and can now weigh. That a practitioner aspirates before injecting is presented as a safety credential; the 2026 review says it cannot reliably exclude intravascular placement.[^lowe] That hyaluronic acid filler is "dissolvable" and therefore low risk is true of the filler and not of an occlusion: hyaluronidase is the reviewed treatment, and the reviews describe prompt recognition as critical rather than optional.[^lowe]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and emphatically not a technique guide. Facial injection is a clinician procedure and the measures above describe what the literature asks of injectors, not anything a reader should attempt. Sudden severe pain, blanching, mottling or any change in vision during or after filler is an emergency: seek immediate medical care from the injector or an emergency department rather than waiting, because the reviewed management is time-critical.
