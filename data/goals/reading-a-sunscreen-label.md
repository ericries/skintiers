In the United States a sunscreen is a drug, not a cosmetic, so it carries a Drug Facts panel with fixed headings: Active ingredients with their percentages, Purpose, Uses, Warnings, Directions and Inactive ingredients.[^mineral][^chemical] Almost everything a shopper argues about lives in the first and last of those, and the words brands argue about are in neither.

> **The panel does not know what "mineral" means.** On a product sold as a mineral sunscreen, the panel reads "Active Ingredient Zinc Oxide, 20.2% Titanium Dioxide, 6.8%" with "Purpose Sunscreen".[^mineral] On an organic-filter product the same two columns read "Avobenzone (3%) Sunscreen Homosalate (10%) Sunscreen Octisalate (5%) Sunscreen Octocrylene (10%) Sunscreen", four actives each carrying the same purpose.[^chemical] Same heading, same Purpose, different chemistry. The word mineral appears on the first product in its name, not as a category the label system recognises. Filter chemistry is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and [[zinc-oxide]].

## What each part of the panel is actually telling you

**Active ingredients and percentages.** These are the UV filters and the only ingredients the product is regulated on. Percentages are the lever that matters most and the one marketing rarely leads with.

**Purpose.** For both labels read here the Purpose against every active is the single word "Sunscreen".[^mineral][^chemical] A product cannot claim a second drug purpose from a filter.

**Uses.** This is boilerplate and worth reading once, because it states a conditional most people drop. The organic-filter label reads: "helps prevent sunburn if used as directed with other sun protection measures (see Directions ), decreases the risk of skin cancer and early skin aging caused by the sun", with the stray space before the bracket as the record prints it.[^chemical] The mineral label carries the same claim.[^mineral] Sunburn prevention is the standalone claim; the cancer and ageing claim is conditional on using it as directed alongside other measures, and the label's own Sun Alert section ties it to a broad spectrum SPF of 15 or higher plus shade, clothing and hats.[^mineral]

**Inactive ingredients.** Everything else. This is where the ingredients a brand advertises usually sit, and sitting there means they carry no drug claim at all. The organic-filter product read for this page lists niacinamide and tocopheryl acetate among its inactives.[^chemical] [[niacinamide]] has real evidence of its own; what it does not have, in that bottle, is a regulated role.

## What a mineral claim does not guarantee

It does not guarantee a higher SPF, better broad-spectrum coverage, or that the formula is otherwise simple. The mineral product read here declares more than twenty inactive ingredients including silicones, a crosspolymer and aluminium hydroxide.[^mineral] It does not guarantee an absence of nanoparticles, which the panel does not address either way. And it does not guarantee a gentler formula, since the inactive list is where fragrance and preservatives would appear.

What it does tell you, reliably, is which two filters are doing the work, because they are named with percentages in the actives.

## One reason to read the panel rather than trust it absolutely

These labels are submitted by the manufacturer. The mineral label read for this page prints its own heading as "Inactive igredients", misspelled, in the structured product label record.[^mineral] A typo changes nothing about the formula, but it is a reminder that nobody at the agency is proofreading the panel for you.
