"Even skin tone" is not one problem. It covers at least three different things happening in the skin, and they call for different treatments: brown patches of excess pigment ([[hyperpigmentation]], often [[melasma]]), flat pink or red marks left behind after a spot or inflammation heals (post-inflammatory erythema, most visible in lighter skin and covered under [[acne-scars]]), and a general dull or uneven texture that is mostly a byproduct of sun damage and slowed cell turnover. A landmark review on post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation notes it "is a common sequelae of inflammatory dermatoses that tends to affect darker skinned patients with greater frequency and severity."[^davis] Melasma, meanwhile, is increasingly understood as photoaging rather than a purely hormonal condition: "melasma might be a photoaging skin disorder affecting genetically predisposed individuals."[^passeron] That shared photoaging mechanism is why sun protection sits underneath every branch of this goal.

## Figure out which one you actually have

- **Brown, tan, or dark patches**, often symmetric on the cheeks/forehead or shaped like a prior blemish: this is pigment. Go to [[hyperpigmentation]] for the general workup and [[melasma]] if it is hormonally-triggered and mask-like. [[brightening]] ranks the depigmenting actives (hydroquinone, tranexamic acid, azelaic acid, and more) head to head.
- **Pink or red flat marks**, usually fading over months, most visible in lighter skin: this is post-inflammatory erythema, not pigment, and topicals do less for it than time and sun protection do. See the PIE section of [[acne-scars]] rather than reaching for a brightening serum.
- **Generally dull, uneven-looking skin with no distinct patches**: this is usually a mix of sun damage, slowed cell turnover, and dehydration rather than a discrete condition. [[retinoids]] and diligent [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] use do more here than any single "glow" product.

## What actually works

Sun protection is not optional groundwork; the trials behind every pigment treatment below assume it. From there, [[retinoids]] and [[azelaic-acid]] have the deepest evidence across both pigment and marks, [[niacinamide]] and [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]] are gentler, better-tolerated options with real but more modest trial support. None of these fix red marks quickly; that mostly needs time.

This is an educational summary, not medical advice. A single dark spot that is changing, asymmetric, growing, or newly appeared is a reason to see a board-certified dermatologist rather than treat it as cosmetic pigment, and prescription options and in-office procedures are decided with a qualified clinician.

## The Evidence

Postinflammatory hyperpigmentation is common and disproportionately affects darker skin: "Epidemiological studies show that dyschromias, including postinflammatory hyperpigmentation, are among the most common reasons darker racial/ethnic groups seek the care of a dermatologist."[^davis] Melasma's link to photoaging reframes prevention around consistent broad-spectrum sun protection rather than only hormonal management.[^passeron] Ranked head-to-head efficacy data for the individual actives, hydroquinone, azelaic acid, tranexamic acid, niacinamide, vitamin C, and others, lives on [[hyperpigmentation]] and [[brightening]] and is not repeated here.
