Chemical peels are the cheapest and most widely available of the acne-scar procedures, and in two separate network meta-analyses a peel combination came out on top.[^li][^wu] **Bottom line:** the pooled evidence favours pairing a peel with microneedling or laser rather than using either alone, but the most recent analysis says the literature is too thin to rank anything reliably, and none of it tells you which acid or what strength.[^liu] For the ranking across all procedures see [[acne-scar-procedures-by-evidence]]; for the scar types themselves see [[acne-scars]].

## What the evidence shows, and where it disagrees

**The case for peels is a case for combinations.** The 2024 analysis pooled 24 studies and 1,546 participants and found that microneedling "combined with chemical peels (CP) exhibited the best results in terms of degree of improvement, patient satisfaction, and treatment efficacy compared to other treatments examined", a list that included microneedling alone, microneedling with hyaluronic acid, with botulinum toxin A, with platelet-rich plasma, platelet-rich plasma alone, peels alone, and laser.[^li] It also reports the more general point that "The results for MN combined with additional treatments were obviously better than for MN alone."[^li] Separately, the 2025 analysis of 68 trials found laser plus chemical peels ranked best for patient satisfaction at a SUCRA of 89.6%.[^wu]

**The case against trusting that ranking is stronger than it looks.** A 2026 review restricted itself to records with verifiable source tables so its numbers could be reproduced, and found only "Nine randomized trials, 19 treatment arms, 11 direct comparisons, and 10 nodes" forming "a sparse connected network".[^liu] In it, microneedling plus chemical peel did have a favourable point estimate against laser alone, "SMD -0.14, 95% CI -1.34-1.07", but that interval comfortably includes no difference, as did every other combination it examined.[^liu] Its own verdict is blunt: "sensitivity analyses changed the top-ranked treatment; therefore, P-scores were treated only as descriptive exploratory statistics and a reliable treatment ranking does not currently exist", with comparisons rated "low or very low confidence".[^liu] That review discloses that it did not systematically search Embase, CENTRAL, Web of Science, Scopus or the trial registries, so it is narrower than the other two by design; it is better read as a reproducibility check on them than as a replacement.[^liu]

**Nobody has published which peel, at what strength.** None of the three analyses names a peel agent or a depth in its abstract. That is the question a reader actually has, and this page will not invent an answer to it. The acids this site covers that appear in peel formulations are [[glycolic-acid]], [[salicylic-acid]], [[lactic-acid]] and [[mandelic-acid]], but their pages grade them as topicals, which is a different exposure from an in-office peel.

**Nothing here protects you from the known risks, and nothing here is worse either.** The 2024 analysis reports that "Side effects such as erythema, pain, and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation showed no significant differences across all treatments assessed", and the 2025 analysis found no strong evidence that any treatment lowered erythema or post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation risk.[^li][^wu] Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation after a peel is the risk that matters most in deeper skin tones, and the absence of a difference between treatments is not the same as the absence of risk.

**Scar type is the gap behind all of this.** The 2026 review notes that "No included trial reported treatment effects by scar morphology, so phenotype-based comparative efficacy could not be tested."[^liu] Icepick, rolling and boxcar scars are structurally different problems, and no pooled evidence yet tells you whether a peel combination suits yours.

## Which peel agent, and at what strength

**Adding Jessner's solution to TCA did not measurably beat TCA alone.** The combination is widely described as the stronger option, and the one trial here that tested it head to head did not confirm that. Fifty patients were split evenly between Jessner's followed by 20% TCA and 20% TCA alone; marked improvement reached 60% of the combination group against 28% on TCA alone, yet "the difference in improvement of acne scars was not statistically significant in both the groups (P value > 0.05)".[^puri] Twenty-five patients per arm is small enough that a real difference of that size could hide, so treat the combination as untested rather than disproved. The paper itself attributes the superiority claim to Monheit's original description rather than to trial data.[^puri]

**Doubling TCA strength bought downtime, not results.** For the CROSS technique, which drives high-concentration TCA into individual scars, 40 patients were randomised to 70% or 100% TCA. The authors report "No significant differences in overall efficacy were observed between the two treatment groups", while "the crusting time following the procedure was longer in the group treated with 100% TCA".[^soysal] The most useful finding sits inside the 100% arm: efficacy was significantly greater for icepick scars than for boxcar scars.[^soysal] That is a partial answer to the morphology question above, from one trial rather than pooled data, and it points the same way as [[acne-scars]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Medium and deeper peels are clinical procedures with real risks of scarring and pigment change, and a peel is not a reasonable first move for active [[acne]] or for scars you have not had assessed.
