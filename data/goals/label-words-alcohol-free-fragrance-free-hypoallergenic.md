Three of the most reassuring words on a cosmetic label, and the FDA's own position on what they require. **Bottom line:** one has a conventional meaning narrower than you think, one has no federal definition at all, and the third the FDA does not address.

> **"The term means whatever a particular company wants it to mean."** That is the FDA on hypoallergenic, verbatim. Its page states that "There are no Federal standards or definitions that govern the use of the term" and that manufacturers "are not required to submit substantiation of their hypoallergenicity claims to FDA".[^hypo] It goes further than neutrality: the term "may have considerable market value in promoting cosmetic products to consumers on a retail basis, but dermatologists say it has very little meaning".[^hypo]

## How to know you have this

### Hypoallergenic: no standard, and a court said so

The FDA tried to fix this and lost. Its own historical note records that "A Federal court has struck down an FDA regulation requiring cosmetic manufacturers to conduct tests to back up any claim that a product is" hypoallergenic, and that "The decision means the term has no real meaning in the marketplace"[^hypo] The agency's current page has stood since October 2000.[^hypo]

What this does not mean is that hypoallergenic products are worse, or that brands using the word are lying. It means the word carries no testing requirement, so it tells you about a company's positioning rather than about a product. If you react to things, [[patch-testing]] identifies what you react to and the word on the box does not.

### Alcohol-free: a real convention, narrower than it sounds

This one has a settled industry meaning and the FDA explains it. The term has long been used for products "that do not contain ethyl alcohol (also known as ethanol, or grain alcohol)", and in cosmetic labelling the term alcohol, "used by itself, refers to ethyl alcohol".[^alc]

So a product can be accurately labelled alcohol-free and still contain alcohols. The FDA names them: products "may contain other alcohols, such as cetyl, stearyl, cetearyl, or lanolin alcohol", which are fatty alcohols whose "effects on the skin are quite different from those of ethyl alcohol".[^alc] It also notes that isopropyl alcohol, the one many people picture, "is rarely used in cosmetics".[^alc]

[[altreno-tretinoin-0-05-lotion]] is the worked example on this site: marketed as alcohol-free while its FDA label lists [[benzyl-alcohol]] among the inactive ingredients. Defensible under the convention above, and not what most buyers hear.

### Fragrance-free: the FDA does not define it here

The FDA's Cosmetics Labeling Claims section covers five terms: alcohol free, cosmeceutical, cruelty free, hypoallergenic and organic.[^alc] Fragrance-free is not among them, and neither claims page read for this entry mentions it.[^alc][^hypo]

That is a statement about those pages rather than about all of US cosmetic regulation, and this page does not claim the term is unregulated everywhere. What it does mean is that the obvious place to look for a federal definition does not supply one. In practice the distinction people most need is between fragrance-free and unscented, which the next section sources, and neither word promises an absence of the fragrance allergens that [[contact-dermatitis]] turns on.

### Unscented: a masking fragrance is still fragrance

The FDA says directly that products labelled `unscented` may still contain fragrance ingredients, and gives the reason: "This is because the manufacturer may add just enough fragrance to mask the unpleasant smell of other ingredients, without giving the product a noticeable scent."[^frag] Unscented is a claim about what the product smells like, not about what is in it.

Two things follow from the same page, and they matter more than the adjective. On a US ingredient list the single word `Fragrance` can stand for an entire mixture, because "FDA requires the list of ingredients under the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act (FPLA)" and that law cannot be used to compel a trade secret, while "Fragrance and flavor formulas are complex mixtures of many different natural and synthetic chemical ingredients".[^frag] And the agency states its own limit plainly: "FDA does not have the same legal authority to require allergen labeling for cosmetics as for food", its advice being that a reader worried about sensitivity "may want to choose products that are fragrance free, and check the ingredient list carefully".[^frag]

So on a US label the specific sensitisers need not appear by name at all. [[linalool]], [[limonene]], [[geraniol]] and [[citronellol]] are the four graded here, and any of them can sit inside that one word.


## The Evidence

Two FDA pages, read in full on 2026-11-18. No study is cited because this page makes no claim about skin: every statement here is about what a word is required to mean.[^hypo][^alc] The agency's hypoallergenic page carries a currency note of October 2000 and says it is "updated only as needed", so it is long-standing policy rather than fresh guidance.[^hypo]

## What to do instead

Read the ingredient list, which is required, rather than the adjectives on the front, which are not. [[reading-a-sunscreen-label]] applies the same habit to one label format, and [[patch-testing]] is what settles an allergy question that no label claim can.
