There is very little that is biologically special about "men's skincare." A 2018 review of male versus female skin found that hydration, transepidermal water loss, sebum, microcirculation, pigmentation, and thickness are generally higher in men, while skin pH is higher in women.[^skin] So a man's facial skin is on average oilier and slightly thicker, which nudges toward lighter, oil-tolerant textures, but the ingredients that actually work are the same regardless of sex. Products marketed "for men" are typically conventional formulas differentiated mainly by scent and packaging rather than by ingredient.

## The routine

The evidence-based core is short and identical to what this site recommends for anyone: a gentle cleanser, a moisturizer, and daily sunscreen are the non-negotiables (the moisturizer case is on [[moisturizing]]; the sunscreen evidence and filters are on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]). For the best-evidenced anti-aging step, add a [[retinoids|retinoid]] at night and build the habit slowly. Treat specific concerns directly rather than by gender: [[acne]] for breakouts, [[oily-skin]] for shine and texture, and [[anti-aging]] for lines and firmness. For a step-by-step order, the [[am-pm-routine-by-evidence]] routine applies unchanged, and the case for keeping it short is on [[minimalist-routine]]. The actives above are what carry the evidence; elaborate multi-step or "for men" kits add cost without adding evidenced benefit.

## Shaving

Shaving is the one genuinely male-skewed factor. A razor abrades the skin and can leave it irritated, and recurring razor bumps (pseudofolliculitis barbae) are common with coarse or curly beard hair, where a cut hair curls back and re-enters the skin. A sharp, clean blade, enough lubrication, and not shaving too closely reduce irritation more than any "men's" product does; persistent or inflamed razor bumps are worth a dermatologist's help.
