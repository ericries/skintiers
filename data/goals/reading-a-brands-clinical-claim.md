Most skincare studies are real. That is the problem: real and uncontrolled, real and run by the seller, or real and impossible to find. **Bottom line:** three questions separate a claim you can weigh from one you cannot, and none of them requires understanding the science.

> **The commonest failure is not fraud, it is an absent control group.** [[spotting-a-fabricated-clinical-claim]] covers studies that were invented. This page covers the far more frequent case where the study happened, the numbers are honest, and it still cannot tell you the product works, because nothing was compared against anything.

## The three questions

### 1. Was there a control arm?

Without one, improvement over twelve weeks tells you that twelve weeks passed. Skin changes with season, routine, attention and regression to the mean, and a single-arm study captures all of that as if it were the product.

[[gopaul-2010-topical-salicin-skin-aging-pmid-20883292]] is the clean example on this site: a company study of its own ingredient, thirty women, no control arm of any kind, concluding the ingredient works.

A control arm is not always a placebo. The strongest version compares against an active alternative, as [[strataderm-silicone-scar-gel]] did against a pressure garment, or lets each patient serve as their own control by treating one side. When a brand's study has no comparator at all, that is usually a choice rather than an impossibility.

### 2. Who employed the authors?

Not disqualifying, and worth knowing. Industry funds most cosmetic science, and the realistic alternative to a company study is usually no study.

What matters is whether you can see the arrangement. [[jeon-2023-salix-alba-bark-extract-dark-circles-pmid-37235713]] lists all eight authors at the company selling the ingredient, which is transparent enough to discount accurately. [[ingredient-evidence-by-who-ran-the-only-trial]] ranks four cases on exactly this and finds the declared company study is the best of them, because the alternatives are worse in a specific way: disclosures that contradict each other, or a study whose independence is asserted and unverifiable.

The good version exists too. The trial behind [[strataderm-silicone-scar-gel]] was run by university researchers, with the manufacturer paying only the open-access publication fee and the disclosure spelling out that it had no part in design, data collection or analysis.

### 3. Can you actually find the study?

A claim with no reference is not checkable, and the word doing the most work in such claims is often the one you cannot verify. [[kravebeauty-great-barrier-relief]] is sold partly on "an independent clinical study on 20 participants" with no citation given.[^krave] Independent is precisely the part an uncited study cannot demonstrate.

To its credit the brand states the sample size rather than hiding it, and twenty is small enough that the number is not flattering. Plenty of claims omit it entirely.

## What these checks cannot do

They sort claims by how much you can assess, not by whether the product works. A study can pass all three and still be small, short, or measuring something you do not care about. [[hughes-2013-sunscreen-prevention-of-skin-aging-rct-pmid-23732711]] is randomised, controlled, publicly funded and easy to find, and it still only supports a narrow conclusion over four and a half years against a comparator that also used sunscreen.

The reverse holds as well. An ingredient with no good study is not thereby useless; it is unproven, which is a different and more common thing.

## A note on the phrase clinically proven

It has no agreed definition in cosmetic marketing, in the same way the label words at [[label-words-alcohol-free-fragrance-free-hypoallergenic]] do not. Treat it as a prompt to ask the three questions above rather than as an answer to them.
