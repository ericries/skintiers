A blackhead is an open comedo: a hair follicle plugged with keratin (skin cell debris) and sebum, its opening dilated enough to expose the plug to air. It is not trapped dirt. StatPearls describes the mechanism directly: "Oxidized lipids and melanin within the comedo contribute to its characteristic dark black color."[^1] A closed comedo (whitehead) is the same plug under an intact skin surface, so it stays white. Neither responds to scrubbing the way dirt does, because there's no dirt to scrub out.

## How comedones form

DermNet's comedonal acne page explains the mechanics: comedones "arise when cells lining the sebaceous duct proliferate (cornification), and there is increased sebum production. A comedo is formed by the debris blocking the sebaceous duct and hair follicle."[^2] Comedonal acne is a pattern where most lesions are comedones rather than inflamed papules or pustules, and it most often shows up on the forehead and chin.[^2] StatPearls describes the same process in more detail: a microcomedone (the earliest, invisible lesion) accumulates keratin and sebum, distends the follicle, and becomes a closed comedo; continued distension opens the follicular orifice into a visible open comedo, i.e. a blackhead.[^1]

DermNet also lists mechanical contributors worth knowing if you're prone to comedones: "rupture of the follicle by injury such as squeezing pimples, abrasive washing, chemical peels or laser treatments," plus oily cosmetics, isopropyl myristate and propylene glycol in some products, overhydrated skin, and smoking.[^2]

## What doesn't work

Scrubbing, "detoxing," or steaming does not dissolve a keratin-and-sebum plug sitting below the follicle opening; it just irritates the surrounding skin, and per DermNet, abrasive washing can itself rupture follicles and worsen comedonal acne.[^2] Pore strips pull out the exposed top layer of some plugs but don't clear the follicle, so pores refill within days to weeks. Neither is a treatment for the underlying process; both are cosmetic and temporary at best.

## What actually works

DermNet's list of "suitable topical agents" for comedonal acne is short and specific: benzoyl peroxide, azelaic acid, salicylic acid, glycolic acid, and retinoids (tretinoin, isotretinoin, adapalene).[^2] The tier list below ranks the actives with published pages here by strength of evidence; [[retinoids]] and [[adapalene]] do the real comedolytic work by normalizing how follicle cells shed, while [[salicylic-acid]], [[azelaic-acid]], and [[benzoyl-peroxide]] each play a supporting role. DermNet is explicit that results take "several weeks to months" and treatment "needs to be continued long-term."[^2]

Persistent, established comedones that don't respond to topical treatment sometimes need in-office extraction; DermNet lists cryotherapy, electrosurgery, and microdermabrasion as surgical options for that scenario.[^2] If most of your lesions are inflamed (red, tender papules or pustules) rather than open or closed comedones, that's a different pattern; see [[acne]]. Persistently high sebum output that keeps refilling pores is covered at [[oily-skin]], and if the follicle openings themselves look permanently stretched rather than plugged, see [[enlarged-pores]]. This is an educational summary, not medical advice; prescription retinoids and in-office extraction are decided with a qualified clinician.

## The Evidence

StatPearls on acne vulgaris pathophysiology: microcomedones "gradually evolve and develop into other acne lesions, which include closed comedones (whiteheads), open comedones (blackheads), and inflammatory papules, pustules, and nodules," and "oxidized lipids and melanin within the comedo contribute to its characteristic dark black color."[^1]

A JAMA systematic review of 29 randomized double-blind trials on acne treatment reported: "Topical retinoids reduce the number of comedones and inflammatory lesions in the range of 40% to 70%. These agents are the mainstay of therapy in patients with comedones only."[^3]

A single-site, investigator-blinded, vehicle-controlled trial of adapalene 0.1% gel counted microcomedones via cyanoacrylate strips before and during a 12-week maintenance phase after combination therapy. Microcomedone counts fell significantly more with adapalene than vehicle: a percent difference versus vehicle of P = 0.04 for alternate-day dosing and P = 0.037 for daily dosing.[^4]
