Home LED light therapy means a wearable mask or handheld panel that shines coloured light on the skin, usually blue, red, or the two combined. It is sold for acne and for ageing skin, and of all the gadget categories you can buy for home use it is the one that comes out of the evidence best. The disease is on [[acne]] and the ageing target on [[anti-aging]].

> **Moderate evidence that LED does something, and no evidence here that it beats a cheap topical.** A 2026 GRADE scoping review of home-use devices found that for acne, "blue light-emitting diodes (LED) and combination blue-red LED devices demonstrated moderate quality evidence (Level B) with favorable safety profiles and Grade I recommendations", while red light alone managed only "low-quality evidence (Level C)".[^home] For facial rejuvenation LED also reached Level B, behind home fractional nonablative lasers at Level A.[^home] Against that, the Cochrane review of light for acne, covering 71 randomised trials and 4,211 participants, concluded that "High-quality evidence on the use of light therapies for people with acne is lacking".[^cochrane] None of the sources cited on this page compares a home LED device against [[benzoyl-peroxide]] or [[adapalene]], so treat a mask as a possible addition to a routine that already works rather than a replacement for one.

## The Evidence

**The two reviews disagree less than they appear to, and the reason matters.** Cochrane's quantified findings are almost entirely about **photodynamic therapy**, which is a drug applied in a clinic and then activated by light, not what a home mask does. Its one pooled result combined three RCTs in 360 participants and found methyl aminolevulinate PDT "was no different to placebo cream plus red light" on inflamed lesion counts.[^cochrane] Read that comparison carefully: **both arms received red light**, so it isolates the drug and says nothing about the light. For light therapies as a class, Cochrane did "not combine the effect estimates but rated the quality of the evidence as very low".[^cochrane] It did look at light against a topical retinoid, and reports studies "comparing MAL-PDT to light-only treatment, to adapalene and in combination with long-pulsed dye laser to long-pulsed dye laser alone", of which "None of these showed any clinically significant effects".[^cochrane] Again that is clinic-based PDT rather than a home mask. Cochrane's searches also ran only to September 2015, so most of the home-device studies the 2026 review graded were not in it.[^cochrane] The honest reading is that Cochrane does not refute home LED; it documents that the literature was too weak and too varied to test it properly.

**Cochrane's description of that literature is the most useful thing it offers here.** Most studies were small, "median 31 participants"; "Two thirds of studies were industry-sponsored" with conflicts either declared or undeclared, which is why it judged that risk of bias unclear; and "less than half of the studies performed assessments later than eight weeks after final treatment".[^cochrane] Those three facts apply to the newer home-device trials as much as the old ones, and they are the reason a Level B grade means moderate confidence rather than a settled answer.

**What the light is thought to be doing.** A 2026 PRISMA systematic review of phototherapy for acne, drawing on 64 articles including 42 clinical trials, places blue and red LED in the inflammation-control pathway: they "provide gradual anti-inflammatory effects suitable for mild-to-moderate acne by reducing C. acnes colonization and modulating cytokines such as TGF-β1, IL-10, and TNF-α".[^photo] Note both halves of that. The mechanism is gradual and anti-inflammatory, and the population is mild-to-moderate acne. The same review reserves the stronger sebum-suppressing claim for clinic-based ALA-PDT in moderate-to-severe disease.[^photo]

**Safety is the strongest thing on this page.** The GRADE review recorded "favorable safety profiles" for blue and blue-red LED and "minimal adverse events" for red LED.[^home] That is the real argument for trying one: the downside is mostly the price. For contrast, the same review put home microneedling at "low-quality evidence (Level D) and associated with infectious complications", and found home intense pulsed light and heat-based devices "lacked supportive clinical data (Level D) and were not recommended".[^home] The needling case is on [[microneedling]].

**An LED mask is the wrong tool for scars and marks.** The phototherapy review assigns scar remodelling to "fractional CO2 and picosecond lasers", and the fading of post-inflammatory redness and pigment to "vascular-targeted lasers (PDL, Nd:YAG) and IPL".[^photo] LED appears in neither list. If the goal is the aftermath rather than active spots, see [[acne-scars]], [[post-inflammatory-erythema]] and [[hyperpigmentation]], and expect a clinic rather than a mask. The review's own framing is that these modalities "are phenotype-specific rather than interchangeable".[^photo]

**What would move this grade.** A trial that compares a home LED device against a standard topical, in the population the mechanism fits, with follow-up past eight weeks and without industry authorship. None of the sources here report one.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. The grades above describe average results in trials of mild-to-moderate acne; scarring or severe acne is a reason to see a dermatologist rather than buy a device.
