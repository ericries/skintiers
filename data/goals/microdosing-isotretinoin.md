Microdosing means taking [isotretinoin](/ingredients/isotretinoin/) at well under the labelled dose, usually for longer, on the reasoning that most of the drug's side effects are dose-dependent, so a smaller dose should buy a gentler ride. The reasoning is sound and the published evidence partly supports it. What the evidence does not support is the thing people most want from it, which is the same durable remission a full course is known for.

Two separate departures from the label get bundled under one word, and they are worth separating. The FDA label for isotretinoin capsules reserves the drug "for patients with severe nodular acne who are unresponsive to conventional therapy, including systemic antibiotics", so using it for mild or moderate acne is off-label on the indication before dose enters the picture.[^label] A 2022 systematic review states this plainly, describing isotretinoin as "used as an off-label indication for the treatment of mild-to-moderate grades of acne not responding to conventional treatment".[^meta] The second departure is the dose and schedule, and that is where the trial data lives.

## The Evidence

The Cochrane review of oral isotretinoin for acne is the anchor: 31 randomised trials, 3,836 participants aged 12 to 55, with mild to severe acne.[^cochrane] On the dose question it could not pool the results. Heterogeneity between the trials comparing doses and regimens "precluded meta-analysis", leaving individual trials, all graded low-quality.[^cochrane]

Read together, those trials say something more specific than a verdict for or against low dosing. It depends entirely on which endpoint you ask about.

- Measured as **average lesion reduction**, dose barely mattered. One trial of 154 participants with severe acne reported a 79%, 80% and 84% decrease in total inflammatory lesion count after 20 weeks at 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 mg/kg/day respectively.[^cochrane]
- Measured as **getting almost completely clear**, dose mattered a lot. Another trial of 150 participants with severe acne compared 0.1, 0.5 and 1 mg/kg/day for 20 weeks, and the proportion of participants achieving a 95% decrease in total inflammatory lesion count was 58%, 80% and 90%.[^cochrane]

So a tenth of the standard dose got most of the average lesion improvement and left roughly a third fewer people near-clear. That tradeoff, not an efficacy cliff, is the honest shape of the low-dose decision.

Schedule turns out to matter more than dose. In one trial of moderate acne over 24 weeks, continuous low dose (0.25 to 0.4 mg/kg/day) and continuous conventional dose (0.5 to 0.7 mg/kg/day) both reduced inflammatory lesion counts more than an intermittent regimen of the same conventional dose given one week in a month, by a mean difference of 3.72 lesions (95% CI 2.13 to 5.31) and 3.87 lesions (95% CI 2.31 to 5.43) respectively, 40 participants per comparison.[^cochrane] Cochrane's own summary is that "daily treatment may be more effective than treatment for one week each month".[^cochrane] A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis found the same ordering, reporting "an overall beneficial effect from using the low-daily dose compared with the pulsed doses".[^meta] If you are going to take less, the evidence read here favours taking a little every day over taking the usual amount in bursts.

On the question microdosing is usually chosen to answer, the same 2022 review is not encouraging. Comparing low daily doses against the conventional dose in adults with mild-to-moderate acne, the pooled estimate "showed an overall benefit for conventional dose", and its conclusion is that "conventional dose isotretinoin improves the odds of prolonged remission in adults with mild-to-moderate acne vulgaris compared to the low doses". The authors rate their own evidence base as low quality by GRADE, writing that "the quality of evidence is low".[^meta] So the best available synthesis points the wrong way for durability, and it is not a confident pointer.

Safety is the part that held up. Fourteen trials covering 906 participants with moderate and severe acne reported no serious adverse events when comparing different doses or regimens, during treatment lasting 12 to 32 weeks and in follow-up to 48 weeks.[^cochrane] Cochrane could not pool the minor effects such as skin dryness, hair loss and itching, so whether a lower dose reliably means fewer dry lips is, in its assessment, uncertain.[^cochrane] The dose-dependence of side effects is the premise of microdosing, and these trials do not confirm it so much as fail to measure it consistently.

A 2021 systematic review of 15 included studies concludes that "daily doses between 0.1 and 0.3 mg/kg can be recommended for more than 6 months due to the occurrence of fewer side effects and more economical concerns".[^sr2021] Treat that as the most favourable reading on offer, and note that the same abstract defines low dose two incompatible ways within a few sentences and states a total dose in units of mg/kg/day.[^sr2021]

## What the label actually says

The current label for isotretinoin capsules gives a recommended dosage of "0.5 to 1 mg/kg/day given in two divided doses without regard to meals for 15 to 20 weeks", allows up to 2 mg/kg/day for adults with very severe disease, and states that "once daily dosing is not recommended".[^label] A popular microdose of one 10 or 20 mg capsule a few times a week is therefore below the labelled dose and also against the labelled dosing frequency, which is a second deviation that discussions of microdosing rarely mention.

One absence is worth naming. Both reviews cited here describe the conventional target as a cumulative dose of 120 to 150 mg/kg, and clinicians treat that number as the benchmark a microdose falls short of.[^meta][^sr2021] The word "cumulative" does not appear anywhere in the isotretinoin capsule label read for this page, which sets a duration of 15 to 20 weeks and a daily dose but no lifetime total.[^label] The cumulative-dose target is clinical convention drawn from the literature, not a labelled instruction, so a microdose is measured against a yardstick the FDA never wrote down.

The label also says a second course "is not recommended before a 2-month waiting period because the patient's acne may continue to improve following a 15 to 20-week course".[^label] Anyone weighing a low dose against the risk of needing a repeat should read that as the label's own position on waiting before judging a result.

There is no evidence tier list on this page. The comparison here is between doses of one drug rather than between products or ingredients, and [isotretinoin](/ingredients/isotretinoin/) is the only item on this site involved in it, so there is nothing to rank.
