What you eat, and what you swallow in capsule form, marketed as a route to better skin. This hub covers only what controlled trials on this site actually tested; the topical side of every condition named here lives on its own page.

> **Bottom line.** The honest summary is narrow. The one skin condition where diet has repeated randomized support is **psoriasis**, and even there the measured effect is small and hard to separate from weight loss and from the extra support the diet groups received.[^medipso][^morrow] For skin aging, the ingestible trials on this site are small, short, and measured with instruments rather than by how anyone looks.[^afzal] Nothing here supports a diet that treats acne, eczema or pigmentation. Where a food genuinely causes a skin problem it is usually not nutrition at all but contact, as with the plant-juice-plus-sunlight burn on [[phytophotodermatitis]].

## Where The Evidence Is Strongest

**Psoriasis is the exception, and it is a qualified one.** Two separate records point the same way. A 2026 meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials in 1145 participants found weight-loss interventions produced a small but statistically significant reduction in psoriasis severity against usual care, with most of those interventions being dietary.[^morrow] A 2025 randomized trial of a dietitian-guided Mediterranean diet reported a larger improvement, but its control group got unsupervised advice while the diet group got counselling, materials and free olive oil, so it cannot separate the food from the support.[^medipso]

Read together, these are **not two independent lines of evidence**. Weight change plausibly drives both, and the meta-analysis is the more conservative estimate because it pools more trials against a cleaner comparator. The practical reading is that losing weight, if someone has weight to lose, modestly helps psoriasis alongside treatment rather than instead of it. Full appraisals are on [[morrow-2026-weight-loss-psoriasis-severity-meta-analysis-pmid-41416383]] and [[perez-bootello-2025-medipso-mediterranean-diet-psoriasis-rct]]; the condition itself is on [[psoriasis]].

## Ingestibles For Skin Aging

Supplements marketed for wrinkles and firmness are the other place trials exist, and they are weaker than the marketing implies. An 8-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of an oral antioxidant-rich synbiotic in 36 participants reported reduced wrinkle severity and improved cheek elasticity against placebo.[^afzal] A separate systematic review pooled 19 randomized trials of oral and topical peptides for skin aging.[^nukaly]

Three limits apply to this whole category and are easy to lose. The trials are **small and short**, so they cannot speak to what a supplement does over the years that aging actually happens. The endpoints are **instrument readings**, such as elasticity probes and wrinkle-severity scores, which are not the same as a visible change anyone would notice. And a **supplement is a proprietary blend**, so a positive result belongs to that specific product rather than to its category. Appraisals are on [[afzal-2026-prospective-randomized-double-blind-placebo-controlled-stud]] and [[nukaly-2026-oral-topical-peptides-skin-aging-meta-analysis]]; the topical case is on [[anti-aging]] and [[peptides]], and the oral-versus-topical question for bacteria is on [[probiotics]].

## What Is Not Established Here

No trial on this site supports a diet for [[acne]], [[atopic-dermatitis]] or [[hyperpigmentation]]. That is a statement about what has been reviewed here rather than a claim about the wider literature, which was not surveyed for this page, and dietary triggers remain a reasonable thing to raise with a clinician. It does mean that elimination diets marketed for clear skin are not resting on anything in this site's evidence base.

**No tier list is published on this hub.** Ranking dietary interventions would require pages for each one, and the site grades ingredients and products rather than diets, so a ladder here would be invented rather than derived.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician or a dietitian. Restrictive diets carry their own risks, and anyone considering one for a skin condition, especially for a child, should take that to a professional rather than to a skincare site.
