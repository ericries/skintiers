"Sensitive skin" is not a medical diagnosis. DermNet describes it as a lay term for "skin with reduced tolerance to the application of cosmetics and personal care products."[^dermnet] It is a remarkably common self-report: "approximately 50% of women and 40% of men may report having sensitive skin."[^dermnet] The skeptical bottom line: this label describes a symptom pattern, often a [[skin-barrier-repair|damaged skin barrier]] plus reactivity, not a single treatable disease. A real underlying cause should be looked for and, where found, treated directly rather than papered over with more "gentle" products.

## How to know you have this

Sensitive skin shows up as subjective symptoms (stinging, itching, burning) and/or visible changes (redness, dryness, scaling, peeling, bumps, hives).[^dermnet] Complaints usually concentrate on the face, where products tolerated elsewhere on the body cause irritation; eyelids are especially reactive, and armpits, groin, and genitals can also be more sensitive because the skin there is thinner.[^dermnet]

## What is actually going on

DermNet lists the recognized medical causes behind a "sensitive skin" complaint: irritant contact dermatitis, allergic contact dermatitis, contact urticaria, [[rosacea]], physical urticaria and dermographism, aquagenic pruritus, plain [[dry-skin]], [[atopic-dermatitis|eczema/dermatitis]] of any type, photodermatoses, and rarer causes like cutaneous mastocytosis.[^dermnet] The most common of these is irritant contact dermatitis (see [[contact-dermatitis]]), "an inflammatory response of the skin to (an) externally applied agent(s) or factor(s) without requiring prior sensitisation," meaning it is not an allergy.[^dermnet] Two forms show no visible rash at all: subjective/sensory irritation, where lactic acid and propylene glycol in cosmetics are common triggers of pure stinging and burning with no biopsy-visible inflammation, and non-erythematous irritation, where inflammation is present on biopsy despite no visible rash, often from ingredients like cocamidopropyl betaine.[^dermnet] Any form of eczema also predisposes to sensitive skin, since a disturbed barrier lets external irritants penetrate more easily and a second problem, like contact dermatitis to a product being used to treat the eczema, can be layered on top and overlooked.[^dermnet] Rosacea specifically can present as skin that suddenly stops tolerating a skincare product used uneventfully for years.[^dermnet]

## What actually works

DermNet's own treatment guidance is unglamorous: treat the underlying condition where one is found, avoid the specific triggering agent, use a soap-free cleanser, use non-irritating moisturizers and emollients, and use sun protection.[^dermnet] That maps onto barrier support ([[skin-barrier-repair]], [[moisturizing]]) as the foundation, since a repaired barrier both reduces the baseline reactivity and blocks irritants from penetrating. [[niacinamide]] is a genuinely anti-inflammatory active with modest evidence for supporting the barrier itself, useful as a soothing adjunct layered onto that foundation rather than a fix on its own. Avoiding common irritants matters as much as adding anything: fragrance, high concentrations of exfoliating acids, and retinoids introduced too fast are frequent triggers of both the visible and the sensory forms of irritation described above.[^dermnet] Gentle, fragrance-free cleansing (a soap-free cleanser per DermNet) removes one of the most common everyday triggers without adding surfactant irritation.[^dermnet]

## What's overhyped

"Sensitive skin" as marketed is often just a claim on a bottle, and no single soothing ingredient reverses a genuine underlying condition; if the skin is reacting to a specific product after years of tolerating it, that is a reason to suspect rosacea or a new allergy and get it checked, not to add more products on top.[^dermnet]

This is an educational summary, not medical advice. Several of the causes behind a "sensitive skin" complaint (rosacea, eczema, allergic or irritant contact dermatitis, urticaria, and rarer conditions like cutaneous mastocytosis) are medical diagnoses that only a clinician can confirm and treat. Skin that stings, burns, flares, or reacts persistently, especially if it is new or spreading, is worth having assessed rather than self-managed with more products.

## The Evidence

DermNet's clinical reference summary anchors the causes and diagnostic categories above, drawing on published reviews of irritant contact dermatitis mechanisms and the epidemiology of self-reported sensitive skin.[^dermnet] The barrier-repair and moisturizing tier entries carry their own load-bearing trial evidence on their linked pages, including the Cochrane review finding regular moisturizer use reduced eczema flares (risk ratio 0.40) and cut topical corticosteroid use, and the separate Cochrane review finding moisturizer use lowered the risk of occupational irritant hand dermatitis (13% vs 19% of controls, RR 0.71) though rated low-quality.[^moisturizing][^contact-dermatitis]
