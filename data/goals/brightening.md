"Brightening" and "even skin tone" are marketing umbrella terms for more than one problem, and the problems need different fixes. Real hyperpigmentation, melasma, post-inflammatory marks, and sun spots, is extra melanin sitting in specific patches; [[melasma]] and [[hyperpigmentation]] cover how those three are told apart and what the trial evidence supports for each. General dullness, a flat or tired look with no distinct dark patches, is a different problem: a surface effect from a rough or dehydrated top layer rather than a color change in the skin. Nothing below bleaches skin lighter than its own natural tone. Even hydroquinone, the ingredient at the center of the FDA's 2006 proposed rule on over-the-counter skin-bleaching drug products,[^fda2006] is not a general lightener: its one FDA-approved form, the fixed triple-combination cream, is restricted to short-term use for diagnosed melasma.[^triluma] The actives below work slowly: the trials behind them typically run 8 to 12 weeks or longer, and none produce a lasting result without daily sun protection underneath.

## Two problems under one word

**Actual hyperpigmentation** is visibly darker patches or spots: melasma's symmetric, hormone-linked patches, post-inflammatory marks left behind by an earlier breakout or injury, and sun-driven spots that track wherever light lands. [[melasma]] covers melasma specifically, including how a clinician diagnoses it and the trial base behind each treatment; [[hyperpigmentation]] covers all three forms together and how they are distinguished. This page reuses that evidence for the tier list above rather than repeating it.

**General dullness** is not a pigment problem. [[glass-skin]] covers the evidence that skin radiance tracks moisture, fine surface texture, and how light scatters versus reflects off the skin, none of which is excess melanin. [[moisturizing]] covers the hydration side of that surface effect, and exfoliating acids such as [[glycolic-acid]], [[lactic-acid]], and [[salicylic-acid]] cover the texture side. A product marketed simply as "brightening" does not say which of these two different jobs it is doing.

## Sun protection is the prerequisite

Every trial behind the actives in the tier list above is run on a base of daily sunscreen, and the quality of that sun protection changes the outcome. In a double-blind randomized trial, melasma patients all using 4% hydroquinone were split by sunscreen type: the group whose SPF 50+ sunscreen also contained iron oxide, which blocks visible light, showed greater improvement on melasma severity, colorimetry, and measured melanin than the group using a UV-only sunscreen, over 8 weeks.[^castanedo] The trial authors concluded that "UV-VL sunscreen enhances the depigmenting efficacy of hydroquinone."[^castanedo] Without broad-spectrum, visible-light-blocking sun protection, the actives below underperform in the same trials that establish their evidence, and any tone improvement they produce tends to fade once sun exposure resumes. What to look for in a filter is covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

## Honest limits

None of these actives are fast or permanent. The trials behind them run 8 to 12 weeks and longer, and the best-evidenced oral option, tranexamic acid, lost most of its gain within months once patients stopped taking it.[^delrosario] Melasma in particular is chronic and relapsing regardless of which active is used, and a changing, asymmetric, or newly appeared dark spot is a reason to see a board-certified dermatologist rather than assume it will respond to an over-the-counter product. Chemical peels, lasers, and prescription-only forms such as the fixed triple-combination cream sit above the actives graded here and are managed by a prescriber, not covered on this page. Nothing here is medical advice.
