When a dermatologist prescribes a drug for something its label does not list, that is off-label use, and it is both ordinary and legal.[^fda] **Bottom line:** off-label is not a synonym for unproven, and it is not a synonym for vetted either. It is a statement about the paperwork, not about the evidence.

## What it actually means

The FDA approves a drug for specific uses after a company submits data showing it is safe and effective for those uses. Off-label use is any use outside that. The FDA's own description covers three distinct shapes:[^fda]

- a different disease or condition from the approved one
- a different route, "such as when a drug is approved as a capsule, but it is given instead in an oral solution"
- a different dose, "such as when a drug is approved at a dose of one tablet every day, but a patient is told by their healthcare provider to take two tablets every day"

All three are off-label. A reader who hears the phrase and pictures only the first will misjudge the second two, which are often far smaller departures.

**Prescribing this way is permitted.** In the FDA's words, "once the FDA approves a drug, healthcare providers generally may prescribe the drug for an unapproved use when they judge that it is medically appropriate for their patient".[^fda]

**And the regulator has not blessed it.** The same page is just as plain: "remember that FDA has not determined that the drug is safe and effective for the unapproved use".[^fda] Both sentences are true at once, which is the whole difficulty.

## The confusion worth clearing up

**Off-label is not the same as a drug that was never approved at all.** An off-label prescription uses an FDA-approved drug outside its listed indications. That is different from the products whose DailyMed marketing category is an unapproved drug, where the drug itself never went through approval. Some older topical prescriptions sit in that second group, and calling them FDA-approved is simply wrong, whereas calling an off-label use of an approved drug unapproved is right only in the narrow sense that this particular use was not reviewed. [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]] covers the neighbouring approved-versus-cleared trap for devices.

**Off-label does not mean evidence-free, and it does not mean evidenced.** Both exist. Sometimes a use is off-label because trials exist but no company has paid to add the indication, which is expensive and only worth doing if a patent makes it pay. Sometimes a use is off-label because nobody has tested it properly. The label cannot tell you which, so the question to carry is not whether a use is off-label but what was actually studied.

## How this site handles it

Where a page records an off-label use, it says what the label does and does not list rather than implying the gap is a verdict. ## The Evidence

None of its own, by design. This page makes no claim about any drug working or not working; it describes a regulatory category and what that category does and does not tell you. Its one source is the FDA's own patient-facing explanation, which is the primary authority on what the agency has and has not reviewed.[^fda]

The distinction it draws between an off-label use and a drug whose marketing category is unapproved is this site's framing, assembled from reading both kinds of record, and is flagged as such in the source note rather than attributed to the FDA.

## Questions the FDA suggests asking

Its list is practical and worth borrowing in a consultation: what the drug is approved for, whether approved alternatives exist for your condition, "What scientific studies are available to support the use of this drug to treat my disease or medical condition?", and whether it is likely to work better than an approved treatment.[^fda]
