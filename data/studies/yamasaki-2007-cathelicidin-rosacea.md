Increased serine protease activity and cathelicidin promotes skin inflammation in rosacea is a 2007 Nature Medicine laboratory study that found people with rosacea carry abnormally high amounts of the antimicrobial peptide cathelicidin in their facial skin, in an unusually processed form linked to an epidermal enzyme (stratum corneum tryptic enzyme, also called kallikrein-5), and that recreating those conditions in mouse skin produced inflammation.[^1][^2]

## What It Asked

Rosacea is a common inflammatory skin condition. The study reports that it "affects 3% of the US population over 30 years of age," causing lasting facial redness along with pus-filled bumps and visible dilated blood vessels.[^2] Its cause was unknown at the time, but flare-ups were known to be set off by things that switch on the body's fast, non-specific first line of defense, the innate immune system, including the release of cathelicidin.[^2] Cathelicidin is a small antimicrobial protein (peptide) the skin makes to fight microbes, and it can also drive inflammation. The researchers set out to test a specific idea: that rosacea involves too much cathelicidin, in a form that has been cut up abnormally by an overactive skin enzyme, and that this combination is enough to inflame skin.[^1][^2]

## Who and What They Did

The work had two halves, a human sample study and a set of mouse experiments.[^1][^2]

In the human half, the team compared facial skin from people with rosacea against skin from people without it, measuring how much cathelicidin was present and what forms of the peptide it had been cut into, and measuring the amount of the cutting enzyme, stratum corneum tryptic enzyme (SCTE), a serine protease of the kallikrein family also known as kallikrein-5.[^1][^2] A serine protease is an enzyme that snips other proteins; SCTE is the enzyme that processes cathelicidin into its active fragments.

In the mouse half, they tested whether these ingredients could actually cause inflammation rather than just accompany it. They did four things: injected mouse skin with the cathelicidin fragments found in rosacea; added SCTE; and raised the skin's overall protease activity by deleting Spink5, the gene for a natural brake on these enzymes (a serine protease inhibitor).[^1][^2] Finally, to test whether cathelicidin was truly necessary, they used mice engineered to lack Camp, the gene that makes cathelicidin, so the peptide could not be produced at all.[^1][^2]

## What It Found

In facial skin, the study reports that "individuals with rosacea express abnormally high levels of cathelicidin in their facial skin," meaning far more of the peptide than in people without the condition.[^2] Beyond the raw amount, the form was abnormal: "the proteolytically processed forms of cathelicidin peptides found in rosacea are different" from those in unaffected skin, so the peptide had been cut into fragments that healthy skin does not normally carry.[^2] The authors tie this abnormal processing to "an increase in stratum corneum tryptic enzyme (SCTE) in the epidermis," that is, more of the cutting enzyme in the outer skin layer.[^2]

The mouse experiments moved the story from association to cause. Injecting the rosacea-type cathelicidin fragments, adding SCTE, and deleting the Spink5 brake gene each increased inflammation in mouse skin, so more enzyme activity and more of the abnormal peptide both produced the inflammatory response.[^2] The role of cathelicidin was, in the authors' words, "verified in mice with a targeted deletion of Camp": with the cathelicidin gene switched off, the enzyme could no longer drive the same inflammation, showing the peptide was needed for the effect.[^2]

The authors' overall conclusion was that "an exacerbated innate immune response can reproduce elements of this disease," offering a mechanism for how rosacea inflammation is generated.[^2]

## How Strong the Evidence Is

This is basic laboratory (mechanistic) research, not a clinical trial, so it is not the kind of study graded by systems such as GRADE. Its strength is that it combines two types of evidence: a comparison of human rosacea skin with unaffected skin, and controlled mouse experiments that manipulate one factor at a time.[^1][^2] The human part shows an association (rosacea skin has more cathelicidin and more SCTE), which on its own cannot prove cause. The mouse experiments add causal weight by showing that supplying the same peptide or enzyme, or removing a protease brake, is enough to inflame skin, and that removing cathelicidin altogether blocks the effect.[^2]

What it does show is a plausible molecular pathway for rosacea inflammation, the cathelicidin and kallikrein-5 cascade, supported by both patient samples and gene-level experiments. What it does not show is that this pathway is the sole or original cause of rosacea in people: the authors state only that the model can "reproduce elements of this disease," not the whole condition.[^2] It also does not identify why cathelicidin and SCTE become elevated in the first place, and it tests no treatment. Nature Medicine published the paper alongside an independent commentary, "Rosacea: skin innate immunity gone awry?", by Bevins and Liu.[^4]

## Funding

The work was supported by two US National Institutes of Health grants: R01-AR45676 from the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases and R01-AI052453 from the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases.[^3] These are government research grants rather than pharmaceutical-company funding.

## Limitations

The human findings are correlational: they establish that rosacea skin carries more cathelicidin and more SCTE, not that these came first or act alone.[^2] The causal experiments were done in mouse skin using injected peptides, added enzyme, and engineered gene deletions, which model parts of the biology rather than the spontaneous human disease.[^1][^2] The authors themselves frame the mouse work as reproducing "elements of this disease" rather than rosacea in full.[^2] The study also leaves open what upstream triggers raise cathelicidin and protease activity, and it does not evaluate any therapy.

A later trial of azelaic acid 15% gel in rosacea patients reported reductions in cathelicidin, kallikrein-5, and serine protease activity during treatment, connecting this proposed pathway to an actual therapy; that work is covered on [[azelaic-acid]].
