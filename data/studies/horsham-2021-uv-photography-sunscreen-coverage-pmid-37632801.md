A study of the camera, not of the sunscreen. It asks whether UV photography can show where someone missed a spot, and it is recorded here partly because a widely repeated coverage-decay statistic is attributed to it and is not in it. Sunscreen labelling is at [[reading-a-sunscreen-label]] and the filters themselves at [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^1]

**Design:** a "Descriptive Study" in three phases: three UV cameras compared, then UV photography tried on a range of sun protection products, then a purpose-built UV webcam field-tested at a beach.[^1] Not a trial of whether sunscreen works and not a measurement of how coverage changes over a day.

**Population:** volunteers, with the only participant count in the abstract being the 35 people of phase 2, of whom 29 answered a satisfaction question.[^1] Conducted by Queensland University of Technology with QIMR Berghofer and Designworks Group, Brisbane.

**Intervention vs comparator:** no treatment arms. Phase 1 had researchers apply three products from "sun protection factor [SPF] 15 to 50+" and compared what three different cameras showed; phase 2 had people apply their own products "in their usual manner", with photographs "collected in the morning and afternoon to assess whether the coverage remained over time".[^1]

**Primary results:** the cameras work, within limits. Products applied at "2 and 1 mg/cm2" were "clearly visible", while "0.4 mg/cm2" showed "lower levels of coverage".[^1] In phase 2, "83% (29/35) of participants" said they were likely to use UV photography in future, and the phase 3 webcam "was used 233 times by the public during field-testing" over seven days.[^1]

**Effect size:** none is reported, because nothing is being compared. Despite photographing morning and afternoon, the abstract gives no figure for how much coverage was lost, no time points and no statistical test.

**Generalizes to:** deciding whether a UV camera is a useful feedback tool, which is what it set out to answer. The authors' conclusion is correspondingly narrow: UV photography "could identify the areas missed by sun protection lotions with chemical filters".[^1]

**Key limitation, and the one worth carrying away:** it does not work on tinted products. The abstract records that "it was observed that UV photography could not identify the areas missed for all tinted products".[^1] Anyone using a UV selfie to check a tinted sunscreen or an SPF foundation is getting an image that cannot tell them what they want to know. A second caution for anyone re-checking this paper: two of the six authors are employees of Designworks Group, the firm named alongside the webcam development, which PubMed records in the conflict statement.[^1]

**What this paper does NOT say.** It is cited in circulation as the source of an average 18% drop in coverage signal after two hours and nearly 29% after six. Searching the full abstract for those figures returns nothing: no percentage of coverage loss, no two-hour or six-hour time point, no decay measurement of any kind.[^1] The claim may come from somewhere else, but it does not come from here, and this page exists partly so that the next person checking does not have to repeat the search.
