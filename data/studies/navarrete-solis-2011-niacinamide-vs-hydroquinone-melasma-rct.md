A split-face, double-blind RCT in 27 melasma patients found 4% niacinamide performed comparably to 4% hydroquinone on colorimetric measures, with a slightly lower subjective-scale (good-to-excellent) response rate and fewer side effects.[^1]

**Design:** Double-blind, randomized, split-face clinical trial; niacinamide applied to one side of the face and hydroquinone (HQ) to the other side of the same patient for 8 weeks, with sunscreen used throughout. Skin color was assessed by noninvasive colorimetry plus subjective scales, and histological sections were taken before and after treatment (on the niacinamide side only).[^1]

**Population / n:** 27 patients with melasma.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** 4% niacinamide cream (one side of face) vs. 4% hydroquinone cream (other side of face), self-controlled split-face design, 8 weeks.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "All patients showed pigment improvement with both treatments. Colorimetric measures did not show statistical differences between both sides. However, good to excellent improvement was observed with niacinamide in 44% of patients, compared to 55% with HQ." "Niacinamide reduced importantly the mast cell infiltrate and showed improvement of solar elastosis in melasma skin." "Side effects were present in 18% with niacinamide versus 29% with HQ."[^1] No p-values are given for the 44%-vs-55% or 18%-vs-29% comparisons; the abstract states only that colorimetric (objective) measures showed no statistical difference between sides.

**Effect size / interpretation:** On the objective colorimetric endpoint the two treatments were statistically indistinguishable; on the subjective "good to excellent" response scale HQ numerically outperformed niacinamide (55% vs. 44%), while niacinamide caused fewer side effects (18% vs. 29%). No effect size or confidence interval is reported for any of these figures in the abstract. The histology-only findings (mast cell reduction, solar elastosis improvement) were assessed on the niacinamide side and are not stated as compared against the HQ side.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies to adults with melasma treated for 8 weeks under daily sunscreen use; the small sample (n=27), lack of statistical testing on the subjective response-rate and side-effect figures, and split-face (not fully independent) comparison design limit how far the response-rate difference can be generalized. Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full text, though PubMed indicates full text is available via PMC/Wiley. Supports [[niacinamide]] as a comparably effective, better-tolerated alternative to [[hydroquinone]] for [[hyperpigmentation]]/melasma, covered generally on those pages.
