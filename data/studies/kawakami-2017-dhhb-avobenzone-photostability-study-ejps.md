An in vitro formulation study found that the UV filter DHHB, despite being photostable itself and quenching [[avobenzone]]'s excited state, did NOT photostabilize avobenzone in sunscreen formulations.[^1]

**Design:** In vitro physicochemical study of sunscreen formulations: photostability assessed by HPLC and spectrophotometry under irradiation, the DHHB/avobenzone (AVO) interaction mechanism probed by steady-state and time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy, and phototoxicity assessed in a 3T3 fibroblast cell-culture assay. Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the EJPS full text.[^1]

**Population / n:** Not a human or clinical study; three sunscreen formulations (labeled F1, F2, F3 in the abstract, containing different DHHB/AVO combinations) and 3T3 fibroblast cultures (standard cell-line background; the abstract does not specify species) for phototoxicity testing. The abstract does not state replicate counts (n) for the HPLC, spectroscopy, or phototoxicity assays.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Formulations containing DHHB alone, AVO alone, and DHHB + AVO combined were compared for UVA protection, photostability under irradiation (HPLC/spectrophotometry), fluorescence-quenching behavior, and in vitro phototoxic potential.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "All formulations provided ultra-protection against UVA radiation." "HPLC results demonstrated that DHHB did not present a photostabilizing effect on AVO." "Fluorescence spectroscopy showed that AVO and DHHB interact by a static quenching mechanism and DHHB did not affect the AVO excited state lifetime." "The energy transfer by Förster mechanism (FRET) ... is unlikely in this work." "Phototoxicity results demonstrated that combinations containing DHHB (C2) did not show a phototoxic potential." "Although DHHB was considered to be photostable for all formulations studied (F2 and F3) it did not increase the photostability of AVO (F3)." The abstract gives no percentage or fold-change figures for the degree of AVO photodegradation, quenching magnitude, or phototoxicity result.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** No quantitative effect size is stated. The qualitative finding is that DHHB is itself photostable and forms a static (ground-state) quenching complex with AVO, but this interaction does not translate into a photostabilizing benefit for AVO: the authors conclude a DHHB-only formulation (F2) is "more advantageous" than an AVO-alone formulation (F1) and an AVO/DHHB combination (F3), since adding AVO does not gain photostability and only DHHB's own stability is retained.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** This is an in vitro chemistry/formulation study (HPLC, spectroscopy, and a 3T3 cell phototoxicity assay), not a clinical or human-skin trial, and its conclusions are specific to the three test formulations studied, so they should not be generalized to all avobenzone-containing sunscreen bases without confirmation. The abstract reports no sample sizes, percentages, or fold-changes, and only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the EJPS full text, so no additional numbers can be verified. Relevant background on avobenzone's own photostability problem and on UV filter formulation generally is covered on [[avobenzone]] and [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].
