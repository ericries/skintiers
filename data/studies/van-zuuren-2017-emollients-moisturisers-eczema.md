Emollients and moisturisers for eczema is a 2017 Cochrane systematic review of 77 randomised controlled trials (6603 participants) that found most moisturisers gave some benefit for eczema, worked better when combined with active treatment, and that no single moisturiser was shown to beat another.[^1][^2]

## Design

The review pooled 77 randomised controlled trials with 6603 participants, drawn from database searches to December 2015.[^1][^3] Participants had mainly mild to moderate eczema, and their age ranged from four months to 84 years (mean 18.6 years).[^3] The mean trial duration was 6.7 weeks.[^1]

The comparisons fell into several groups: moisturiser versus no moisturiser; specific moisturisers containing glycerol, urea, or oat versus their vehicle or placebo; all moisturisers pooled versus control; and active treatment plus a moisturiser versus the active treatment alone.[^1]

## What It Found

Moisturiser versus no moisturiser: lower eczema severity (SCORAD mean difference -2.42, 95% CI -4.55 to -0.28; 3 studies, 276 participants), fewer flares (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.70; 2 studies, 87 participants), a longer median time to flare (180 versus 30 days; hazard ratio 3.74, 95% CI 1.86 to 7.50), and less topical corticosteroid used (mean difference -9.30 g, 95% CI -15.3 to -3.27; 2 studies, 222 participants).[^1]

Glycerol-containing moisturiser versus vehicle or placebo: lower investigator-assessed severity (SCORAD mean difference -2.20, 95% CI -3.44 to -0.96; 1 study, 249 participants).[^1]

Urea-containing cream versus placebo: more participants reported improved skin (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.53; 1 study, 129 participants), with more adverse events in the urea group (RR 1.65, 95% CI 1.16 to 2.34).[^1]

Oat-containing moisturisers versus control: no statistically significant difference in participant-assessed severity, satisfaction, or investigator-assessed severity; the authors did report fewer flares (RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.77; 1 study, 43 participants), reduced use of topical corticosteroids, and more adverse events in the oat group.[^1]

All moisturisers pooled versus control: lower investigator-assessed severity (SMD -1.04, 95% CI -1.57 to -0.51; 12 studies, 1281 participants), less itch (SMD -1.10, 95% CI -1.83 to -0.38; 7 studies, 749 participants), and fewer flares (RR 0.33, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.62; 6 studies, 607 participants). Adverse events did not differ from control (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.30; 10 studies, 1275 participants).[^1]

Active treatment plus a moisturiser versus active treatment alone: lower investigator-assessed severity (SMD -0.87, 95% CI -1.17 to -0.57; 3 studies, 192 participants) and fewer flares (RR 0.43, 95% CI 0.20 to 0.93; 1 study, 105 participants).[^1]

On comparisons between one moisturiser and another, the authors reported: "We did not find reliable evidence that one moisturiser is better than another."[^2] Their overall statement was that "Most moisturisers showed some beneficial effects."[^2]

## Certainty of the Evidence

The review graded each outcome using GRADE. Certainty was rated high for investigator-assessed disease severity in two comparisons: glycerol-containing cream versus control (SCORAD mean difference -2.20) and all moisturisers pooled versus control (SMD -1.04).[^1][^3] Certainty was moderate for the flares and adverse-event outcomes in the pooled comparison, for the urea adverse-event outcome, and for the active-plus-moisturiser severity outcome.[^1] Certainty was low for the moisturiser-versus-no-moisturiser outcomes (severity, flares, corticosteroid use) and for participant-reported skin improvement with urea, and low to very low for the oat-containing comparison.[^1] The plain-language summary states that most other outcomes rested on low to moderate certainty evidence.[^3]

## Funding

Forty-six of the 77 included trials were funded by pharmaceutical companies.[^3]

## Limitations

The authors rated 36 of the 77 trials at high risk of bias and 34 at unclear risk, leaving 7 at low risk.[^1] The plain-language summary attributes the main limits to risk of bias, such as lack of blinding or missing data, and to trials with too few participants, which reduces precision.[^3] The database searches ran to December 2015.[^1] Across comparisons, the authors reported that no moisturiser was shown to be better than another.[^2]
