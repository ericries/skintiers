A split-scar randomized trial in Mohs micrographic surgery patients found that rapidly absorbable polyglactin 910 suture produced better blinded dermatologist scar-cosmesis scores at 6 months than fast absorbing gut suture, relevant to [[scarring]] outcomes after facial surgery.[^1]

**Design:** Randomized, blinded, split-scar clinical trial comparing two absorbable suture materials for epidermal closure.[^1]

**Population / n:** 105 patients with facial wounds from Mohs micrographic surgery.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** 5-0 rapidly absorbable polyglactin 910 (Vicryl Rapide) versus 5-0 fast absorbing gut suture, each placed on one side of the same split scar for epidermal closure.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** At 6 months, blinded dermatologist assessments favored polyglactin 910 over fast absorbing gut on all three scales: "VAS 76.5(14.5) versus 73.2(15.2), p = .05; SBSES 4.4(0.9) versus 4.0(1.1), p = .006; WES 5.4(1.0) versus 5.1(1.1), p = .05." Plastic surgeon assessments also favored polyglactin 910, but the abstract does not report those differences as statistically significant.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** The differences are small in absolute terms (roughly 3-4 points on the Visual Analog Scale and 0.3-0.4 points on the Stony Brook and Wound Evaluation Scales) and two of the three dermatologist comparisons sit right at the p = .05 threshold; only the Stony Brook Scar Evaluation Scale (p = .006) is more clearly significant. The abstract reports no effect-size statistic (e.g., Cohen's d) beyond these raw score differences and p-values.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies to epidermal suture choice for facial scars after Mohs micrographic surgery, evaluated by blinded dermatologist photographic review; plastic surgeon raters did not find the same differences statistically significant, and the abstract states no adequately powered randomized comparison of absorbable suture types existed before this trial, without disclosing further limitations (e.g., blinding method, follow-up beyond 6 months). Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full Dermatologic Surgery text.[^1]
