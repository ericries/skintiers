A year-long randomized vehicle-controlled trial asking whether daily sunscreen use prevents the microscopic skin changes that ordinary, non-holiday sun exposure produces across the seasons; it is the trial behind the sunscreen row on [[skin-longevity]] and a rare controlled human result for [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^1]

**Design:** Randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled, real-life exposure study over one year in Paris, France, with assessments at four seasonal time points. Measurement was by non-invasive in vivo multiphoton microscopy, not clinical grading or photography.[^1]

**Population:** 59 women of European descent, aged 55 to 65, Fitzpatrick phototypes II or III.[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** A daily broad-spectrum SPF and PPD photoprotection product "with enhanced UVA filtration", versus its own vehicle.[^1]

**Primary result, and what is missing from it:** Between winter and summer the vehicle group showed "a significant increase in skin color, melanin density, elastin density and a decrease in the 3D-SAAID aging index", and "These changes were not observed in the photoprotected group."[^1] The abstract reports directions and statistical significance only. It gives no effect sizes, no confidence intervals, no p-values and no baseline or endpoint values for any of the five measures, so the magnitude of the protection cannot be stated from the published abstract.

**Effect size:** Not reportable from the abstract. See above.

**Outcomes measured:** Skin colour, melanin density and its z-epidermal distribution, epidermal morphology, elastin density, and fibrillar collagen density in the papillary dermis, defined as the "first 20 µm-thick dermal sublayer below the dermal-epidermal junction", combined into a 3D SAAID index the authors describe as "a marker of photoaging".[^1]

**Generalizes to:** Women in their late fifties and early sixties with light skin, under everyday urban sun exposure at the latitude of Paris. One city, one phototype band, one sex, one age decade. It says nothing directly about darker phototypes, younger skin, or high-intensity or holiday exposure.

**Key limitation:** The outcomes are subclinical rather than visible. The authors say so themselves, framing the work as "highlighting subclinical skin changes" and concluding only that daily photoprotection mitigated them, with prevention of "long-term UV-induced clinical aging signs" stated as the implication rather than as something the study measured.[^1] No participant was shown to look different.

**Funding and conflicts:** The PubMed record carries no conflict-of-interest statement and names no funder.[^1] That is an absence in the record, not evidence of independence, and the trial tests a commercial photoprotection formula with enhanced UVA filtration.

**Why it is on this site:** It is the only randomized controlled trial in people among the recent literature that uses the "skin longevity" framing, which is the point [[skin-longevity]] turns on. Independently of that framing it is also a controlled, year-long test of daily sunscreen against its own vehicle, which is unusual; most sunscreen evidence is either short-term or uncontrolled. The general case for sunscreen is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and is not repeated here.
