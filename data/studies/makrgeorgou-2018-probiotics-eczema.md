Probiotics for treating eczema is a 2018 Cochrane systematic review of 39 randomised controlled trials (2599 participants) that found orally taken probiotics probably make little or no difference to eczema symptoms, and concluded that using probiotics to treat existing eczema is not currently evidence-based.[^1][^2]

## What It Asked

Eczema (atopic dermatitis) is a common, long-lasting, itchy skin condition. Probiotics, meaning live micro-organisms swallowed in capsules, drinks, or formula, have been marketed and studied as a treatment for it, on the idea that changing the gut's bacteria might calm the immune activity behind the disease. This review set out to answer one question: in people who already have eczema, does taking probiotics reduce their symptoms, improve their quality of life, or lower the severity a doctor measures, and is it safe? It is an update of a Cochrane review first published in 2008, which had suggested probiotics may not work and pointed to gaps in the evidence.[^1]

Note the scope. This is about *treating* eczema someone already has, taken by mouth. It is a separate question from whether a mother or infant taking probiotics early in life lowers a baby's chance of *developing* eczema later, which is a different body of evidence.[^1]

## Who Was In It and What They Did

The authors pooled 39 randomised controlled trials with 2599 randomised participants, from database searches updated to January 2017.[^1][^2] Participants ranged in age from the first year of life to 55 years, and had mild to severe eczema diagnosed by a doctor; only six of the studies looked at adults.[^1] The trials were run mostly in Europe or Asia, in both primary and specialist care.[^1]

The probiotics tested were bacteria of the *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacteria* species, taken alone or in combination, with or without prebiotics.[^1] There was no standard dose: each research team used its own strains, doses, and concentrations.[^1] Treatment lasted from four weeks to six months, and participants were followed for anywhere from zero to 36 months after treatment ended.[^1] In the main results, probiotics were compared against no probiotic (placebo or no treatment).[^1]

A randomised controlled trial randomly assigns people to the treatment or a comparison so the groups are alike except for the treatment, which makes it the strongest single-study design for testing whether something works. A systematic review like this one combines many such trials using a method called meta-analysis. The authors also used GRADE, a standard system for rating how much confidence to place in each result, and ran a trial sequential analysis, which checks whether enough people have now been studied to settle a question.[^1]

## What It Found (With the Numbers Explained)

**Patient-rated symptoms: probably no meaningful difference.** Across 13 trials (754 participants), symptoms rated by patients or parents on a 0-to-20 scale were 0.44 points lower with probiotics (95% CI -1.22 to 0.33; moderate-quality evidence).[^1] That is under half a point on a 20-point scale, and the confidence interval crosses zero, meaning the range of plausible true effects includes "no difference at all." A confidence interval is the span within which the real effect most likely sits; when it straddles zero, the study cannot rule out that probiotics did nothing. The authors' trial sequential analysis found that the number of participants needed to detect even a modest improvement had already been passed, so, as they put it, "further trials with similar probiotic strains for this outcome at the end of active treatment may be futile."[^1]

**Quality of life: no evidence of a difference.** Across six studies (552 participants), quality of life measured with validated eczema-specific questionnaires showed a standardised mean difference of 0.03 (95% CI -0.36 to 0.42; low-quality evidence).[^1] A standardised mean difference near zero means the two groups scored essentially the same, and again the interval crosses zero.

**Doctor-rated severity: a small reduction, below what a patient would notice.** Across 24 trials (1596 participants), total SCORAD, a 0-to-103 score that combines a doctor's rating of the eczema with the patient's own scoring of itch and sleep loss, was 3.91 points lower with probiotics (95% CI -5.86 to -1.96; low-quality evidence).[^1] Here the interval does not cross zero, so this reduction is statistically significant. But size matters: the smallest change on SCORAD that patients tend to actually notice, the minimum clinically important difference, has been estimated at 8.7 points.[^1] A 3.91-point drop is well under that threshold, which is why the authors called it small and of uncertain importance.

**Safety: no signal of extra harm in the trials.** Across seven trials (402 participants), the risk of an adverse event during treatment was not clearly different with probiotics (risk ratio 1.54, 95% CI 0.90 to 2.63; low-quality evidence).[^1] A risk ratio of 1.54 points toward more events, but the interval crosses 1.0 (the "no difference" mark for a ratio), so the trials cannot confirm a real increase. The side effects that were reported were gastrointestinal, meaning tummy and gut upset.[^1]

The authors flagged a major caveat running through the numbers: "significant to extreme levels of unexplainable heterogeneity between the results of individual studies," meaning the trials disagreed with each other far more than chance would explain, which makes any single pooled figure harder to trust.[^1]

Their overall conclusion was that, compared with no probiotic, currently available strains "probably make little or no difference in improving patient-rated eczema symptoms," and that the "use of probiotics for the treatment of eczema is currently not evidence-based."[^1][^3]

## How Strong Is the Evidence

The design is strong in principle: a Cochrane systematic review pooling randomised controlled trials is near the top of the evidence hierarchy. The weakness is in the underlying trials. Using GRADE, the authors rated the patient-rated symptom result as moderate-quality (meaning fairly confident, but the true effect could still differ from the estimate) and rated quality of life, doctor-rated severity, and adverse events as low-quality (meaning limited confidence, and the true effect may well be different).[^1] The two problems dragging certainty down were the disagreement between trials and the general risk of bias in how the trials were run.[^1]

## Funding and Conflicts of Interest

Among the included trials, the review reports that "Ten studies were funded by companies supplying the probiotics," and another four did not declare their funding source.[^1] Several of the review's authors declared industry ties: the lead clinical author disclosed speaker honoraria, conference travel support, and a research grant from Danone Research, whose parent company markets probiotic products, and another author disclosed past advisory-board roles with Danone Nutricia and Nestlé and speaker fees from companies including Danone Nutricia, Abbott, and Nestlé Health Science.[^1] Other authors declared nothing to disclose.[^1]

## Limitations

The evidence is limited by the trials themselves, not the review's methods. Most included studies were judged at unclear risk of bias; six had high attrition bias (too many participants dropped out), and only nine of the 39 were judged at low risk of bias overall.[^1] The trials used many different probiotic strains, doses, and durations rather than one standardised treatment, and they disagreed with one another to a degree the authors could not explain.[^1] Only six trials studied adults, so the findings lean heavily on children.[^1] Some outcomes, including quality of life and adverse events, were measured by only a small number of studies.[^1] The searches were updated to January 2017 (with further searches in 2018 not yet incorporated), so trials published afterward are not included.[^1]

The review also draws a boundary around what it does and does not show. It addresses *treating* established eczema with oral probiotics; it is not about *preventing* eczema in babies, and it does not test topical "probiotic" creams. On safety, the authors note that while this update found no increase in adverse events during the trials, a separate search from the original review indicated "probiotic treatment carries a small risk of adverse events."[^1]
