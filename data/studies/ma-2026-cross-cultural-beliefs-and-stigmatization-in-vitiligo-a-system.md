A systematic review of 23 studies found that cultural beliefs about [[vitiligo]] (contagion, divine punishment, supernatural causes) and the resulting stigma, discrimination, and quality-of-life impairment are consistently more severe in African, Middle Eastern, and South Asian populations, in women, and in darker skin phototypes, than in Western cohorts.[^1]

**Design:** Systematic review of the literature (databases: PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO), including "original studies in English assessing relevant psychosocial or cultural domains" of vitiligo.[^1]

**Population / n:** Twenty-three studies met inclusion criteria; the abstract does not state a total pooled participant count across those studies.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Not an intervention trial, but a thematic synthesis comparing cultural attributions, stigmatization, and quality-of-life (QoL) outcomes across geographic/cultural populations (e.g., African, Middle Eastern, and South Asian cohorts versus Western cohorts).[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "Cultural attributions of vitiligo varied widely, with some populations linking the disease to contagion, divine punishment, or supernatural causes," and "these beliefs were more prevalent in regions with lower health literacy and limited access to dermatologic care." Stigmatization manifested as "social exclusion, employment and marital discrimination, and internalized shame," and was "more severe in female patients and individuals with darker skin phototypes in certain regions." "QoL impairment was greater in African, Middle Eastern, and South Asian populations compared to Western cohorts." Reported coping strategies included "concealment, spiritual reliance, and use of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM)."[^1] The abstract gives no percentages, sample-size breakdowns, or statistical test figures (no %, n=, or p-values) for any of these comparisons.

**Effect size / interpretation:** No quantitative effect size is reported; this is a qualitative/narrative synthesis of thematic patterns across 23 heterogeneous studies, not a pooled or meta-analytic estimate. The consistent direction across studies (greater stigma and QoL burden in lower-health-literacy, non-Western regions and in women and darker skin phototypes) is the review's central finding, stated descriptively rather than numerically.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies broadly to patients with vitiligo, with the clearest signal for those in African, Middle Eastern, and South Asian settings and in regions with limited dermatologic-care access, where cultural stigma appears most amplified. Key limitation: only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full text, so the review's quality appraisal, per-study sample sizes, and any quantitative synthesis (if present in the full article) could not be verified; the abstract itself reports no effect sizes or statistics, only descriptive thematic findings.[^1]
