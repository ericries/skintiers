A systematic review of 40 studies mapping how menopause and menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) relate to six common skin conditions, finding the strongest and most consistent association for hair loss and only mixed or directional signals for the rest, with no pooled effect sizes.[^1]

**Design:** Systematic review following PRISMA, searching PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science from inception to September 2024. Narrative synthesis, with no meta-analysis or pooled effect sizes.[^1]

**Population / n:** 40 included studies; the investigational cohorts were largely menopausal women, though participant characteristics varied. Conditions examined were alopecia, psoriasis, acne, rosacea, melasma, and hidradenitis suppurativa.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** None; this is a synthesis of observational evidence relating menopausal status and MHT exposure to dermatological outcomes, not a trial.[^1]

**Primary results (directional, no effect sizes reported):**

- Alopecia, particularly frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA) and female pattern hair loss (FPHL), "showed the strongest postmenopausal associations, with most cases presenting after menopause and earlier or surgical menopause conferring greater risk."[^1]
- Acne and rosacea "generally improved" after menopause, whereas psoriasis "frequently persisted or worsened" (though objective assessments were limited), melasma showed mixed outcomes including greater extra-facial involvement, and hidradenitis suppurativa responses were inconsistent.[^1]
- MHT "was linked to increased risk of FFA and rosacea, whereas findings for other dermatoses were more variable or absent."[^1]

**Effect size:** None reported. The review gives directions of association only, not magnitudes, confidence intervals, or pooled risk estimates.

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies to menopausal women, but the evidence base is observational and heterogeneous, "most of the studies involved MHT formulations that are less commonly used in current clinical practice," and objective disease assessment was limited for some conditions. It establishes plausible hormone-linked patterns worth considering clinically, not causal or quantified effects.
