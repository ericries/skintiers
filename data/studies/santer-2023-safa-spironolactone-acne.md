The SAFA trial is a 2023 randomised, double blind, placebo-controlled trial of oral spironolactone for persistent facial acne in adult women in England and Wales (410 randomised), the largest trial of its kind to date. It found spironolactone improved acne-related quality of life more than placebo, with a small difference at 12 weeks that grew substantially by 24 weeks.[^1]

## Design

The trial recruited women aged 18 and over with facial acne present for at least six months, of "sufficient severity to warrant treatment with oral antibiotics, as judged by the trial clinician," through primary and secondary healthcare and through community and social media advertising in England and Wales.[^1] Eligible participants had an investigator's global assessment (IGA) score of mild or worse; women with clear or almost clear skin (IGA 0-1) were excluded, along with those who had used spironolactone before, used oral isotretinoin in the previous six months, used oral antibiotics for more than a week in the previous month, had recently changed contraception or hormone treatment, were planning a pregnancy, or had a contraindication to spironolactone such as an estimated glomerular filtration rate under 60 mL/min/1.73m2 or a raised serum potassium.[^1] Women at risk of pregnancy had to be "willing to use their usual hormonal or barrier method of contraception for the first six months of the trial and for at least four weeks afterwards," and baseline visits included a pregnancy test and blood tests to rule out renal impairment or raised potassium.[^1]

Between 5 June 2019 and 31 August 2021, 1,267 women were assessed for eligibility and 410 were randomly assigned 1:1 to spironolactone (201) or matched placebo (209).[^1] The primary analysis included 342 participants (176 spironolactone, 166 placebo).[^1] Participants started at 50 mg/day, increasing to 100 mg/day (two tablets) at week 6 if tolerated, continuing to week 24; they could keep using any topical acne treatment alongside the trial medication.[^1] At baseline, mean age was 29.2 years (SD 7.2), 84% of participants were of white ethnicity, acne severity was 46% mild, 40% moderate and 13% severe by IGA, 42% were already using a hormonal treatment, and 84% were using a topical treatment.[^1] The trial ran partly during the covid-19 pandemic, which forced some follow-up visits to move to phone or video call.[^1]

## What It Found

The primary outcome was the Acne-QoL (Acne-Specific Quality of Life) symptom subscale at week 12, scored 0 to 30 with higher scores meaning better quality of life.[^1] At week 12, mean scores were 19.2 (SD 6.1) for spironolactone versus 17.8 (SD 5.6) for placebo, an adjusted difference of 1.27 (95% CI 0.07 to 2.46).[^1] At week 24, a secondary timepoint, mean scores were 21.2 (SD 5.9) for spironolactone versus 17.4 (SD 5.8) for placebo, an adjusted difference of 3.45 (95% CI 2.16 to 4.75), a larger gap than at week 12.[^1] The authors reported that "results for all the other Acne-QoL subscales and for total Acne-QoL score at 12 and 24 weeks were also statistically significant in favour of the spironolactone group."[^1]

Participant-reported improvement followed the same pattern: at week 24, 82% of participants on spironolactone reported their acne had improved versus 63% on placebo, an odds ratio of 2.72 (95% CI 1.50 to 4.93).[^1] At week 12 the same measure had not yet separated the groups (72% versus 68%).[^1]

Investigator's global assessment (IGA) treatment success, defined as clear or almost clear skin (grade 0 or 1) with at least a two-grade improvement from baseline, was assessed at week 12: 31 of 168 (19%) on spironolactone achieved it versus 9 of 160 (6%) on placebo, an odds ratio of 5.18 (95% CI 2.18 to 12.28).[^1] Participant's global assessment success (the same clear/almost-clear-plus-two-grade definition, self-rated) was 21% versus 12% at week 12 (not statistically significant) and 32% versus 11% at week 24, an odds ratio of 3.76 (95% CI 1.95 to 7.28).[^1] By week 24, 71% of participants on spironolactone reported being satisfied with treatment versus 43% on placebo, an adjusted odds ratio of 3.12 (95% CI 1.80 to 5.41).[^1]

A prespecified analysis restricted to participants who took at least 80% of their study medication found a larger effect: an adjusted mean difference in the Acne-QoL symptom subscale of 5.13 (95% CI 3.17 to 7.08).[^1]

## Adverse Events

More participants on spironolactone reported an adverse reaction than on placebo: 128 of 201 (64%) versus 107 of 209 (51%), P=0.01.[^1] The clearest individual difference was headache, reported by 41 of 201 (20%) on spironolactone versus 25 of 209 (12%) on placebo, P=0.02.[^1] Other events, including dizziness or vertigo (19% versus 12%), polyuria (31% versus 25%), breast tenderness (20% versus 18%), breast enlargement (15% versus 12%), irregular menstrual bleeding between weeks 12 and 24 (32% versus 35%), nausea (11% versus 8%), fatigue (11% versus 14%) and reduced libido (8% versus 5%), did not differ significantly between groups.[^1] "No serious adverse reactions were reported."[^1] The authors noted that although "spironolactone is cautioned against in pregnancy, the effects are probably less teratogenic than oral tetracyclines, which are commonly used for acne in young women."[^1]

## Limitations

The authors flagged that the week 12 difference in the primary outcome, a point estimate of 1.27, fell short of the two-point difference the trial was originally powered to detect, though the confidence interval extended past it and the week 24 result exceeded it.[^1] Covid-19 forced adaptations that the authors said were "crucial to completing the trial but led to limitations," including remote follow-up visits by phone or video, reduced in-person collection of investigator-assessed severity, and less complete adherence data.[^1] Follow-up was slightly less complete in the placebo group than the spironolactone group (79% versus 88%).[^1] Only 7% of the 389 participants with ethnicity recorded were from an ethnicity other than white.[^1] The trial compared spironolactone against placebo only; it did not test spironolactone head-to-head against other acne treatments such as oral antibiotics or combined oral contraceptives.[^1] An accompanying analysis of the minimum clinically important difference for the Acne-QoL "showed no firm conclusions," so the authors could not say how large a symptom-subscale gap needs to be before it is noticeable to a typical patient.[^1]

The trial was funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research's Health Technology Assessment programme, and the authors reported no financial relationships with any organisation that might have an interest in the results.[^1] The authors concluded: "Spironolactone improved outcomes compared with placebo, with greater differences at week 24 than week 12. Spironolactone is a useful alternative to oral antibiotics for women with acne."[^1]

This is randomised, placebo-controlled evidence for oral spironolactone in women's acne, one of the prescription hormonal options named on [[acne]]. As an oral, prescription-only, hormonally active drug, it is a different category of treatment from the topical options such as [[azelaic-acid]] and [[benzoyl-peroxide]]: it requires a prescriber and the potassium, renal, and contraception monitoring described above, and the trial positioned it as an alternative to oral antibiotics rather than to topical acne treatments.
