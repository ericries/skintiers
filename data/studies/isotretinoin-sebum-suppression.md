The magnitude of oral isotretinoin's effect on skin oil comes from a cluster of 1982 clinical studies; the most-cited, by Goldstein and colleagues in Archives of Dermatology, measured an 88.4% drop in sebum production in seven men with severe acne after 16 weeks of treatment, alongside a histologic shrinking of the sebaceous glands.[^1]

## What They Asked and Why It Matters

Sebum is the oily material the skin's sebaceous glands secrete. Overproduction of it is one of the core features of acne, because oil plus dead-cell debris and bacteria is what blocks and inflames a pore. In the late 1970s and early 1980s a new oral drug, 13-cis-retinoic acid (isotretinoin, later sold as Accutane), was found to clear severe nodulocystic acne, the deep, scarring kind. The obvious question was mechanistic and medical: how much does it actually cut oil output, how long does that suppression last after the drug is stopped, and is the reduction in oil enough to explain why the acne clears. Three groups measured this directly in acne patients.[^1][^2][^3]

## Who Was Studied and What They Did

Goldstein, Comite, Mescon and Pochi gave oral isotretinoin at 1 mg per kg of body weight per day for 16 weeks to seven men with severe acne. They measured sebum production before, during and after treatment, took skin biopsies to look at the sebaceous glands under the microscope, and counted acne lesions, following the men to 32 weeks (16 weeks past the end of dosing).[^1]

Strauss and Stranieri looked at the other end of the timeline: durability. They measured sebum production in twenty patients whose isotretinoin had been stopped for at least 20 weeks, tracking how far and for how long oil output stayed below its starting level, with some patients followed for more than a year.[^2]

King, Jones, Daltrey and Cunliffe ran the most rigorous design of the three: forty-eight acne patients in a double-blind, dose-response study (neither patient nor assessor knew the dose). They measured the sebum excretion rate, the production rate of free fatty acids in the oil, the skin's bacterial populations, and clinical acne severity, so they could see the order in which those things changed.[^3] Sebum excretion rate is measured by collecting and weighing the oil that comes to the skin surface over a fixed time, so a lower rate means less oil is reaching the surface.

## What They Found

The headline magnitude is large. Goldstein's group reported "there was an 88.4% decrease in sebum production" after 16 weeks, together with "a marked reduction histologically in sebaceous gland size after 16 weeks of treatment."[^1] Sebum output did not stay fully suppressed once the drug stopped: the same paper describes "a partial recovery of glandular activity at 32 weeks."[^1] So the biology behind the number is a physical shrinking of the oil glands, not a surface effect, and it partly reverses after treatment ends.

The durability data fill in that recovery curve. Strauss and Stranieri found that in some patients oil output bounced back quickly while in others it stayed low for a long time: "sebum production had returned to pretreatment levels by 30 weeks in seven subjects," but a "30% to 80% reduction in sebaceous gland activity was still present" in eight subjects for as long as 80 weeks.[^2] Four of those eight, followed for more than a year, still had marked suppression when last tested.[^2] The authors were cautious about reading too much into this, noting that continued gland inhibition "obviously is not the only" reason acne stays in remission after treatment.[^2]

The double-blind study confirmed the direction and added the sequence. King and colleagues reported "there was a marked clinical improvement with a concomitant reduction in sebum excretion rate" and free fatty acid production.[^3] The skin's bacteria (including the acne-associated propionibacteria) also fell, but the timing mattered: "the decline in micro-organisms occurred after the reduction in sebum and FFA production," which the authors read as the bacterial drop being a downstream consequence of removing the oil the bacteria feed on rather than a direct antibacterial action.[^3]

One consistent caveat runs through all three. The oil reduction was very large, but it did not perfectly track the acne result. Goldstein's group noted that two of their seven patients responded poorly despite strong gland suppression, and concluded that "the marked sebostatic effect of isotretinoin may not be the sole explanation" for how it works in acne.[^1] The acne numbers in that study (a 36.2% reduction in nodulocystic lesions at the end of treatment, rising to 47.2% by the end of the 16-week follow-up) improved more slowly than oil output fell.[^1]

## How Strong the Evidence Is

These are small, early clinical studies, and their strengths differ. Goldstein's and Strauss's were open studies in single-digit-to-twenty patient groups without a placebo arm, so they document the size and time-course of an effect rather than proving it against a control.[^1][^2] King and colleagues' 48-patient design was double-blind and dose-response, which is a stronger footing for the causal claim that the drug, not the passage of time, drove the change.[^3] The endpoint in all three is a biological one, sebum output and gland size, measured directly rather than by appearance. The three agree closely on direction and rough magnitude despite different teams, countries and designs, and the finding has held up: the modern FDA prescribing information for isotretinoin describes the same mechanism, that the drug "inhibits sebaceous gland function and keratinization," and that the drop in oil "reflects a reduction in sebaceous gland size and an inhibition of sebaceous gland differentiation."[^4] The label also states plainly that "the decrease in sebum secretion is temporary and is related to the dose," matching the partial recovery Goldstein and Strauss measured.[^1][^2][^4]

## Funding and Conflicts

Isotretinoin (13-cis-retinoic acid) was developed and marketed by Hoffmann-La Roche as Accutane.[^4] The three 1982 trials predate structured conflict-of-interest disclosure, and their published records carry no funding statement.[^1][^2][^3]

## What It Does and Does Not Show

These studies quantify one thing well: oral isotretinoin produces the largest and most durable suppression of sebaceous-gland output of any documented intervention, on the order of the 88.4% Goldstein's group measured, driven by a physical reduction in gland size that partly reverses after the course ends.[^1][^2] That is a biological effect on an organ, studied as the mechanism behind clearing severe, scarring acne, a medical condition, and the same reduction is what a patient experiences cosmetically as much less oily, less shiny skin. The health context, treating nodulocystic acne, is where the drug is used and where it was measured; the reduction in surface oiliness is a secondary, cosmetic consequence of the same gland shrinkage.

They do not show that this makes isotretinoin an oil-control option to reach for casually. The suppression is a systemic drug effect, and isotretinoin carries a boxed FDA warning that "there is an extremely high risk that life-threatening birth defects will result if pregnancy occurs" during treatment, so in the United States it is prescription-only and dispensed under a restricted program, iPLEDGE, that ties it to prescriber and patient registration.[^4] The 1982 studies also do not settle mechanism completely: their own authors found the acne benefit did not fully track the oil reduction, so gland suppression is a major part of how the drug works but, by their account, not the whole of it.[^1][^2][^3] Whether isotretinoin is appropriate for a given person is a clinical decision made with a prescriber under that program.[^4]
