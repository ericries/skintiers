A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 19 randomized trials of peptides for skin aging and found only a modest overall wrinkle benefit, one that was largely driven by oral peptide supplements rather than the topical peptides used in skincare.[^1]

**Design:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, following PRISMA, with a MEDLINE / CENTRAL / Web of Science search, Cochrane RoB 2 risk-of-bias assessment, and a random-effects model.[^1] Prospectively registered (CRD420250652779).[^1]

**Population / n:** 19 RCTs involving 1,341 participants.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Oral or topical peptide formulations versus control, assessed on hydration, elasticity, wrinkles, and brightness.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** Peptides "significantly improved hydration and brightness, with a modest pooled effect on wrinkle reduction (MD = 0.27, p = 0.04)." Subgroup analysis "indicated that this benefit was largely driven by oral polypeptides (MD = 1.5, p = 0.01)." The review reported that "effects on elasticity and density were inconsistent," while peptides "were well tolerated, with minimal adverse events reported across trials."[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Bears on [[peptides]] and the topical peptides the site profiles ([[palmitoyl-pentapeptide-4-matrixyl]], [[copper-peptides]], [[argireline-acetyl-hexapeptide-8]]): the measurable wrinkle benefit came mainly from oral polypeptides, so this analysis does not establish that topical peptides reduce wrinkles. The authors conclude peptides "appear to be safe, non-invasive anti-aging agents, though larger RCTs with standardized outcomes and histopathologic assessment are warranted."[^1] Pooling two delivery routes (oral and topical) across heterogeneous peptides and outcome scales limits how much any single topical product's effect can be read from the pooled numbers.[^1] The authors declared no commercial or financial conflict of interest.[^1]
