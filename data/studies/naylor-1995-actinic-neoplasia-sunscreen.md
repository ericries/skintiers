A prospective, double-blind controlled trial in high-risk patients in Lubbock, Texas found that daily high-SPF sunscreen use reduced the rate of new precancerous skin lesions versus placebo over a 2-year follow-up.[^1]

**Design:** Prospective, double-blind, controlled trial of daily sunscreen versus placebo, conducted December 1987 to December 1990 over a 2-year period.[^1]

**Population / n:** High-risk volunteers from university and Veterans Affairs dermatology practices in Lubbock, Texas, with existing actinic keratoses or a nonmelanoma skin cancer history, ongoing sun exposure, and no regular sunscreen use. "Fifty-three volunteers were initially enrolled in the study, and 37 came for the final 24-month visit."[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Daily application of a high-SPF sunscreen versus a placebo.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "The rate of appearance of new precancerous skin lesions was less for the treatment group than for control subjects." The abstract also reports that darker skin was associated with fewer actinic keratoses, women developed fewer lesions than men, and lower baseline lesion counts predicted lower follow-up counts. "The numbers of new nonmelanoma skin cancers appearing during the study period were too small for statistical analysis." The abstract does not state a percentage reduction, lesion count, or p-value for the primary precancerous-lesion result.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** No quantitative effect size (percentage, count, or p-value) is given in the abstract for the reduction in new precancerous lesions; the conclusion states only that "the regular use of sunscreens can significantly reduce cutaneous neoplasia, as indicated by its suppression of precancerous lesions."[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies specifically to a high-risk, secondary-prevention population (existing actinic keratoses or prior nonmelanoma skin cancer), distinct from general-population photoprotection trials; only 37 of 53 enrolled volunteers completed the 24-month visit, and the study was underpowered to assess nonmelanoma skin cancer incidence itself, with the authors noting "a longer and/or larger study would be necessary to demonstrate an effect on malignant lesions." Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full Archives of Dermatology text. Feeds the [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] photoprotection-evidence discussion as a third, high-risk-population data point alongside the Nambour and Thompson/Marks trials.[^1]
