A 24-month prospective, single-center case-control study in 120 matched organ-transplant recipients found that daily use of a high-SPF sunscreen reduced new actinic keratoses and eliminated new invasive squamous-cell carcinomas compared with sun-protection counseling alone.[^1]

**Design:** Prospective, single-center, case-control study comparing a sunscreen-use intervention group against a sun-protection-counseling-only control group in solid-organ transplant recipients, followed for 24 months.[^1]

**Population / n:** "Hundred and twenty matched (age, sex, skin type, graft, transplant duration, previous post-transplant skin malignancies) organ transplant recipients (40 heart, 40 kidney, 40 liver grafted)"; 60 assigned to the sunscreen group and 60 to the control group. "All 120 patients completed the 24 months study."[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** "Both groups received equally written and oral information on sun protection measures. Sixty patients were provided with a free broad spectrum study-sunscreen (SPF>50, high-UVA absorption) for daily application of 2 mg cm(-2) to the head, neck, forearms, and hands"; the other 60 received the counseling only, without study sunscreen.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "Within this 24 month study interval 42 of the 120 patients developed 82 new AK (-102 sunscreen group vs. +82 control; P<0.01), 8 new invasive SCC (0 vs. 8; P<0.01) and 11 BCC (2 vs. 9; ns). In spite of equal numbers of AK at baseline, a marked difference in favor of the intent-to-treat sunscreen group was recorded after 24 months (89 vs. 273; P<0.01, mean difference 3.07 [1.76-4.36]) and the lesion count was significantly lower as compared to the initial visit (89 vs. 191; P<0.01, mean difference 1.7 [0.68-2.72]). With an average of 5.6 applications per week throughout the 24 months the study sunscreen was generally well tolerated."[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** Zero new invasive SCCs occurred in the sunscreen group versus 8 in controls (P<0.01), and final actinic-keratosis counts were 89 (sunscreen) vs. 273 (control), P<0.01, mean difference 3.07 [1.76-4.36] (interval type not specified in abstract); the BCC difference (2 vs. 9) was not statistically significant. As a secondary finding, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was lower in the sunscreen group than controls (mean 53 ng/mL vs. 60 ng/mL); the abstract reports this difference without a p-value. The authors' own interpretation: "Regular use of sunscreens, as part of a consequent UV-protection strategy, may prevent the development of further AK and invasive SCC and, to a lesser degree, BCC in immune-compromised organ transplant recipients."[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies specifically to chronically immunocompromised solid-organ transplant recipients (heart, kidney, and liver grafts), a population at markedly elevated non-melanoma skin cancer risk versus the general population; the abstract does not establish how far the effect generalizes to immunocompetent users, which is where [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]'s broader evidence base applies. Key limitations: this is a case-control design (not randomized), sunscreen use was unblinded and self-/investigator-assigned rather than placebo-controlled, group sizes were modest (n=60 per arm) at a single center, and only the PubMed abstract was accessed here, not the full British Journal of Dermatology text, so methodological detail (e.g., exact statistical tests, whether vitamin D groups differed significantly) beyond what the abstract states could not be verified.
