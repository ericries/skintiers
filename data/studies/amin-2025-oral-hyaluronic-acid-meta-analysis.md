A meta-analysis of 7 randomized trials of ORAL hyaluronic acid supplements, not topical.[^1] **Record it with two cautions: the abstract reports significance with no effect size anywhere, and it narrates its three null outcomes as "a general trend of improvement".** Topical hyaluronic acid is a separate question, graded on [[hyaluronic-acid]], and nothing here transfers to it.

**What it tested:** oral HA supplementation against placebo on "hydration, elasticity, firmness, wrinkle reduction, and transepidermal water loss".[^1]

**Design:** meta-analysis of "7 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the use of oral HA as a supplement", comparing "the standardized mean differences between the treatment (HA) and control (placebo) groups".[^1] PubMed indexes the publication type as Meta-Analysis.

**Search:** "Electronic databases (PubMed and Google Scholar) were searched for relevant articles."[^1] Two sources, one of which is a general search engine. No Embase, no Cochrane CENTRAL, no trial registries, no grey literature. For a meta-analysis that is a thin evidence base to draw from, and the paper does not say whether screening was done in duplicate.

**Population / n:** NOT STATED. The abstract gives no total participant count, no per-trial sample sizes, no age range and no completion rates. Its own conclusion refers to "the study's limited sample size", without saying what it was.[^1]

**Primary result:** "The meta-analysis revealed statistically significant improvements in skin hydration, elasticity, and wrinkle depth following oral HA supplementation."[^1] No standardized mean difference, confidence interval or p-value appears for any outcome, so the DIRECTION is reported and the MAGNITUDE is not. For deciding whether a supplement is worth taking, magnitude is the part that matters.

**The three outcomes that failed, and how they are described:** "Although the effects of oral HA on skin firmness, wrinkle volume, and transepidermal water loss were not statistically significant, a general trend of improvement was observed in these parameters."[^1] Three of six outcomes did not reach significance. Calling them a general trend of improvement reads a direction into results the analysis could not distinguish from chance, which is the standard way a null is narrated as a near-positive. Treat those three as undetermined.

**Effect size:** not computable from the abstract.

**Who it generalizes to:** cannot be determined, because no population is described.

**Key limitation:** the paper names it itself, that "the study's limited sample size and heterogeneity among included studies call for larger, more robust trials to confirm these findings".[^1] Add the two this page found: a two-database search, and significance reported without magnitude. Also note what the PubMed record does not contain: no author affiliations and no conflict-of-interest statement are recorded, so funding and sponsorship cannot be assessed either way, and no claim is made here about them.
