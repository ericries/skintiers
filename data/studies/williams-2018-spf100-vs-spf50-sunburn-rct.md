A randomized, double-blind, split-face trial under natural sunlight found SPF 100+ sunscreen prevented sunburn better than SPF 50+ when people applied it themselves during a real day outdoors.[^1]

**Design:** Natural sunlight, single-exposure, split-face, randomized, double-blind clinical trial in Vail, Colorado; each participant wore both sunscreens simultaneously (one per side of the face) during normal activities, with no use restrictions other than which side got which product. Erythema was clinically assessed the day after exposure.[^1]

**Population / n:** 199 healthy men and women aged 18 years or older.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** SPF 100+ sunscreen on one side of the face vs. SPF 50+ on the other, applied in conditions of actual use.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "Following an average 6.1 +/- 1.3 hours of sun exposure, investigator-blinded evaluation identified 55.3% of the participants (110 of 199) as more sunburned on the SPF 50+ protected side and 5% (10 of 199) on the SPF 100+ protected side. After exposure, 40.7% of the participants (81 of 199) exhibited increased erythema scores (by >=1) on the SPF 50+ protected side as compared with 13.6% (27 of 199) on the SPF 100+ protected side."[^1] The authors concluded SPF 100+ "was significantly more effective in protecting against sunburn than SPF 50+ sunscreen in actual use conditions."[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies to adults using sunscreen as people actually do (self-applied, typically under-applied) during prolonged outdoor sun exposure, and bears on the labeled-SPF ceiling debate covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]. The authors' stated limitation: "Single-day exposure may not extrapolate to benefits of longer-term protection."[^1] The first four authors were affiliated with Johnson & Johnson Consumer Inc, a sunscreen manufacturer.[^1] The endpoint was sunburn (a UVB-driven health outcome); the trial did not measure UVA protection or long-term outcomes.[^1]
