A randomized controlled trial found that mineral sunscreens leave a white cast that is three times more pronounced on darker skin tones, and that when application is uncontrolled, users apply less product and get less UV protection.[^1]

**Design:** Randomized controlled trial. Five mineral sunscreens (differing whiteness) and one chemical sunscreen were applied at a standardized 2 mg/cm² dose on the forearm; two of the six sunscreens were then randomly assigned for uncontrolled (participant-directed) application on the lower legs, and each participant's preferred product from that pair was applied to the face at their own discretion. Whitening and sun protection (UVA protection factor and SPF) were measured via imaging and Hybrid Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy (HDRS).[^1]

**Population / n:** Participants of "diverse skin tones"; the abstract does not state the number of participants (n).[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Controlled, standardized-dose application (2 mg/cm², forearm) versus uncontrolled, participant-directed application (lower legs, then face); mineral sunscreens (5 products, varying whiteness) versus a chemical sunscreen comparator.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "Controlled application with all mineral sunscreens led to significant whitening, which was 3 times more pronounced on darker skin tones." "Uncontrolled sunscreen application showed a significant reduction in the amount applied, whitening, and sunscreen efficacy." "Participants had reduced preference for more whitening sunscreens and selected sunscreens in product pairings that resulted in less whitening."[^1] The abstract states these effects as "significant" but gives no p-values, percentages, or numeric SPF/UVAPF values.

**Effect size / interpretation:** The one quantified figure in the abstract is the 3x greater whitening effect on darker versus lighter skin tones under controlled, standardized application. Under uncontrolled (real-world-style) use, participants applied less product, which the authors link to reduced whitening but also reduced sunscreen efficacy: people appear to underapply visibly-whitening mineral sunscreens, undermining the sun protection those products are meant to provide, especially for darker skin tones.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies to mineral [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] formulations and their real-world (mis)use across skin tones, not to any specific branded product tested. Only the PubMed abstract was accessed (not the full JDD text via SanovaWorks), so sample size, exact SPF/UVAPF values, and statistical test results (p-values) cannot be verified or reported beyond what the abstract states.
