A hairless-mouse barrier-disruption study found that occlusive membranes (which block transepidermal water loss) prevent stratum corneum lipid repletion and barrier recovery after acetone stripping, while vapor-permeable membranes (which allow water loss) permit normal recovery, identifying transepidermal water flux itself as the signal that triggers epidermal lipid synthesis.[^1]

**Design:** In-vivo mechanistic study in hairless mice. The barrier was abrogated with acetone, then animals were covered with occlusive membranes, covered with vapor-permeable membranes, or left uncovered, comparing rate of stratum corneum lipid repletion (biochemical and histochemical), barrier function recovery, and epidermal lipogenesis across the three conditions.[^1]

**Population / n:** Hairless mice; the abstract does not state the number of animals.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Acetone-induced barrier disruption followed by occlusive membrane vs. vapor-permeable membrane vs. no covering (uncovered).[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "Acetone treatment of hairless mice removed stainable neutral lipids from the stratum corneum, with repletion evident both biochemically and histochemically within 48 hr in uncovered animals." "When the animals were covered with an occlusive membrane, the usual return of stratum corneum lipids was aborted... barrier function did not recover normally." "Occlusion with vapor-permeable membranes allowed barrier function to recover normally," and "epidermal lipid synthesis was increased in animals covered with a vapor-permeable membrane" but not with an occlusive one. The abstract states no percentage, fold-change, or p-value figures for any of these effects.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** No quantitative effect size is given in the abstract; the result is reported as presence/absence of lipid repletion, barrier recovery, and lipogenic increase across the three membrane conditions, with normal recovery taking "within 48 hr" in uncovered animals. The authors conclude transepidermal water flux itself, not occlusion or barrier disruption per se, is the signal that regulates epidermal lipid synthesis and downstream barrier recovery.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** A hairless-mouse mechanistic model, not a human or clinical trial; only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full J Lipid Res text, so sample size and any quantitative figures beyond the "48 hr" timepoint cannot be verified. It is the foundational mechanistic basis for the "stop the insult, let TEWL signal repair" rationale behind [[skin-barrier-repair]] and [[skin-barrier-strengthening]].
