A laboratory ecotoxicology study found that oxybenzone (BP-3), a UV filter used in some [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] formulations, kills and deforms coral larvae and damages coral cells at concentrations found in reef waters near Hawaii and the U.S. Virgin Islands.[^1]

**Design:** Laboratory toxicology study exposing coral planulae (larvae) and cultured primary coral cells to a concentration range of oxybenzone under light and dark conditions, paired with environmental water sampling for oxybenzone contamination.[^1]

**Population / n:** Planulae of the coral species *Stylophora pistillata*; cultured primary cells from *S. pistillata* and seven other coral species (LC50/LC20 results reported for 7 species); environmental water samples from multiple sites in Hawaii and the U.S. Virgin Islands. The abstract does not state the number of larvae, cell replicates, or water samples per site.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Oxybenzone exposure across a concentration range versus unexposed controls, tested separately under light and dark conditions and at two exposure durations (8h and 24h for planulae; 4h for cells).[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** Planula LC50 values were "3.1 mg/L and 139 µg/L" (light, 8h/24h) and "16.8 mg/L and 779 µg/L" (darkness, 8h/24h). The deformity EC20 at 24h was "6.5 µg/L" in light and "10 µg/L" in darkness. Coral cell LC50s at 4h under light ranged "8-340 µg/L" across the seven species tested, and coral cell LC20s ranged "0.062-8 µg/L." Environmental sampling found oxybenzone contamination of "75 µg/L to 1.4 mg/L" at U.S. Virgin Islands sites and "0.8 and 19.2 µg/L" at Hawaiian sites. The authors report oxybenzone caused planula bleaching, deformity, skeletal (ossification-related) abnormality, and DNA damage in a concentration-dependent pattern, with light exposure worsening toxicity relative to darkness.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** The lowest reported toxicity threshold (cell LC20 of 0.062 µg/L) is below the lowest environmental concentrations measured at some Hawaiian sites (0.8 µg/L), and the deformity EC20 (6.5 to 10 µg/L) is below the concentrations measured in the U.S. Virgin Islands (75 µg/L to 1.4 mg/L), which therefore exceed the deformity threshold, a comparison the authors cite as evidence that oxybenzone contamination at measured reef sites can plausibly reach concentrations toxic to coral. Photoactivation (worse toxicity under light) is presented as a mechanistic driver.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Findings are laboratory toxicity thresholds (LC50/LC20/EC20) in one primary coral species (*Stylophora pistillata*) for planulae, plus cell assays across seven species; they establish hazard and a lab-to-field concentration comparison, not a controlled field study proving oxybenzone caused reef decline at any specific site, and the abstract gives no sample sizes (n) for larvae counts, cell replicates, or water samples. Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full text in *Archives of Environmental Contamination and Toxicology*, so methodological details (e.g., exact n per group, statistical tests) cannot be verified beyond what is stated above. This is the primary study underlying "reef-safe" sunscreen claims discussed on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^1]
