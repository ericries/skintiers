A vehicle-controlled trial in photodamaged forearm skin found that 10 to 12 months of 0.1% tretinoin cream raised collagen I formation 80 percent above baseline, versus a 14 percent decrease with vehicle alone.[^1]

**Design:** Two linked parts. A cross-sectional comparison of photodamaged forearm skin against sun-protected buttock skin in 26 healthy subjects established the baseline deficit. A separate vehicle-controlled treatment arm ran 10 to 12 months, with collagen I formation assessed by immunohistology.[^1]

**Population / n:** 26 subjects contributed the baseline photodamaged-vs-protected-skin comparison; 29 patients with photodamaged skin were treated, 15 with tretinoin and 14 with vehicle.[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** 0.1% tretinoin cream applied daily (n=15) versus vehicle cream applied daily (n=14).[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** At baseline, "collagen I formation was 56 percent less in the papillary dermis of photodamaged skin than in skin protected from the sun (P < 0.001) and was correlated with the clinical severity of photodamage (r = -0.58, P = 0.002)." With treatment, tretinoin produced "an 80 percent increase in collagen I formation, as compared with a 14 percent decrease in collagen formation with the use of vehicle alone (P = 0.006)."[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** The contrast between an 80 percent increase in collagen I formation with tretinoin and a 14 percent decrease with vehicle (P = 0.006) indicates tretinoin partially reverses, rather than merely halts, the collagen I deficit measured in photodamaged skin.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** The finding applies to sun-photodamaged forearm skin in adults treated for 10 to 12 months; collagen I formation is an immunohistologic biopsy marker, not a clinical wrinkle or biopsy-confirmed structural-collagen-content outcome, so it establishes a mechanism rather than a measured cosmetic result.
