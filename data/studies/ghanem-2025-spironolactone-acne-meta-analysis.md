A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised placebo-controlled trials of oral spironolactone for acne in women. It found a large effect on the objective endpoint and no significant difference on the subjective one, and its authors conclude the drug should move from off-label use to recommended standard of care.[^1] Spironolactone has no ingredient page here yet; the largest single trial is on [[santer-2023-safa-spironolactone-acne]] and the condition is on [[acne]].

**Design:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials comparing oral spironolactone with placebo in women with acne, searching PubMed, Embase and Cochrane, with trial sequential analysis. Analysis in Review Manager 5.4, heterogeneity by I-squared. Registered as PROSPERO CRD42024626984.[^1]

**Population:** 563 patients pooled from 5 RCTs, of whom 251 (42.9%) received spironolactone.[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** Oral spironolactone against placebo. The abstract does not state doses, treatment durations or how the pooled "objective assessment" endpoint was harmonised across the five trials.[^1]

**Primary result:** Objective assessment of acne improvement gave an odds ratio of 6.59 (95% CI 3.50 to 12.43, p < 0.00001) with I-squared 0%, which the authors describe as sixfold higher than placebo. The subjective assessment gave an odds ratio of 5.22 but a confidence interval of 0.62 to 44.24 (p < 0.13) with I-squared 85%, so it did not reach significance.[^1]

**Effect size:** The objective effect is large and the heterogeneity across trials for it is nil, which is unusually clean. The subjective estimate points the same way but its interval spans from a 38% reduction in odds to a 44-fold increase, which is another way of saying the pooled patient-reported data cannot locate the effect at all. On safety, menstrual irregularities (OR 1.09, 95% CI 0.37 to 3.25, p = 0.88) and breast enlargement (OR 1.37, 95% CI 0.79 to 2.38, p = 0.26) were both nonsignificant. Trial sequential analysis is reported as confirming the required sample size was reached in favour of spironolactone.[^1]

**Generalizes to:** Women with acne vulgaris prescribed an oral prescription drug. Spironolactone is systemic and prescription-only, so nothing here transfers to topical or over-the-counter care. The abstract reports no age range, acne severity threshold or geography for the pooled population.[^1]

**Key limitation:** The two endpoints disagree, and the abstract's conclusion follows only the one that worked. A clinician-rated measure improving sixfold while the patient-rated measure fails to separate is the finding a reader should carry away, not the headline. The safety reassurance is also narrower than it sounds: two specific adverse events were nonsignificant in a pooled 563 patients, which is far too small to characterise the risk profile of a potassium-sparing diuretic, and the abstract addresses neither hyperkalaemia nor use in pregnancy. The authors' recommendation that spironolactone "should be elevated from 'off-label' use to an officially recommended standard of care" is a policy position reaching well past five trials of 563 patients.[^1]

**Arithmetic worth noting:** the SAFA trial covered on this site randomised 410 women on its own, which is more than two thirds of this meta-analysis's entire pooled population of 563 across five trials. That makes it unlikely SAFA is among the five pooled here, and SAFA's primary endpoint was patient-reported acne-specific quality of life, the kind of measure that did not separate in this pool. Only the abstracts of both were read, so neither the inclusion list nor the reason for any exclusion was checked.[^1]
