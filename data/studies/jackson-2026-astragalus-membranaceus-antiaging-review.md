A systematic review concluded that Astragalus membranaceus and its bioactive compounds show anti-aging promise in mostly laboratory and experimental models, but that the human clinical evidence is thin and unquantified, so the authors call for randomized trials before its use in anti-aging skincare can be considered proven.[^1]

**Design:** Systematic review following PRISMA guidelines, based on a comprehensive PubMed search from 2015 to 2025 for clinical and experimental studies of the anti-aging effects of Astragalus membranaceus.[^1]

**Population / n:** Not a single-population trial; the review pooled heterogeneous "clinical and experimental studies" (in vitro, animal, and human). The abstract reports no number of included studies, no participant totals, and no pooled sample size.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Astragalus membranaceus and its isolated bioactive compounds, "including astragaloside IV, cycloastragenol, flavonoids, and polysaccharides," across the included studies. Being a narrative systematic review, it defines no single comparator arm.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim, no quantified pooled figures reported):** The abstract gives no effect sizes or response rates. Qualitatively, the bioactive compounds "showed photoprotective effects, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) reduction, inflammatory signaling inhibition, mitochondrial preservation, and promotion of collagen synthesis," and "elongation of telomeres under oxidative stress was also demonstrated with Astragaloside IV." For humans, it states only that "clinical trials showed improvements in skin hydration, tone, and wrinkle reduction," without numbers, sample sizes, or comparators.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** None can be extracted. The mechanistic and preclinical signals (antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, collagen, telomere) are laboratory findings, and the human claims are unquantified, so this review supports a hypothesis rather than a measured anti-aging effect in people.

**Applicability + key limitation:** The evidence is weighted toward in vitro and experimental models, not controlled human trials; the abstract reports no pooled numbers, no risk-of-bias assessment, and the authors themselves conclude "further randomized trials are needed to confirm long-term efficacy and safety." Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, so the count and quality of included human trials could not be independently verified. Treat Astragalus as a preliminary, unproven botanical for anti-aging rather than an evidence-backed active.
