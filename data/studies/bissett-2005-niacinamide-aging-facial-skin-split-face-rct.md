A 12-week, double-blind, split-face trial run by Procter & Gamble found that 5% topical niacinamide improved several signs of facial photoaging versus its vehicle, though the abstract reports the improvements as statistically significant without giving effect-size numbers.[^1]

**Design:** Double-blind, left-right randomized, vehicle-controlled split-face trial: each subject applied 5% niacinamide to one half of the face and the vehicle control to the other half, twice daily for 12 weeks, with facial images and instrumental measures taken at baseline and every 4 weeks.[^1]

**Population / n:** 50 white female subjects with clinical signs of facial photoaging (fine lines and wrinkles, poor texture, and hyperpigmented spots).[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** 5% niacinamide versus its own vehicle control, on opposite halves of the same face.[^1]

**Primary result:** The niacinamide side showed "significant skin appearance improvement effects" over vehicle, specifically "reductions in fine lines and wrinkles, hyperpigmented spots, red blotchiness, and skin sallowness (yellowing)," plus improved elasticity measured by cutometry.[^1] The abstract states the effects were significant but does not report their numeric size.

**Applicability + key limitation:** Bears on [[niacinamide]] as part of the evidence for its use on aging and pigmented skin. The limitations are substantial: all three authors were employees of The Procter & Gamble Company, which sells niacinamide products (a clear conflict of interest); the sample was 50 white women only; and because the abstract reports only that the effects were significant, not how large they were, the clinical size of the benefit cannot be judged from it. The split-face design does control for between-person differences by testing both treatments on one face.
