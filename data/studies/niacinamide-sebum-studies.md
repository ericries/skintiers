The effect of 2% niacinamide on facial sebum production is a 2006 report of two short clinical trials, one in 100 Japanese subjects and one in 30 Caucasian subjects, that tested whether a topical 2% niacinamide moisturizer lowers the amount of oil the face produces, and found a significant drop in one sebum measure in each population but not in every measure.[^1][^2]

## What It Asked

Sebum is the oily substance the skin's sebaceous glands make. The authors frame why it matters: sebum on the face is, in their words, "responsible for both facial shine and the formation of comedonal and inflammatory acne lesions."[^1] Facial shine is a cosmetic concern; the link to comedones (clogged pores) and inflammatory acne is the health-relevant one, because excess sebum is one of the drivers of acne. The authors note that most over-the-counter oil-control products only mop up oil that is already there: such "products function by absorbing sebum from the face rather than modulating its production."[^1] The trials set out to test something different, whether niacinamide could lower how much oil the glands actually put out. The stated objective was to show "the effect of topical 2% niacinamide on sebum excretion rates and casual sebum production."[^1]

Niacinamide is a form of vitamin B3 that is widely used in topical skincare; the broader evidence for it is covered on [[niacinamide]].

## What Sebum Excretion Rate and Casual Sebum Level Mean

The two trials used two different ways of measuring facial oil, and the distinction matters for reading the results.[^1]

Sebum excretion rate (SER) is a measure of how fast the glands are pushing oil onto the surface. The skin is first wiped clean of existing oil, then the fresh oil that appears over a set time is collected and measured. SER is closer to the gland's actual production rate.

Casual sebum level (CSL) is the amount of oil simply sitting on the skin surface at the moment of measurement, without first clearing it. It is a snapshot of surface oiliness, which reflects both how much the glands produce and how much has built up.

A treatment can move one of these without clearly moving the other, which is what happened here.

## Who and What They Did

The report combines two separate trials run in two countries with different designs.[^1]

The Japanese trial enrolled 100 subjects in a double-blind, placebo-controlled comparison of two separate groups. Fifty subjects applied the 2% niacinamide moisturizer to the face for 4 weeks, and 50 applied a placebo moisturizer (the same base without niacinamide) for 4 weeks. "Double-blind" means neither the subjects nor the assessors knew who got which cream. SER was measured at the start, at week 2, and at week 4.[^1]

The US trial enrolled 30 Caucasian subjects in a randomized split-face study lasting 6 weeks. In a split-face design, each person gets the active product on one side of the face and the comparison on the other, so each subject serves as their own control. Both SER and CSL were measured at the start, at week 3, and at week 6.[^1]

The abstract uses the term "Oriental" for the Japanese group; this summary uses the population actually studied.

## What It Found

In the Japanese trial, the two groups started out the same: "the SER of the two groups was not significantly different at baseline."[^1] Over the following weeks the treated group's oil-production rate fell relative to placebo. The authors report that "the 2% niacinamide treated group demonstrated significantly lowered SER after 2 and 4 weeks" of use.[^1] So in this larger, placebo-controlled group, niacinamide lowered the rate at which the glands put out oil.

In the US split-face trial the picture was mixed. After 6 weeks, "the CSL was significantly reduced, but the SER was not significantly reduced."[^1] In other words, the amount of oil sitting on the treated side of the face went down, but the measured production rate did not reach statistical significance in this smaller, shorter-population comparison.

The authors' overall conclusion reflects that split result: "Topical 2% niacinamide may be effective in lowering the SER in Japanese individuals" and casual sebum in Caucasian individuals.[^1]

The available record reports the direction of each change and whether it was statistically significant. It does not state the size of the reductions (for example, the percentage drop in sebum), which are in the full paper.[^1][^2]

## How Strong the Evidence Is

The stronger of the two trials is the Japanese one: it was double-blind, placebo-controlled, used two independent groups of 50, and confirmed the groups were matched at baseline before the treated group separated from placebo.[^1] That design controls for the effect of the moisturizer base itself, because the placebo group used the same base without niacinamide, so the difference is attributable to the niacinamide rather than to moisturizing alone.

The US trial's split-face design has a different strength, each person is their own control, and a known weakness, that a topical applied to one side of the face can spread to the other, which can blunt a real difference between sides. Its result was also weaker: only the surface-oil snapshot (CSL) reached significance, not the production rate (SER).[^1]

Both endpoints are biological measures of oil output, not clinical outcomes. The trials measured sebum, not acne. They do not report whether fewer pimples or comedones resulted, so the link to acne remains a mechanistic inference rather than a demonstrated treatment effect. Both trials were also short (4 and 6 weeks) and small (100 and 30 subjects).

## Funding

The indexed record for the article carries no funding or conflict-of-interest statement, and Europe PMC lists no associated grants.[^1][^3] The listed corresponding author, Z.D. Draelos, is affiliated with the Department of Dermatology at Wake Forest University School of Medicine.[^1][^2] Both trials evaluated a formulated 2% niacinamide moisturizer against a matched placebo moisturizer rather than a bare ingredient.[^1]

## What It Does and Does Not Show

What it shows: in a 4-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 100 Japanese subjects, a topical 2% niacinamide moisturizer significantly lowered the sebum excretion rate compared with placebo at weeks 2 and 4; and in a 6-week split-face trial of 30 Caucasian subjects, it significantly lowered casual surface sebum on the treated side.[^1]

What it does not show: it does not report the magnitude of those reductions in the abstract; it does not measure acne lesions or any clinical outcome, so it does not establish that niacinamide clears or prevents acne; and because the sebum-excretion-rate reduction reached significance in the Japanese trial but not the Caucasian one, and the two trials differed in design, duration, and population, the results cannot be read as a clean comparison between ethnic groups. The trials are short and small, and no longer-term or larger replication is reported here.[^1]
