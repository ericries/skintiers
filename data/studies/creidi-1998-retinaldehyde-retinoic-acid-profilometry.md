This is a 1998 randomized controlled trial from the Department of Functional Dermatology at University Hospital, Besançon, France, that compared 0.05% [[retinaldehyde]] cream against 0.05% retinoic acid cream and vehicle in 125 patients with photodamaged facial skin, finding both active creams significantly reduced wrinkle and roughness measurements at 18 weeks while retinaldehyde caused less irritation than retinoic acid.[^1]

## What It Asked

Retinoic acid (tretinoin) was already an established treatment for photodamaged skin, but its use is limited by irritation. Retinaldehyde is a precursor in the [[retinoids]] conversion pathway, one enzymatic step short of retinoic acid itself. The trial's stated objective was to compare "the activity and tolerance profile of a 0.05% retinaldehyde cream with a 0.05% retinoic acid cream and the retinaldehyde vehicle in patients with photodamaged skin of the face."[^1] The question was whether retinaldehyde could match retinoic acid's effect on visible photodamage while being better tolerated.

## Who and What They Did

This was a randomized, controlled, comparative clinical trial (PubMed classifies it as a Clinical Trial, Comparative Study, and Randomized Controlled Trial).[^1] The abstract accessible via PubMed does not state whether the trial was blinded or describe the randomization method in further detail.

The design measured skin texture directly rather than relying on visual grading. "A silicone replica of the left crow's feet area was taken at baseline and at weeks 18 and 44. Skin replicas were then analyzed by means of an optical profilometry technique. Standard wrinkle and roughness features were then calculated and statistically analyzed."[^1] Profilometry works from a physical mold of the skin surface, so it captures fine-scale texture change rather than a photograph or a clinician's visual score. "The tolerance profile of the test products was also clinically evaluated during the entire study."[^1]

"A total of 125 patients (40 in the retinoic acid group, 40 in the retinaldehyde group, and 45 in the vehicle group) were studied."[^1] A separate, larger group of "135 patients constituted the safety population" for tracking irritation and tolerability.[^1] The abstract does not report age range, sex distribution, or the exact statistical tests used, beyond describing the wrinkle and roughness changes as significant or not significant at each time point.

## What It Found

"At week 18, a significant reduction of the wrinkle and roughness features was observed with both retinaldehyde and retinoic acid. At week 44, a less pronounced reduction was demonstrated in both active groups."[^1] Both active creams improved the profilometric wrinkle and roughness measurements after 18 weeks, and that improvement was smaller by week 44 in both groups. "No statistically significant changes were observed with the retinaldehyde vehicle at any assessment point."[^1] The vehicle alone did not change the skin-texture measurements at week 18 or week 44.

On tolerability, the two active creams diverged: "Retinaldehyde was well tolerated during the entire study. In contrast, retinoic acid caused more local irritation, and affected compliance of the patients."[^1] The authors' conclusion: "Retinaldehyde was efficacious and well tolerated in patients with photodamage."[^1]

The abstract accessible via PubMed does not report the exact percentage change in wrinkle or roughness scores, p-values for those changes, or a head-to-head statistical comparison between the retinaldehyde and retinoic acid arms; it states only that both active arms showed a significant reduction versus their own baseline and that the vehicle did not.

> Both 0.05% retinaldehyde and 0.05% retinoic acid produced a measurable, statistically significant improvement in wrinkle and skin-roughness texture (measured from silicone skin replicas) by 18 weeks, with a smaller effect remaining at 44 weeks and no change in the vehicle group, while retinaldehyde caused less irritation and better treatment adherence than retinoic acid.[^1]

## Limitations

This write-up is based on the PubMed abstract; the full text of this 1998 Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology article was not reachable to check. The exact magnitude of the wrinkle and roughness reduction (percentages or p-values), the randomization and blinding methods, and patient demographics are therefore not confirmed here beyond what the abstract states, and are not repeated as if verified.

The endpoint is profilometric: a physical measurement of surface texture from a silicone skin replica, not a biopsy-based measure of collagen or other skin biology, and not a clinician's or patient's visual rating. It is a cosmetic-appearance surrogate rather than a health outcome. The trial is nearly three decades old, and the abstract does not state a funding source or any conflict-of-interest disclosure. This is one trial; no cited head-to-head trial compares retinaldehyde against prescription-strength tretinoin for [[anti-aging]] use outside this study.
