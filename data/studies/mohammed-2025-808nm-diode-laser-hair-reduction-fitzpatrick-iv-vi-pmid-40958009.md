A prospective record of 808 nm diode laser hair reduction run entirely in Fitzpatrick skin types IV to VI, the population whose complication risk is the whole reason the question is asked. It reports a little under 60% hair reduction after three sessions, with no control group and ten patients.[^1]

**Design:** Prospective clinical study, single-arm. Reading the whole abstract, it contains no occurrence of randomised, control or placebo, and describes no blinding. Three sessions at three-week intervals.[^1]

**Population:** "ten Sudanese women with Fitzpatrick skin types IV-VI", at Sudan University of Science and Technology, Khartoum. They are split by treatment site, "the underarm (n = 6) and chin (n = 4) areas", so each of the two results below rests on six and four people.[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** An 808 nm diode laser at "20 W, 12 mm spot size, 8-9 J/cm² fluence, 36 ms pulse width, 1 Hz frequency" with integrated sapphire cooling, against nothing. There is no comparator and no untreated site, so neither percentage has a counterfactual.[^1]

**Primary result:** After three sessions, "the mean hair reduction was 57.9% (range 45.7-66.7%) in the underarm group and 56.7% (range 45.5-70.8%) in the chin group".[^1]

**Effect size:** A mean percentage and a range, and nothing else. Checked across the whole abstract, it carries no p-value, no confidence interval and no standard deviation, and gives no baseline hair count. It also never states how hair was counted, saying only that "Efficacy was assessed by quantifying hair reduction".[^1]

**Safety, which is the reason to index it.** With ten patients the adverse events are individual cases rather than rates: one patient with skin type VI "reported pain during the first session", and one "developed a mild, superficial blister at 9 J/cm², with no further complications after fluence adjustment", the higher of the two fluences used. The authors report "No cases of keloid formation, scarring, wound infection, prolonged erythema, purpura, or pigmentary changes were observed".[^1]

**A tension inside the results paragraph.** The same paragraph that reports no pigmentary changes also reports that "skin at treated sites appeared smoother and lighter". Skin appearing lighter in Fitzpatrick IV to VI is a pigmentary observation, and it is recorded here as a cosmetic improvement rather than as an effect to track. The abstract does not say who judged either statement or by what method, so this page reports the discrepancy and does not resolve it.[^1]

**Satisfaction:** "All patients (100%) reported satisfaction with the treatment, attended all follow-up visits, and would recommend the procedure; 80% rated their results as excellent (> 6/10), and 20% as good (4-6/10)" on a 10-point Likert scale.[^1] Unblinded self-report in a single-arm study of ten, which measures acceptability rather than efficacy.

**Generalizes to:** Underarm and chin hair in women with Fitzpatrick IV to VI, treated three times at three-week intervals at the settings quoted above. It says nothing about durability: the abstract notes that patients "attended all follow-up visits" without stating when those visits were or how long after the third session anyone was assessed, and its own authors ask for "longer follow-up".[^1]

**Key limitation:** Ten patients, no control arm, and no stated method for counting hair. That combination makes it a safety case series with an efficacy number attached rather than an efficacy trial, so the two percentages should be read as what happened to these ten women, not as an expected result.

**Why it is indexed here:** [[light-based-hair-removal-deeper-skin-tones]] turns on how much evidence exists in Fitzpatrick IV to VI specifically rather than extrapolated from lighter skin, and [[laser-hair-removal]] carries the modality story. This is a primary record in that population that publishes its dose, which is what makes the single blister at 9 J/cm² usable.
