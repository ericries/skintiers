This is a 2019 randomized, double-blind trial from the University of California, Davis that compared topical [[bakuchiol]] against [[retinol]] for facial photoaging over 12 weeks in 44 patients, finding that both significantly reduced wrinkle surface area and hyperpigmentation with no statistical difference between them, while retinol users reported more scaling and stinging.[^1]

## What It Asked

Bakuchiol is a plant-derived compound sold as a "retinol alternative." The authors write that "early studies have suggested that bakuchiol is a functional analogue of topical retinoids, as both compounds have been shown to induce similar gene expression in the skin and lead to improvement of cutaneous photodamage," but note that "no in vivo studies have compared the two compounds for efficacy and side-effects."[^1] This trial was designed to fill that gap: a head-to-head test of bakuchiol against [[retinol]], the most-studied over-the-counter member of the [[retinoids]] family, in the same patients under the same conditions. The stated objective was "to compare the clinical efficacy and side-effect profiles of bakuchiol and retinol in improving common signs of cutaneous facial ageing."[^1]

## Who and What They Did

The trial was "a randomized, double-blind, 12-week study in which 44 patients were asked to apply either bakuchiol 0.5% cream twice daily or retinol 0.5% cream daily," run out of the Department of Dermatology at the University of California, Davis.[^1] The two creams were not dosed on the same schedule: bakuchiol was applied twice a day and retinol once a day.

Two kinds of measurement ran in parallel. First, "a facial photograph and analytical system was used to obtain and analyse high-resolution photographs of patients at 0, 4, 8 and 12 weeks," which is how wrinkle surface area was tracked over time.[^1] Second, "during study visits, a board-certified dermatologist, blinded to study group assignments, graded pigmentation and redness," so the clinician scoring each patient's skin did not know which cream that patient was using.[^1] Patients also "completed tolerability assessment questions to review side-effects," giving a separate, self-reported record of scaling, stinging, and other skin reactions alongside the dermatologist's grading.[^1]

## What It Found

The results, in the authors' own words: "Bakuchiol and retinol both significantly decreased wrinkle surface area and hyperpigmentation, with no statistical difference between the compounds."[^1] Both creams worked, by the photographic wrinkle measure and the dermatologist-graded pigmentation score, and neither outperformed the other on those two measures.

Tolerability was where the two creams diverged: "the retinol users reported more facial skin scaling and stinging."[^1] Wrinkle surface area and hyperpigmentation are appearance (cosmetic) endpoints rather than measures of underlying skin biology such as collagen synthesis; scaling and stinging are self-reported tolerability outcomes.

The authors' conclusion states: "Our study demonstrates that bakuchiol is comparable with retinol in its ability to improve photoageing and is better tolerated than retinol. Bakuchiol is promising as a more tolerable alternative to retinol."[^1]

> Bakuchiol matched 0.5% retinol on wrinkle and pigment improvement over 12 weeks, with no statistically significant difference between the two, and caused less scaling and stinging than retinol did.[^1]

## Limitations

This is one trial of 44 patients, run at a single center, so it is a first data point rather than a replicated finding. Twelve weeks is a short window for judging a cosmetic anti-aging effect, and the published abstract does not report whether treatment gains held up beyond that point. The two arms were not dosed on equal terms: bakuchiol was applied twice daily and retinol once daily, which complicates reading the result as a strict potency comparison between the two compounds at matched dosing.

Retinol itself sits at the milder, over-the-counter end of the [[retinoids]] family, a precursor several enzymatic steps removed from the receptor-active acid that prescription retinoids such as [[tretinoin]] deliver directly. Matching 0.5% retinol is not the same as matching prescription-strength treatment, so this trial does not show that bakuchiol is comparable to prescription retinoids, only to one over-the-counter retinol formulation. The wrinkle and pigment endpoints are photographic and dermatologist-graded appearance measures, not biopsy-based measures of collagen or other skin biology.
