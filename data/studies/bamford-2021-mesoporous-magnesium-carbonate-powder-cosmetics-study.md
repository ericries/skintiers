An industry-affiliated materials/human-panel study found that mesoporous magnesium carbonate (MMC, trade name Upsalite) outperformed most benchmark powder fillers on oil absorption, mattifying duration, and flashback avoidance, with no irritation seen in a 50-person patch test.[^1]

**Design:** A parallel set of in vitro/instrumental characterization tests and human volunteer panels benchmarking MMC powder against silica, mica, kaolin, talc, and/or corn starch on oil and moisture uptake, skin-mattifying effect, photographic flashback, in vitro skin-irritation potential (reconstructed human epidermis, OECD 439), and dermatological safety (Human Repeated Insult Patch Test).[^1]

**Population / n:** Three separate human panels, not one cohort: 14 volunteers with normal-to-oily skin (mattifying-effect test), 8 volunteers (flashback-photography test), and 50 volunteers (HRIPT dermatological safety test). The material-characterization and in vitro irritation portions used no human subjects.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "MMC absorbed more than 110% of its weight, which is slightly more than silica." BET specific surface area and pore volume were "300 m² g⁻¹" and "0.5 cm³ g⁻¹" respectively. In dual-loading tests, "approximately 80% of the moisture uptake capacity was maintained in material pre-loaded with 0.5 g g⁻¹ oil," and "approximately 90% of its original oil absorption capacity was maintained...when pre-loaded with 30 wt% water." "MMC provides an instant mattifying effect...outperforming all other materials tested except kaolin" and "exhibited a long-lasting mattifying effect of at least 8 h." "Neither MMC, mica nor talc induced any flashback. However...silica exhibits a clearly measurable flashback effect." In the in vitro irritation model, "the relative viability of reconstructed human epidermal model exposed to MMC was 85.2 ± 3.5%, which is well above the acceptance criteria of >50%." In the 50-person HRIPT, "MMC neither induced any erythema, dryness, oedema nor any other signs of irritation. None of the volunteers presented any allergic reaction."[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** The source states that "a two-sided t-test using Excel software was performed to statistically evaluate the null hypothesis between the means at a significance level of 0.95 (*p < 0.05 as compared to control)"; however, the specific performance figures quoted above are reported as descriptive point values, without per-comparison test statistics or effect sizes given alongside them. Authors conclude MMC "has good sensory and visual characteristics as well as excellent absorbing and mattifying properties, suggesting that it has great potential to replace or compliment [sic] other powder ingredients currently used as fillers and absorbers in powder cosmetics."[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** This is a cosmetic raw-material performance and safety study, not a trial of a marketed product or a clinical skin-disease outcome; it was conducted at a single lab under fixed conditions (20°C/76% RH), used small-to-moderate volunteer panels (8 to 50 people) with short observation windows, and the flashback-photography method described is novel and not an established standard. It bears on formulating [[oily-skin]] mattifying powders and on how MMC compares to the established filler [[silica]], covered on those pages.[^1]
