A systematic review of 77 epidemiological studies found that most studies report harmful associations between air pollution exposure and several skin diseases, but rated the overall certainty of that evidence as low to very low due to high risk of bias.[^1]

**Design:** Systematic review of epidemiological studies. The authors "systematically searched two comprehensive databases, SCOPUS and PubMed, from 1 January, 1990 to 30 April, 2025."[^1]

**Population / n:** After "screening 1393 eligible studies, 77 studies were selected" covering seven skin conditions: atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, urticaria, acne, melanoma skin cancer, non-melanoma skin cancer, and skin aging. The abstract states "studies were mostly from the upper-middle and higher income countries."[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Exposure to ambient air pollutants (particulate matter PM₁₀ and PM₂.₅, and gaseous pollutants: nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, ozone, carbon monoxide) versus lower/comparator exposure levels, as reported across the 77 included epidemiological studies.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "The majority of studies strongly advocated the harmful effects of air pollution" on the seven skin conditions, though results were heterogeneous. Risk-of-bias assessment found high risk "in the domains of confounding, selection bias, and exposure assessment." "Consequently, certainty in evidence or causal inference was usually low to very low." "In most studies, high air pollution had a more immediate effect (same day) and lasted up to a week after exposure." The abstract states no pooled odds ratios, relative risks, or p-values for any of the seven conditions.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** No quantitative pooled effect size (OR, RR, or %) is given in the abstract for any condition; this is a qualitative/vote-counting synthesis across heterogeneous studies, not a meta-analysis. The authors' overall conclusion is that "despite numerous epidemiological studies on air pollution and skin diseases, the overall quality of evidence is low," and they call for more longitudinal studies, improved exposure assessment, and research in low- and middle-income countries.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Findings are drawn predominantly from upper-middle- and higher-income-country populations, so applicability to low- and middle-income countries and to vulnerable subpopulations is explicitly uncertain per the authors. The key limitation is the high risk of bias across the underlying studies (confounding, selection, exposure assessment) that the authors say drives the low-to-very-low certainty rating; only the PubMed abstract was accessed (a PMC full-text link exists but was not fetched), so no further numeric detail from the full text can be verified here. It bears on the epidemiological air-pollution evidence for conditions covered on [[acne]] and [[atopic-dermatitis]], among others, without establishing quantified risk for either.[^1]
