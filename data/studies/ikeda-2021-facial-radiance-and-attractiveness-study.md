An experimental psychophysical study found that facial images rendered with radiant skin reflection were rated more attractive and more positively across several affective impressions than the same faces rendered oily/shiny or matte, by 160 female raters.[^1]

**Design:** Experimental study in which participants rated manipulated facial images (with skin reflection altered across conditions) spanning seven skin-reflection conditions (three reflection types: radiant, oily and shiny, matte; crossed with three facial positions: entire face, cheeks only, T-zone only) on perceived age and on attractiveness/other affective impressions.[^1]

**Population / n:** 160 female participants rated images of unfamiliar female faces; the abstract does not state the raters' age range or the number of distinct face stimuli/models used.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Radiant vs. oily-and-shiny vs. matte skin-reflection renderings, applied to the entire face, cheeks only, or T-zone only (seven conditions total), compared against one another for perceived age and attractiveness/impression ratings.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "The facial images of radiance on entire faces were rated as appearing younger than the facial images of oily shine on entire faces and the matte faces." "Attractiveness ratings and other positive impressions increased in the order of the matte (ranked lowest), the oily shine on entire face, and the radiance on entire face (ranked highest) conditions." "...attractiveness ratings and other positive impressions were higher in the radiance on entire face condition than in the radiant cheeks and the radiant T-zone conditions." "Interestingly, the radiant cheek faces were rated more radiant and healthier but less feminine and less bright than the radiant T-zone faces."[^1] The abstract reports no numeric statistics (no means, SDs, F- or p-values) for any of these comparisons.

**Effect size / interpretation:** The abstract gives only a rank ordering (matte lowest, oily shine middle, full-face radiance highest) and qualitative comparisons (higher/lower, more/less radiant, healthier, feminine, bright); it does not quantify how large the radiance effect is relative to the oily-shine or matte conditions, so effect size cannot be assessed from the abstract alone.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Findings come from manipulated images of unfamiliar female faces judged only by female raters, so applicability to male faces, mixed-sex or male rater pools, or radiance achieved on real skin rather than in altered images is not established. Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full *International Journal of Cosmetic Science* text, so participant demographics, stimulus construction, and statistical figures beyond the qualitative rank-ordering above cannot be verified. It sits alongside the objective, instrument-measured account of skin glow in [[roessle-kerscher-2025-skin-glow-objectification-300-women-study]], covered there.[^1]
