A controlled human trial found that a high-SPF sunscreen blocked the local immunosuppressive effect of UVB exposure on contact hypersensitivity, supporting the mechanistic case that [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] guard against UV-induced immune suppression in addition to erythema and photoaging.[^1]

**Design:** Controlled clinical trial with three groups: UVB-only, sunscreen-plus-UVB, and unirradiated control, using contact hypersensitivity to dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB) as the outcome.[^1]

**Population / n:** 29 total subjects (19 across the two irradiated groups, 10 controls).[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Three minimal erythema doses of UVB daily for 3 consecutive days, either alone or with an SPF 29 sunscreen (containing octyl methoxycinnamate, oxybenzone, and octyl salicylate) applied before each UVB exposure, versus a no-UVB control group. Contact hypersensitivity was assessed by forearm challenge with DNCB at four concentrations (3.125, 6.25, 8.8, and 12.5 micrograms); 12.5 micrograms was the one dose at which the UVB-only group showed no significant suppression.[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** The UVB-only group showed a "reduced response rate to all challenge doses of DNCB (3.125, 6.25, and 8.8 micrograms), except for the highest dose (12.5 micrograms)" compared with controls (P ≤ .008), and the UVB-only group differed from the sunscreen-plus-UVB group at P ≤ .02. The sunscreen-plus-UVB group showed "no significant differences in response rates" versus controls (P ≥ .53). The abstract as retrieved does not state the exact response-rate percentages for each group, only the P-value thresholds.[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** UVB exposure alone significantly suppressed the DNCB contact hypersensitivity response at the three lower challenge doses (3.125, 6.25, and 8.8 micrograms) but not at the highest dose tested (12.5 micrograms); pre-treating with SPF 29 sunscreen abolished the suppression seen at the lower doses, with response rates statistically indistinguishable from unexposed controls. This is read as evidence that adequate sunscreen use prevents rather than merely reduces local UV-induced immunosuppression at the doses where UVB alone caused suppression.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Findings apply to acute, high-dose UVB exposure and a local (not systemic) contact-hypersensitivity endpoint in a small sample (n=29); only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full Arch Dermatol text, so exact response-rate percentages and any subgroup or dose-response detail beyond the stated P-values cannot be verified here.
