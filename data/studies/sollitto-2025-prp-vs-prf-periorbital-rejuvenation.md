A systematic review comparing two injectable, in-office autologous platelet treatments, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), for rejuvenating the area around the eyes found that neither is clearly superior and that PRF's improvements often faded by six months.[^1]

**Design:** PRISMA-guided systematic review (searched PubMed, Embase, and Wiley Library) comparing injectable PRP and PRF for periorbital rejuvenation on preparation, injection technique, treatment frequency, clinical outcomes, adverse events, and satisfaction. Not a meta-analysis: results are summarized narratively, not pooled.[^1]

**Population:** 14 studies met inclusion criteria; the review does not report a single pooled patient count, and the included studies were heterogeneous in method.[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** Injectable PRP versus injectable PRF, both autologous (prepared from the patient's own blood). These are in-office injectable procedures, not topical or over-the-counter skincare.[^1]

**Primary result:** "PRF was associated with improvements in skin texture, wrinkles, and crepiness, while PRP showed stronger evidence for treating hyperpigmentation. Both modalities demonstrated favorable safety profiles and high patient satisfaction, with only mild, transient adverse effects reported."[^1] The authors conclude that "current evidence does not support the superiority of one modality over the other."[^1]

**Effect size:** Not quantified. As a narrative systematic review of heterogeneous studies with no standardized outcome measures, it reports directions of benefit (PRF for texture and fine lines, PRP for pigmentation) rather than pooled effect sizes.[^1]

**Generalizes to:** Adults considering in-office injectable autologous platelet procedures for the periorbital area. It says nothing about topical skincare for under-eye concerns; the general evidence for at-home approaches to pigmentation and under-eye appearance is on [[hyperpigmentation]] and [[dark-circles]].[^1]

**Key limitation:** Durability is uncertain and short: "PRF improvements often diminished by 6 months," and the review rests on 14 heterogeneous studies without standardized, objective outcome measures, so the authors call for "larger randomized controlled trials with standardized, objective outcome measures."[^1]
