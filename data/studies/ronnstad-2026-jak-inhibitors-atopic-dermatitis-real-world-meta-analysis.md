A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled real-world (observational) studies of the three oral Janus kinase inhibitors used for moderate-to-severe [[atopic-dermatitis]], abrocitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib, and found their day-to-day effectiveness and safety broadly matched what the clinical trials showed at the highest doses.[^1]

**Design:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of observational (real-world) studies, not a randomized trial; PubMed and EMBASE were searched from inception to 3 January 2025. Because it pools separate single-drug cohorts rather than randomizing patients between drugs, any cross-drug comparison here is indirect.[^1]

**Population:** 63 studies covering 517 patients treated with abrocitinib, 574 with baricitinib, and 2779 with upadacitinib for atopic dermatitis. Most studies reported outcomes with doses combined rather than separated.[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** Oral abrocitinib, baricitinib, or upadacitinib; the "comparator" is the drugs' own randomized-trial performance, which the review checks the real-world numbers against rather than a head-to-head arm.[^1]

**Primary result:** The primary outcome was the proportion reaching EASI-75 (a 75% improvement in the Eczema Area and Severity Index). Across all doses at 16 weeks, EASI-75 and EASI-90 rates were 75% and 38% for abrocitinib, 51% and 24% for baricitinib, and 83% and 55% for upadacitinib.[^1]

**Effect size:** Upadacitinib showed the highest clearance rates and baricitinib the lowest, with abrocitinib in between; the authors conclude these real-world results are similar to what the clinical trials showed at the highest treatment doses. Acne and herpes simplex virus (HSV) were the most frequently reported adverse events (acne 21% / HSV 2% for abrocitinib, 8% / 6% for baricitinib, 15% / 6% for upadacitinib), highest with 100 mg abrocitinib for acne and 30 mg upadacitinib for HSV; few serious adverse events were reported.[^1]

**Generalizes to:** Adults with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis managed by a dermatologist on a systemic oral JAK inhibitor. These are prescription systemic immunomodulators, not topical skincare; the site's role for atopic dermatitis is barrier repair and trigger avoidance (see [[atopic-dermatitis]]), with systemic drugs like these physician-managed.

**Key limitation:** The evidence is pooled observational data, not randomized, and many studies did not report the dose used, which the authors say complicated interpretation; the apparent ranking between the three drugs is therefore indirect, not a randomized head-to-head. Acne and HSV were the most common reasons for stopping treatment. An erratum to this article was published in July 2026.[^1]
