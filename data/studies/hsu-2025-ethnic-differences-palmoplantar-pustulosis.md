A systematic review comparing palmoplantar pustulosis across ethnicities on epidemiology, genetics, clinical features and treatment response. Related to but distinct from [[psoriasis]]; the site has no palmoplantar pustulosis hub yet.[^study]

**Design:** PRISMA 2020 systematic review with a registered protocol (INPLASY202530108), four databases searched to 1 February 2025, two independent assessors, and "A narrative synthesis was performed owing to heterogeneity".[^study] Narrative rather than meta-analytic, so there is no pooled estimate anywhere in it.

**Population / n:** "Of 2250 studies screened, 101 studies were included."[^study] Of those, 46 studies covering 216,257 patients supplied the clinical characteristics, with 13 cohort studies on epidemiology and 14 on treatment type and response.[^study] The 46 were "42 cohort studies, 1 case-control, and 3 case series", so observational throughout.[^study]

**Intervention / comparator:** None. The comparison is between ethnic groups across separate cohorts in different countries, not between treatments and not within a randomised design.

**Primary results (verbatim):** "Incidence and prevalence rates of PPP were higher among East Asians, especially in Japanese populations."[^study] On sex, "While PPP is a female-predominant disease worldwide, more men are affected in Asia, likely owing to a lower prevalence of smoking among Asian females".[^study] Genetics: "Mutations in IL36RN, CARD14, and different HLA alleles contribute to the genetic landscape of PPP across ethnicities."[^study] Asian patients had "less concurrent psoriasis vulgaris and psoriatic arthritis, as well as lower rates of smoking, obesity, celiac disease, and thyroid disease, while metal allergies were more common", with nail involvement and SAPHO syndrome more common in Asians and plantar-only or unilateral involvement more frequent in non-Asians.[^study] On treatment, "Among conventional treatment modalities, acitretin, colchicine, and tonsillectomy seemed more effective for Asians. Biologics were less commonly used in Asia, with ethnic-related differences in treatment response noted across classes."[^study]

**Effect size:** Not reportable. The review publishes no pooled effect estimate, no confidence interval and no p-value in its abstract, and the word the authors themselves choose for the treatment comparison is "seemed".[^study]

**Who it generalises to:** Clinicians and researchers working on palmoplantar pustulosis. It does not generalise to any product or routine decision, and nothing in it concerns cosmetic skincare.

**The one key limitation:** The treatment-response signal is confounded by design and cannot be read as an efficacy difference. Comparisons run across separate cohorts in different countries and health systems, so "acitretin, colchicine, and tonsillectomy seemed more effective for Asians" is entangled with which treatments those systems reach for first, and "Biologics were less commonly used in Asia" is a prescribing and access observation rather than a finding about how well biologics work.[^study] The authors state the constraint plainly, that the review is "limited by study size and heterogeneity".[^study] Treat the whole treatment section as hypothesis-generating. Tonsillectomy in particular is a procedure, not a drug, and its appearance here is not evidence that it should be offered.
