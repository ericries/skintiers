This is a 2019 systematic review from QIMR Berghofer Medical Research Institute that pooled four experimental studies, three field trials, and 69 observational studies on sunscreen and vitamin D, concluding that "there is little evidence that sunscreen decreases 25(OH)D concentration when used in real-life settings" while also flagging that no trial has tested the high-SPF sunscreens now widely recommended.[^1]

## What It Asked

Sunscreen is one of the best-evidenced steps for lowering skin-cancer risk (see [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]), but the same UV exposure it blocks is also how skin manufactures vitamin D3, and that has fed a common worry that regular sunscreen use could push people toward vitamin D deficiency. The review states the concern plainly: "Sunscreen use can prevent skin cancer, but there are concerns that it may increase the risk of vitamin D deficiency."[^1] The authors set out "to review the literature to investigate associations between sunscreen use and vitamin D3 or 25 hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentration," the standard blood marker of vitamin D status.[^1]

## Who and What They Did

This was a systematic review, not a new trial. The authors "systematically reviewed the literature following the Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines," searching for manuscripts "published in English between 1970 and 21 November 2017."[^1] They accepted three kinds of study: experimental studies that exposed skin to an artificial UV source, field trials of real-world sunscreen use, and observational studies that measured associations between self-reported sunscreen use and vitamin D status. "The results of each of the experimental studies and field trials are described in detail," and for the much larger body of observational studies, "two authors extracted information from observational studies, and applied quality scoring criteria that were developed specifically for this question," synthesizing those qualitatively rather than pooling them statistically.[^1]

The pool: "four experimental studies, three field trials (two were randomized controlled trials) and 69 observational studies."[^1] PubMed's record lists funding from the National Health and Medical Research Council.[^1]

## What It Found

The three study types pointed in different directions, and the review reports each separately rather than blending them into one number.

**Experimental studies (artificial UV, lab conditions).** Here the theoretical concern held up: "In the experimental studies sunscreen use considerably abrogated the vitamin D3 or 25(OH)D production induced by exposure to artificially generated UVR."[^1] Blocking UV with sunscreen blocked vitamin D synthesis when the UV source and dose were tightly controlled.

**Field trials (real-world sunscreen use, including two RCTs).** The result flipped: "The randomized controlled field trials found no effect of daily sunscreen application, but the sunscreens used had moderate protection [sun protection factor (SPF) ~16]."[^1] People who used a moderate-SPF sunscreen daily under real conditions did not end up with lower vitamin D than those who did not.

**Observational studies (69 studies of self-reported use).** The pattern held here too, and if anything ran the opposite way from the original worry: "The observational studies mostly found no association or that self-reported sunscreen use was associated with higher 25(OH)D concentration."[^1] People who reported using sunscreen were, if anything, somewhat more likely to have higher vitamin D levels than those who did not, not lower.

The authors' own framing of why the three designs diverge, from the paper's "What does this study add?" summary: "While the experimental studies support the theoretical risk that sunscreen use may affect vitamin D, the weight of evidence from field trials and observational studies suggests that the risk is low."[^1]

The review's conclusion: "There is little evidence that sunscreen decreases 25(OH)D concentration when used in real-life settings, suggesting that concerns about vitamin D should not negate skin cancer prevention advice."[^1]

> Sunscreen blocked vitamin D production under controlled lab UV exposure, but real-world field trials and 69 observational studies mostly found no drop in vitamin D among sunscreen users, and some found sunscreen users had higher vitamin D, so the review concludes vitamin D concerns should not be a reason to skip sunscreen's skin-cancer protection.[^1]

## Limitations

The authors name the review's central gap themselves: "There have been no trials of the high-SPF sunscreens that are now widely recommended."[^1] Both randomized field trials behind the "no effect" finding used sunscreens with SPF around 16, not the very high-SPF products now widely recommended (see [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]).[^1] The review's own "What does this study add?" section repeats the point: "We highlight the lack of adequate evidence regarding use of the very high sun protection factor sunscreens that are now recommended and widely used."[^1] Whether a modern high-SPF product, applied consistently, would behave like the moderate-SPF sunscreens in these trials is not something this evidence base can answer.

The 69 observational studies were synthesized qualitatively with a study-specific quality-scoring tool rather than pooled into a single meta-analytic estimate, and they rest on self-reported sunscreen use, which is a less precise exposure measure than a trial-assigned protocol. The literature search ran through November 2017, so more recent studies are not included. And the gap between the experimental and field-trial/observational results shows that a tightly controlled artificial-UV exposure is not equivalent to how people actually apply sunscreen and spend time outdoors.
