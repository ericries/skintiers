A systematic review of every published treatment for underarm hyperpigmentation, topical and device-based. The site's pigment coverage generally is on [[hyperpigmentation]]; what is specific here is a body site with, in the authors' words, "no standard treatment".[^study]

**Design:** Systematic review, narrative. Four databases searched, "PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase", using axillary and hyperpigmentation keywords and MeSH terms.[^study] The abstract names no PRISMA adherence, no protocol registration, no search end date, no screening or extraction procedure and no quality or risk-of-bias assessment, all checked by string search.[^study] There is no meta-analysis and no pooled estimate.

**Population / n:** Not reportable at the review level. "Ten studies on AH were categorized into topical treatments, light/laser-based therapies, and comparative approaches."[^study] The abstract contains no participant total, no per-study sample size, no skin-type distribution and no follow-up duration. It contains no numeral of any kind: the count of included studies is the spelled-out ten, and that is the only quantity in it.

**Intervention / comparator:** Topical agents listed as "niacinamide, desonide, sweet orange extract, Perilla frutescens leaf extract, Cyperus rotundus oil, glycolic acid, and novel serums", plus Q-switched Nd:YAG laser and intense pulsed light.[^study] Comparators vary by included study and are not standardised across the review. One study of Curcuma aeruginosa was excluded "due to its primary focus on hair growth reduction rather than pigmentation".[^study]

**Primary results (verbatim):** The topicals "demonstrated varying degrees of pigmentation reduction, with desonide and C. rotundus oil showing significant effectiveness and minimal side effects".[^study] On devices, "Q-switched Nd:YAG laser was effective across three studies", and both it and IPL "showed significant improvement with minimal adverse events; though pain was more reported with the laser".[^study] Head to head, "A comparative study found IPL superior to alpha-hydroxy acid in skin lightening and texture improvement."[^study]

**Effect size:** None exists to report. No percentage change, colorimetric delta, confidence interval or p-value appears anywhere in the abstract, so every claim above is a direction without a magnitude.[^study] The phrase "significant effectiveness" is the authors' summary wording, not a statistic.

**Who it generalises to:** People seeking treatment for darkened underarm skin, and clinicians choosing between a topical and a device for it. It does not generalise to facial pigment: nothing here was tested on the face, and the axilla differs in occlusion, friction, hair removal and sweat exposure. Two of the named topicals have their own pages, [[niacinamide]] and [[glycolic-acid]], and desonide is a prescription topical corticosteroid rather than a cosmetic.

**The one key limitation:** The conclusion credits treatments the results paragraph does not support. It states that "Various topical treatments including Niacinamide, desonide, HQ, AHA, and a few herbal extracts were shown to be effective for AH", but hydroquinone is never named in the results paragraph nor anywhere else in the abstract except that sentence, and alpha-hydroxy acid appears in the results only as the comparator that IPL beat.[^study] Being outperformed is not the same as being shown effective. Read the results paragraph, not the conclusion.

**A second caution, from this site's own reading of a primary source.** The review passes through "C. rotundus oil showing significant effectiveness" without qualification.[^study] The underlying trial is covered in detail on [[cyperus-rotundus]], where the abstract states in consecutive sentences both that the oil significantly beat hydroquinone and that the two did not differ significantly, and where all four human trials of the plant share one author group with no independent replication. A narrative review with no risk-of-bias assessment has no mechanism for catching that.
