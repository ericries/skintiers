A randomized, double-blind, controlled trial found that 12 weeks of topical 10% [[vitamin-c]] serum significantly reduced skin glycation, carbonylation, and inflammation markers and improved skin color versus a blank-group control in healthy Chinese women.[^1]

**Design:** Randomized, double-blind, controlled trial measuring skin glycation, carbonylated protein content, interleukin-1α (IL-1α) levels, free radical scavenging capacity, and skin color parameters before and after treatment.[^1]

**Population / n:** 66 healthy Chinese females; 31 in the blank (control) group, 35 in the topical treatment group.[^1]

**Intervention / comparator:** Topical 10% vitamin C serum applied for 12 weeks, versus a blank-group control (the abstract does not specify whether the blank group received a vehicle/placebo or no product).[^1]

**Primary result (verbatim figures):** "After 12 weeks, the topical group showed significant improvements: AGEs reduced by 17.65%, carbonylation fluorescence intensity decreased by 49.22%, IL-1α content dropped by 58.73%, and ABTS free radical scavenging rate increased by 12.14%. Skin yellowness (b* value) and redness (a* value) decreased by 6.13% and 16.46%, respectively (all p < 0.001)."[^1]

**Effect size / interpretation:** All six reported measures moved in the beneficial direction with p < 0.001; the largest relative change was in carbonylation fluorescence intensity (-49.22%) and IL-1α (-58.73%), with smaller relative changes in AGEs (-17.65%), ABTS free radical scavenging (+12.14%), b* yellowness (-6.13%), and a* redness (-16.46%). The abstract does not report between-group statistical comparisons (e.g., treatment vs. blank group p-values) or absolute baseline values, only within-group percentage changes for the treatment group.[^1]

**Applicability + key limitation:** Applies to healthy Chinese adult women using a 10% vitamin C serum for 12 weeks; generalizability to other skin tones, ages, or vitamin C concentrations/formulations is not established. Only the PubMed abstract was accessed, not the full text in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology, so methodological details (randomization method, blinding procedure, blank-group composition, statistical tests used) cannot be verified beyond what the abstract states. This supports the general case for [[vitamin-c]] in [[anti-aging]] and [[hyperpigmentation]]/skin-brightening use, covered on those pages.
