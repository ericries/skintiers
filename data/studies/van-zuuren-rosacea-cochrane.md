Interventions for rosacea is a pair of systematic reviews led by Esther J. van Zuuren, a 2015 Cochrane review of 106 randomised controlled trials (13,631 participants) and a 2019 update covering 152 trials (20,944 participants), that assessed which rosacea treatments work and how certain the evidence is.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

Rosacea is a long-term facial skin condition with flushing, lasting redness, visible small blood vessels, and acne-like bumps and pustules; the eyes can be affected, and in some people the skin of the nose thickens.[^1] Many creams, tablets, and light-based treatments are marketed for it, and the practical question these reviews set out to answer is which of them actually help, and how confident anyone can be in that answer.

## What They Asked

Both reviews asked the same core question: how well do rosacea treatments work, and how safe are they.[^1][^3] The 2015 Cochrane review, an update of an earlier version, stated its aim as: "To assess the efficacy and safety of treatments for rosacea."[^1] The 2019 version, published in the British Journal of Dermatology, reorganised the same evidence around the way rosacea is now described. As its authors put it: "Classification of rosacea has evolved from subtyping to phenotyping."[^3] In plain terms, doctors moved away from sorting patients into fixed subtypes and toward matching treatment to the specific features a person actually has, such as persistent redness, bumps and pustules, or eye involvement. The 2019 update grouped its findings that way.[^3]

## Who Was In Them and What They Did

Both are systematic reviews, meaning the authors searched for every relevant randomised controlled trial, judged how well each was run, and combined the results where the trials were similar enough to pool. A randomised controlled trial assigns participants to a treatment or a comparison (a placebo, an inactive vehicle cream, or another drug) by chance, which is the design least prone to bias.

The 2015 review searched databases up to July 2014 and included 106 trials with 13,631 participants.[^1] More women than men took part, the mean age was 48.6 years, and most participants had papulopustular rosacea (the bumps-and-pustules pattern), followed by erythematotelangiectatic rosacea (redness with visible vessels).[^1] Trials of 30 to 100 people lasting two to three months were the most common, and 67 different treatment comparisons were evaluated.[^1] The authors judged each trial's reliability independently, in duplicate.[^1]

The 2019 update repeated the searches to March 2018 and grew the evidence base to 152 trials (46 of them new) with 20,944 participants.[^3] It covered topical treatments (brimonidine, oxymetazoline, metronidazole, azelaic acid, ivermectin, and others), oral treatments (antibiotics and isotretinoin), and laser or light-based therapy, presenting the results by rosacea feature rather than by subtype.[^3]

## What They Found, With the Numbers Explained

The findings are reported as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A risk ratio compares the chance of improvement on the treatment with the chance on the comparison: an RR of 1.50 means people on the treatment were about 50% more likely to improve. A confidence interval is the range the true effect is likely to fall in; when that range stays above 1.0, the benefit is statistically clear. The strongest results below rested on trials the authors rated high certainty.

**Bumps and pustules (papulopustular rosacea).** Topical metronidazole and azelaic acid were the two treatments with enough data to pool in 2015. As the review put it: "Both were shown to be more effective than placebo in papulopustular rosacea."[^1] Pooled physician assessments favoured metronidazole over placebo (RR 1.98, 95% CI 1.29 to 3.02; three trials), and participants' own assessments favoured azelaic acid over placebo (RR 1.46, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.63; four trials).[^1] Topical ivermectin beat placebo in two trials on participants' assessments (RR 1.78, 95% CI 1.50 to 2.11; and RR 1.92, 95% CI 1.59 to 2.32), and one trial suggested it was slightly better than metronidazole.[^1] The 2019 update reaffirmed this group, reporting "high-certainty evidence for topical azelaic acid and topical ivermectin" for reducing papules and pustules, moderate certainty for metronidazole, and moderate certainty that topical and oral minocycline were about as effective as low-dose modified-release doxycycline.[^3]

**Oral treatments for bumps and pustules.** In 2015, physician assessments in two trials favoured doxycycline over placebo (RR 1.59, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.47; and RR 2.37, 95% CI 1.12 to 4.99).[^1] A 40 mg dose worked about as well as 100 mg but caused fewer side effects (RR for adverse events 0.25, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.54, favouring the lower dose).[^1] Low-dose isotretinoin was rated slightly more effective than doxycycline 50 to 100 mg by both participants (RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.43) and physicians (RR 1.18, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.36).[^1] Oral tetracycline beat placebo on moderate-certainty evidence, though the authors noted this rested on two old, short trials.[^1]

**Persistent facial redness.** Topical brimonidine, a gel that temporarily narrows facial blood vessels, reduced redness more than its vehicle at every time point over 12 hours. At three hours, participants' assessments favoured brimonidine (RR 2.21, 95% CI 1.52 to 3.22; and RR 2.00, 95% CI 1.33 to 3.01), and physician assessments agreed.[^1] The review added a reassurance on stopping it: "There was no rebound or worsening of erythema after treatment cessation."[^1] The 2019 update added topical oxymetazoline, another redness-reducing cream: a two-grade improvement three hours after application occurred in 99 of 446 people on oxymetazoline 1% versus 59 of 439 on vehicle (RR 1.65, 95% CI 1.23 to 2.21, P < 0.001), meaning roughly 11 people had to be treated for one extra person to reach that improvement (number needed to treat 11, 95% CI 7 to 27; moderate certainty).[^3]

**Eye involvement (ocular rosacea).** Evidence here was thinner. The 2015 review found low-certainty support for ciclosporin ophthalmic emulsion.[^1] The 2019 update reported "moderate-certainty evidence that oral omega-3 fatty acids were effective" for symptoms of ocular rosacea.[^3] That rested on a single 130-person trial of a fish-oil capsule twice daily, where a dry-eye symptom score fell by 5.30 points (standard deviation 1.52) in the 65 people on omega-3 versus 0.20 points (SD 1.59) in the 65 on placebo (mean difference -5.10, 95% CI -5.63 to -4.57, P < 0.001).[^3] Both reviews flagged this area as under-studied; the 2015 conclusion stated plainly: "Further studies on treatment of ocular rosacea are warranted."[^1]

**Laser and light.** In 2015, pulsed dye laser was more effective than Nd:YAG laser in one trial and appeared about as effective as intense pulsed light, both on low-certainty evidence.[^1] The 2019 update rated laser and intense pulsed light for redness and visible vessels as low-to-moderate certainty.[^3]

## How Strong the Evidence Is

Both reviews used GRADE, a formal system that rates how much confidence to place in each result as high, moderate, low, or very low, based on the trials' design, consistency, precision, and risk of bias. A high rating means further research is unlikely to change the estimate; a low or very low rating means the true effect could be quite different.

The 2015 review summarised its strongest results: high-certainty evidence backed topical azelaic acid, topical ivermectin, brimonidine, doxycycline, and isotretinoin; moderate certainty backed topical metronidazole and oral tetracycline; and low certainty covered low-dose minocycline, laser and intense pulsed light, and ciclosporin for the eyes.[^1] The 2019 update kept the same top tier for bumps and pustules (azelaic acid and ivermectin at high certainty) and for persistent redness (brimonidine at high certainty, oxymetazoline at moderate), while much of the ocular and light-based evidence stayed at moderate or below.[^3] In short, the creams and tablets for bumps, pustules, and redness are the best-supported treatments, and the eye and device treatments are less certain.

## Who Funded Them and Conflicts of Interest

The 2015 Cochrane review reported no internal funding and external support from the Acne and Rosacea Society of Canada and the UK National Institute for Health Research, which the review names as the largest single funder of the Cochrane Skin Group.[^2] Its authors declared no conflicts of interest; a clinical referee, Joel Bamford, disclosed that he had authored several of the included and referenced trials.[^1][^2]

The 2019 update was funded by the Dutch Society for Dermatology and Venereology and reported author conflicts of interest.[^4] The lead author served on the global ROSacea COnsensus panel and had received nonfinancial and other support from Galderma in October 2016; a co-author disclosed advisory, consultancy, investigator, or speaker roles with Allergan, Bayer, Cipher, Galderma, and Valeant; and another co-author reported support from Galderma and speaker fees from Janssen Cilag and AbbVie.[^4] Several of the drugs assessed, including brimonidine, ivermectin, and azelaic acid formulations, are made by companies named in those disclosures, which is why the funding and independence of the underlying trials matter to how the results are read.

## Limitations

The reviews are only as reliable as the trials they pool, and the authors were direct about the weaknesses. In 2015, most included trials were rated as unclear risk of bias (57 of 106), with 37 at high risk and only 12 at low risk; 22 studies provided no usable data.[^1] Only 11 trials measured the review's primary outcome, change in quality of life, and time to response and how long remission lasted were often incompletely reported or not reported at all.[^1] Sample sizes were mostly small (30 to 100) and trials were short (two to three months), so long-term effects and rare harms are not well captured.[^1] Some head-to-head questions rested on a single trial, and for a few comparisons the evidence was low or very low certainty.[^1] The 2019 update, while larger, drew on the same body of frequently small and short trials.[^3]

## What They Do and Do Not Show

Taken together, the two reviews show that several rosacea treatments are well supported by high-certainty evidence for specific features: topical azelaic acid and ivermectin for bumps and pustules, brimonidine for temporary redness, and oral doxycycline and isotretinoin for papulopustular disease.[^1][^3] They do not show that any one of these is the single best choice for every person, that any treatment cures rosacea rather than controlling it, or that the sparse ocular and laser evidence is settled.[^1][^3] The results describe how treatments perform in trials over two to three months, not lifelong outcomes, and the certainty ratings, not the marketing around any product, are what carry the weight.
