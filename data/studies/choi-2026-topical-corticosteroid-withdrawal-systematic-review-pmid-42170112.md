The review behind the numbers people quote about topical steroid withdrawal. The condition itself is at [[topical-steroid-withdrawal]] and the drugs at [[topical-corticosteroids]]; neither is retold here.[^1]

**Design:** a systematic review searching "Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, and the Cochrane Library from October 2020 to June 2024", updating two earlier reviews.[^1] No protocol registration, risk-of-bias tool or meta-analysis is named in the abstract.

**Population:** "Seven studies were included in the analysis, including five case reports, one case series, and one qualitative cross-sectional survey".[^1] The authors describe this themselves as "reflecting the limited and descriptive nature of the currently available evidence on TCSW".[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** none. This is a descriptive synthesis of reported cases, not a comparison of treatments, and there is no control group anywhere in it.

**Primary results:** a symptom and demographic profile, reported as percentages. TCSW "appears to predominantly occur in adult women (90%) using TCS for atopic dermatitis (99.5%), mostly on the face (90%)".[^1] The symptoms given are "burning (89.8%), itching (85.6%), and skin hypersensitivity (82.3%), with erythema (89.6%) and desquamation (88.7%)".[^1]

**Read those percentages carefully.** They are pooled across seven reports of which five are case reports, and a case report describes one patient. A figure of 99.5% requires a denominator in the hundreds, so nearly all of that weight must come from the single cross-sectional survey. The abstract gives no total patient count and no per-study denominators, so this page cannot say how many people any percentage represents. Treat them as a description of who turns up in the literature and in one survey, not as an incidence or a population estimate.

**Effect size:** not applicable and none reported. Nothing here measures how often TCSW follows steroid use, because there is no denominator of exposed patients.

**Generalizes to:** adult women who used high-potency topical corticosteroids on the face long-term for atopic dermatitis, which is the profile the review itself identifies. It says nothing quantitative about risk for anyone using a mild steroid briefly, or on the body, and should not be read as doing so.

**Key limitation:** the authors' own, and it is the whole finding. "Inconsistent diagnostic criteria, variable definitions, and the primarily descriptive nature of the currently available evidence complicate accurate diagnosis and treatment of TCSW."[^1] Without an agreed definition the percentages above are counting cases that different authors defined differently.

**Context the review adds:** it frames its own motivation around "the phenomenon of steroid phobia" gaining attention on social media, which "has contributed to increased anxiety among patients and potentially influenced their treatment adherence".[^1] That is the review's framing, not a measured outcome in it.
