A method paper, not an efficacy paper. It builds a forearm provocation test to replace the facial stinging test and validates it against that facial test, so what it establishes is agreement between two provocation methods. What a provocation test can and cannot tell you is on [[sensitive-skin-tests-by-what-validation-shows]]; the irritant itself is at [[capsaicin]].[^1][^2]

**Design:** "A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial", described in the methods as "This randomized, double-blind clinical trial".[^1][^2] A separate sub-experiment "enlisted" twenty two subjects "to investigate the necessity of combining tape stripping (TS) and capsaicin testing (CAT)".[^2] No registration number, allocation method or sample-size calculation appears in the paper.

**Population:** "74 healthy subjects aged 20–50 years, comprising 67 facial capsaicin-positive subjects", of whom the facial-positive subgroup "participated in the F-CAT model and forearm model".[^2] Healthy volunteers screened for capsaicin reactivity, not patients with a sensitive-skin diagnosis. The work was done in Guangzhou, China.[^1]

**Intervention vs comparator:** Two comparisons run at once, and they are easy to conflate.

- *For the model:* the new forearm test (FA-TS-CAT, tape stripping five times then a 10-minute 0.01% capsaicin patch across four zones Z1 to Z4) against the facial capsaicin test (F-CAT). The comparator is another provocation test, not a diagnosis.[^2]
- *For the product:* a "4-t-butylcyclohexanol complex emulsion" against placebo. The complex is three supplier ingredients, not one: 4-t-butylcyclohexanol from Symrise (INCI "4-t-butylcyclohexanol, 1,2-pentanediol"), Crocus sativus flower extract from SEQENS, and Gentiana rigescens extract from Yunnan Ingredi.[^2] Any page attributing this result to 4-t-butylcyclohexanol alone is attributing a three-ingredient result to one of them.

**Primary results:**

- **Model agreement.** "Consistent stimulation and natural recovery trends of a* value and TEWL were observed on both the FA-TS-CAT and facial capsaicin test (F-CAT) models within 50 min."[^1] Reported as trends; no correlation coefficient, agreement statistic or Bland-Altman analysis appears.
- **Redness, against placebo.** The a* value was "significantly reduced by 3.99-fold and 3.28-fold at T3 and T4 (p < 0.001), compared to the placebo".[^1] T3 and T4 are 40 and 50 minutes after patch removal.[^2] Elsewhere the same effect is given as a percentage: "Redness was observed to improve by 4.92% after using the complex for 10 min, and by 61.18% after 40 min."[^2]
- **Pain, forearm versus face.** Immediately after patch removal "the perception of pain caused by capsaicin on the forearm was significantly weaker compared to that on the face, with scores of 2.93 and 4.30", on a 1 to 5 semi-subjective scale.[^2]
- **Acceptability.** "94.59% of the subjects reported experiencing stronger discomfort after the F-CAT model", and "up to 95.95% were more willing to participate in the FA-TS-CAT model".[^2] **The abstract renders that second figure as "(95.95% vs. 4.05%)", which reads like two measured rates and is not.** The body reports one percentage; 4.05 is simply the remainder of 100. The two percentages are what 71 of 74 and 70 of 74 come to, which is arithmetic consistent with the stated n, not a count the paper gives.
- **Throughput.** "the test efficiency of the FA-TS-CAT model was found to be three times higher than that of the F-CAT model, given that facial models were limited to comparing the split-face between the control and sample".[^2] This is samples per session, from four forearm zones against a split face, not statistical efficiency or sensitivity.

**Effect size:** Not reportable as stated. A "3.99-fold" reduction in a change-rate of a colorimetric a* value has no clinical scale attached, the paper gives p-values without confidence intervals or standard deviations in its text, and the 4.92% and 61.18% redness figures are improvements over time rather than differences from placebo with a spread.

**Generalizes to:** screening soothing formulations for redness and stinging provoked by capsaicin, in adults aged 20 to 50 who react to facial capsaicin. The authors set that boundary themselves: "sensitive skin is caused by a variety of complex factors. The model was specifically designed to screen soothing ingredients for subjective tingling sensation and skin redness caused by irritants like capsaicin or lactic acid."[^2] It does not establish that anything helps spontaneous day-to-day sensitive skin. A practical caveat for anyone running it: "Persistent skin redness was observed around the elbows and wrists during practical procedures".[^2]

**Key limitation:** the whole paper is its own product's validation, run by the company that sells into this market, and the disclosures do not line up. All five authors are at N.O.D Topia (GuangZhou) Biotechnology or Simpcare (GuangZhou) Biotechnology.[^1] The funding line reads "The authors received no specific funding for this work", the acknowledgments "thank N.O.D topia (GuangZhou) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for financial support", one author's listed contribution is "funding acquisition", and "The authors declare no conflicts of interest."[^2] Separately, the soothing complex's own rationale rests on work nobody can read: "The alleviation of pain and improvement in redness resulting from the combination was found in our internal study."[^2]
