Compares the vitamin C forms this site profiles on the factors that actually differ between them, rather than by a single verdict. Every form shares one active endpoint, L-ascorbic acid, but they part ways on four things: whether the molecule works as applied or must be converted to L-ascorbic acid on the skin, how stable it is in the bottle, its usable pH and how likely it is to sting, and how much controlled human evidence stands behind it. The full mechanism and the per-form citations live on the hub, [[vitamin-c]]; this page maps the family that hub describes.

The pattern is a trade, not a ranking of quality: the form that acts most directly and has the most human data, [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|L-ascorbic acid]], is also the least stable and the most likely to sting, while the gentler, more stable derivatives give up proven efficacy in exchange.

## Active as-is, most human data

[[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|L-ascorbic acid]] (pure vitamin C) is the only form the skin uses without first converting it, and it carries the deepest human record, with repeated vehicle-controlled trials on wrinkles, pigment and hydration. The cost of that directness is formulation: it needs a pH under 3.5 to penetrate, oxidizes readily, and is the form most likely to sting sensitive skin. It is the benchmark the derivatives are measured against.

## Converts on skin, with direct human data

The two phosphate salts are stable near neutral pH and gentler than L-ascorbic acid, and each has one piece of molecule-specific human evidence, though neither is for the anti-aging use vitamin C is usually bought for. [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate|Sodium ascorbyl phosphate]] has the only vehicle-controlled RCT that isolates any of these derivatives, and it tested acne, where it performed about on par with retinol on inflammatory lesions. [[magnesium-ascorbyl-phosphate|Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate]] has the family's one direct pigment result, but from a single uncontrolled 1996 cohort; its controlled trials test it only inside combinations. Both are pro-drugs that skin enzymes must dephosphorylate, and both are poorly absorbed relative to L-ascorbic acid.

## Converts on skin, combination data only

[[ascorbyl-glucoside|Ascorbyl glucoside]] is very stable and gentle, and its conversion to ascorbic acid is shown in ex-vivo human skin, though without a measured rate. Its controlled human trials, however, test either an ascorbyl-glucoside arginine complex or a combination with a separate anti-inflammatory active, so the measured benefit cannot be attributed to the plain molecule. No vehicle-controlled trial isolates ascorbyl glucoside as a standalone active.

## On-skin conversion unproven

These three are grouped by a shared gap: none has controlled human evidence isolating it, and for each, the step of converting to L-ascorbic acid in living skin is unproven. [[tetrahexyldecyl-ascorbate|Tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate]] is oil-soluble and stable and penetrates well in models, but its widely repeated penetration claim comes from a computer simulation, and the one trial with a THD-only arm found that arm did not improve wrinkles, with the benefit tracking to a co-ingredient (ascorbyl tetraisopalmitate is the same molecule under another name). [[3-o-ethyl-ascorbic-acid|3-O-ethyl ascorbic acid]] permeates ex-vivo human skin well and is very stable, but no controlled human efficacy trial exists and its conversion to vitamin C in skin is not demonstrated. [[ascorbyl-palmitate|Ascorbyl palmitate]] has no trial isolating it, an undemonstrated in-skin conversion, and an in-vitro report that it promoted UVB-induced damage in skin cells.
