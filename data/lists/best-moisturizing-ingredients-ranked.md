Moisturizing works by one of a small number of mechanisms: blocking water from leaving the skin (occlusion), replacing the skin's own barrier lipids, or binding water into the outer layer (humectancy), covered in general on [[moisturizing]]. The ten ingredients below are ranked within those mechanisms by how strong the underlying evidence is, not by marketing prominence, and each carries an on-site product where one formula makes a clean example.

The single best-evidenced entry on this list is an occlusive, petrolatum, backed by a primary mechanistic study rather than category-level trial data. Next is the physiologic barrier-lipid triad, ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids, which only works as a proportioned mixture and whose flagship ceramide moisturizers move barrier measurements without a shown edge on eczema severity itself. Glycerin and urea are the two best-documented humectants by a systematic review of the moisturizer field; hyaluronic acid is the most commercially prominent humectant but weaker on the health-outcome side and molecular-weight-dependent. Squalane, panthenol, and colloidal oatmeal close the list as supporting emollient and soothing ingredients rather than headline actives.

> Petrolatum is the reference occlusive and the strongest single entry here; the ceramide-cholesterol-fatty-acid triad is mechanistically necessary but not shown to beat a plainer moisturizer on eczema severity; glycerin and urea are the best-evidenced humectants, with a systematic review favoring urea as the first-line choice; hyaluronic acid, squalane, panthenol, and colloidal oatmeal are real but smaller or more preliminary contributors, each covered in full on its own ingredient page.

## Occlusives

[[petrolatum|Petrolatum]] is the reference occlusive against which every other moisturizing agent is measured. A primary human mechanistic study found it does more than sit on the skin: it accelerates barrier recovery after damage and permeates the stratum corneum rather than forming a suffocating film. That mechanism, plus a national regulatory review finding no expected health risk from cosmetic-grade product, gives it a strong effect on solid evidence, the top grade on this list. Its eczema/dry-skin use draws on the same category-level Cochrane evidence as every other moisturizing ingredient here. On site, [[cerave-healing-ointment|CeraVe Healing Ointment]] is a petrolatum-forward ointment (46.5%-49.5% of the formula, by the manufacturer's and Target's own listings) graded a strong effect on solid evidence for occlusion.

## The physiologic barrier-lipid triad

[[ceramides|Ceramides]], [[cholesterol]], and [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] are the three lipids that pack the stratum corneum's intercellular spaces, and the foundational experiment behind them, Man and colleagues 1996, found they work only as a proportioned mixture: applying just one or two of the three to damaged skin delayed barrier recovery, and only an equimolar (roughly 1:1:1) combination restored it. That makes cholesterol and free fatty acids necessary components of a barrier-repair formula rather than standalone actives; neither is sold or graded as an active ingredient on its own on this site.

Ceramides carry the most product-level evidence of the three. Eczema-prone skin is measurably short of them, and a randomized trial of a ceramide-dominant cream and cleanser found it beat a matched inactive base on barrier measurements (transepidermal water loss, hydration) at every time point. The same trial found no advantage over that base on eczema severity itself, which caps the grade at a modest effect on solid barrier-measurement evidence rather than a proven clinical edge. On site, [[aestura-atobarrier-365-cream|Aestura Atobarrier 365 Cream]] is a ceramide-led barrier cream graded a modest effect on solid evidence for dry, sensitive, barrier-impaired skin, and only a minimal effect on mixed evidence specifically for eczema severity, matching the ceramide page's own split.

## Humectants

[[glycerin|Glycerin]] has the cleanest mechanistic case on the site: an aquaporin-3 knockout mouse model isolated glycerol specifically (not other similar small polyols) as the fix for a stratum-corneum hydration defect, and a placebo-controlled human trial confirmed both hydration and barrier gains against a matched vehicle. A systematic review of 48 moisturizer studies named glycerin and urea the two best-documented humectants in the field, though it rated glycerin's trials as more prone to bias than urea's and concluded urea "may be preferable as a first-line treatment." On site, [[round-lab-1025-dokdo-cream|Round Lab 1025 Dokdo Cream]] rests mainly on its declared 10% glycerin, graded a modest effect on solid evidence for hydration and moisture-barrier support.

[[urea|Urea]] is the humectant that same systematic review favored. In the one eczema trial Cochrane's review isolated for urea against placebo, investigators recorded more improvement in dryness and fewer flares, at low-to-moderate certainty, alongside more reported adverse events than placebo. A separate trial found a urea-containing moisturizer delayed eczema relapse far longer than no treatment at all, though that comparison was against no moisturizer rather than a plainer one. On site, [[prequel-urea-repair-10-moisturizing-milk|Prequel Urea Repair 10% Moisturizing Milk]] carries a confirmed 10% urea concentration and is graded a modest effect on preliminary, category-level evidence.

[[hyaluronic-acid|Hyaluronic acid]] is the most commercially prominent humectant on this list but grades lower on the health-outcome axis than glycerin or urea. A placebo-controlled trial found it reliably raises a skin-hydration reading at every molecular weight tested, a notable effect on solid evidence, but a Raman-spectroscopy study found only lower-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid actually crosses the stratum corneum, with the high-molecular-weight forms common in serums staying on the surface. Its strongest medical evidence, faster wound healing, belongs to a separate product category (hyaluronic-acid dressings and derivatives), not the cosmetic serum. On site, [[torriden-dive-in-low-molecular-hyaluronic-acid-serum|Torriden Dive-In Low Molecular Hyaluronic Acid Serum]] uses several of the lower-molecular-weight hyaluronate forms the penetration data favors, graded a notable effect on solid evidence, though the product's own grade notes glycerin sits ahead of all five hyaluronate forms on its ingredient list.

## Emollient and soothing support

[[squalane|Squalane]] is grouped with petrolatum as an occlusive hydrocarbon in a general moisturizer review, and the Cosmetic Ingredient Review's expert panel found it safe at current use concentrations, but no controlled human trial isolates squalane's own hydration or water-loss effect; the antioxidant signal reported in 2025 is preliminary, cell-culture-only work from a single lab, at concentrations far below what a facial oil applies to skin. On site, [[prequel-am-pm-modern-moisturizer|Prequel AM/PM Modern Moisturizer]] names squalane among its emollients alongside jojoba seed oil, though its grade rests on the formula's dimethicone-plus-glycerin barrier combination as a whole rather than squalane specifically.

[[panthenol|Panthenol]]'s best-supported role is short-term barrier recovery and irritation relief: several controlled trials found a dexpanthenol-containing cream sped barrier repair and cut redness versus a plain vehicle or, after laser resurfacing, versus plain petrolatum, though a notable share of that literature is linked to Bayer, which owns the original dexpanthenol brand. A separate dose-ranging trial found panthenol at 1% and 5% (not the higher concentrations many products use) significantly cut transepidermal water loss. On site, [[purito-panthenol-moisturizer|Purito Mighty Bamboo Panthenol Cream]] declares plain panthenol at 10%, above the concentrations directly tested, and is graded a modest effect on solid evidence for barrier support.

[[colloidal-oatmeal|Colloidal oatmeal]] is one of the few cosmetic ingredients with a formal FDA skin-protectant monograph, and two randomized trials, both funded and run by the same manufacturer, found a 1% cream non-inferior to a prescription barrier cream and able to cut eczema severity scores over 14 days. On site, [[first-aid-beauty-ultra-repair-cream|First Aid Beauty Ultra Repair Cream]] declares colloidal oatmeal at 0.5%, half the 1% concentration used in those trials though still above the FDA's monograph minimum, and is graded a modest effect on mixed evidence for itch and irritation relief.
