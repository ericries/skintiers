[[sensitive-skin]] records the consensus definition and the fact that it contains no test. This page is the practical consequence: the three instruments trials actually use instead, and what each one's validation established. Read a "clinically proven for sensitive skin" claim against whichever of these the sponsor chose.

**The thing to notice before the list.** Not one of these validates against a diagnosis, because there is nothing to validate against. Each is checked against a questionnaire, or against another provocation test, or against its own internal consistency.

## 1. The Sensitive Scale-10 questionnaire, the only one PubMed indexes as a validation study

A ten-item self-report scale tested in 11 countries on 2,966 participants.[^ss10] What the paper established is that "The internal consistency was high" and that scores correlated with "the dry skin type, higher age, female gender, fair phototypes and Dermatology Life Quality Index".[^ss10] Internal consistency means the ten items agree with each other; correlation with age and sex is not a check against a diagnosis. The authors are explicit that it measures severity rather than presence: "Sensitive skin is common but until now there has been no scale for measuring its severity."[^ss10]

Worth knowing: the first author of this scale is also the first author of the consensus definition on [[sensitive-skin]]. The instrument and the syndrome it measures come from the same group.

## 2. The lactic acid sting test, useful as an addition rather than as a standard

The largest recent attempt to pin the diagnosis down screened 975 women in Beijing and Shanghai by questionnaire, then put 307 of them through a patch test, the lactic acid sting test and biophysical measurements.[^last] Its finding was additive: "The diagnostic questionnaires, especially when combined with LAST, improved sensitivity and reduced false negatives for identifying SS."[^last] Combined with. The sting test earns its place as a supplement to a questionnaire, not as a replacement for one.

The same study contains the more awkward result. Comparing sensitive and non-sensitive groups it found "higher hydration and lower pH on the forehead, reduced ceramide and fatty acid levels, and fewer amino acids in the stratum corneum, although skin irritation scores were not significantly different".[^last] The irritation scores, the thing a provocation test exists to produce, did not separate the two groups.

## 3. Capsaicin provocation, validated against another provocation test

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 74 subjects built a forearm model combining tape stripping with capsaicin, explicitly as an alternative to the facial stinging test, whose limitations it names as "its restrictive application site, intense pain sensation, and adverse effects on physical appearance".[^cat] The model was then checked against the facial capsaicin test, and the headline advantages are practical rather than diagnostic: threefold test efficiency, and willingness to participate of "95.95% vs. 4.05%".[^cat]

That is a real improvement in how unpleasant the test is to sit through. It is not evidence that either version identifies sensitive skin, since the comparator is the other test.

## What this means for a product claim

- **"Tested on sensitive skin" most often means self-report**, which, as [[sensitive-skin]] sets out, actually matches the definition. That is the honest end of the range, not the weak end.
- **A sting or capsaicin result is a reaction, not a diagnosis.** It tells you the panel reacted to lactic acid or capsaicin on the day.
- **Ask which instrument, and whether it was combined.** The one study here that set out to improve diagnosis concluded that questionnaire plus sting test beat either alone, and found the irritation scores on their own did not distinguish the groups.[^last]
- **A redness endpoint is measuring something the definition excludes**, since the consensus definition says the skin can appear normal; see [[sensitive-skin]].
