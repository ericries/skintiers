Four different things get called FDA something on a skincare label, and they require wildly different amounts of evidence. This page ranks them by the only question that matters to a buyer: **how much proof did the FDA actually demand before this could be sold?** Every rung below is illustrated by a product or ingredient already profiled here, with its primary document cited on that page.

> **The single most useful sentence is the FDA's own.** In its clearance letters the agency writes that "FDA's issuance of a substantial equivalence determination does not mean that FDA has made a determination that your device complies with other requirements of the Act", and reminds the manufacturer that "device labeling must be truthful and not misleading".[^fda] Clearance is permission to sell. Whether the product works is a separate question, and the agency says as much in the document that grants it.

## The Four Rungs

**1. NDA approval. Efficacy trials required.** A New Drug Application must show the drug works for its stated use. [[delgocitinib]] was approved under NDA219155, and its page carries the trial results and the label's safety sections. If a skincare product says FDA-approved and means it, this is what it means. Very few do.

**2. OTC Monograph. The active is accepted; this product was not tested.** The FDA publishes monographs listing actives and permitted claims. A manufacturer can sell a conforming product without trials of that product. [[clotrimazole]] and [[tolnaftate]] are both OTC Monograph Drugs under citation M005. The active has an evidence base; the specific cream on the shelf was not required to demonstrate anything.

**3. 510(k) clearance. Equivalent to something already sold; no efficacy required.** The device route, and the most misunderstood. A manufacturer shows the device is substantially equivalent to a predicate already on the market. The five device pages on this site document what that looks like in practice, and none of their clearance files contains a clinical efficacy study. [[revive-light-therapy-essentials]] is the clearest case: its summaries state the conclusions come from "nonclinical tests", and its predicates are the same company's earlier devices, three generations deep.

**4. Unapproved drug. Never reviewed at all.** Some marketed drugs predate modern requirements and carry the DailyMed marketing category "unapproved drug other". [[avar-sodium-sulfacetamide-10-sulfur-5]] is one. It is sold legally, it may well work, and the FDA has never assessed it for safety or efficacy.

## What This Ranking Is Not

**It is not a quality ranking, and reading it as one would be a mistake.** [[tolnaftate]] sits on rung two and has randomised placebo-controlled evidence behind its active. A rung-three device may be perfectly good. The axis is regulatory scrutiny, not effectiveness, and the two come apart constantly.

**Disclosure varies within a rung too.** Among 510(k) filings a manufacturer may publish a Summary describing the testing, or file a Statement that publishes nothing and merely certifies it will supply information on request. [[foreo-espada-blue-light-pen]] took the second route, so its public file names no predicate, no wavelength and no testing. [[nuface-trinity-microcurrent-device]] took the first, which is why its page can report that its testing section is headed NON-CLINICAL.

**The indication is narrower than the marketing, routinely.** Check what the product was cleared or approved FOR, not what the box implies. The NuFACE clearance covers "facial and neck stimulation" for "over-the-counter cosmetic use", which is not a claim about lifting or firming.

## How to Check Any Product Yourself

Both registries are free and public. For drugs, DailyMed carries the label and states the marketing category, which is where NDA, OTC Monograph and unapproved drug are distinguished. For devices, the openFDA 510(k) database gives the clearance record, and accessdata.fda.gov hosts the clearance letter and any summary. Every regulatory claim on this site was established that way.
