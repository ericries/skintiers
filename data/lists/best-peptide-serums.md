Peptides are well-tolerated cosmetic ingredients with a modest, mostly preliminary effect on the appearance of aging, and the evidence differs sharply by peptide class; the full breakdown is on [[peptides]]. The most consistent measured benefit across the class is short-term hydration, not the antiwrinkle claims that dominate labels: a 2026 meta-analysis of 19 trials found the pooled wrinkle benefit was largely driven by peptides taken by mouth rather than applied to skin. One peptide carries extra weight in several serums below, copper tripeptide-1 (GHK-Cu), whose laboratory and wound-healing record is strong but whose human cosmetic case rests mainly on reviews from the peptide's own discoverer; that split evidence is on [[copper-peptides]]. Most of these serums also lean on hyaluronic acid and other humectants, a separate and better-established hydration mechanism covered on [[hyaluronic-acid]]. Interventions with stronger anti-aging evidence, daily sunscreen and prescription tretinoin among them, are ranked on [[anti-aging]].

> None of these serums has been tested in a trial of its own. They are ranked by how their peptide blends line up against the evidence on [[peptides]] and [[copper-peptides]]: breadth of peptides, a well-supported hydration base, and inclusion of the better-studied peptides push a serum up; a single peptide whose human cosmetic case is weak (such as acetyl hexapeptide-8) pushes a serum down.

## Broad peptide blends with a hydration base

**[[cosrx-6-peptide-skin-booster-serum|COSRX The 6 Peptide Skin Booster Serum]].** Six peptides, including copper tripeptide-1 (GHK-Cu) and the botox-like acetyl hexapeptide-8, plus niacinamide, in a glycerin, hyaluronic-acid and amino-acid base, at about $25 for 150 mL, several times the volume of a typical peptide serum. The breadth and the price per mL make this the cheapest way among the three to try a large peptide blend that includes the better-studied copper peptide, though acetyl hexapeptide-8, the peptide it names first, has limited skin penetration by a 2025 review, and the anti-aging endpoints stay cosmetic rather than a measure of skin health.

**[[the-ordinary-multi-peptide-ha-serum|The Ordinary Multi-Peptide + HA Serum (Buffet)]].** Five peptide complexes (Argirelox, Matrixyl 3000, Matrixyl synthe'6, Syn-Ake, Relistase) in a glycerin, hyaluronic-acid and amino-acid base, about $19.90 for 30 mL. It shares the same hydration case as the COSRX serum and a comparably broad peptide list, but leaves out copper peptide and niacinamide and costs more per mL for less volume.

**[[paulas-choice-peptide-booster|Paula's Choice Pro-Collagen Multi-Peptide Booster]].** The most legitimately peptide-dense serum here: eight peptides sit high on the list (fourth through eleventh), including the Matrixyl 3000 pair, so it is not a trace "fairy-dusted" formula. It is graded on the same ceiling as the rest, since the topical-peptide evidence class is modest, but its formulation is the strongest case in this group.

**[[naturium-multi-peptide-advanced-serum|Naturium Multi-Peptide Advanced Serum]].** A multi-peptide serum in a hydrating base at a low price point; a reasonable broad-blend option ranked, like the others, against the modest peptide evidence on [[peptides]].

**[[allies-of-skin-peptides-antioxidants-advanced-firming-daily-treatment|Allies of Skin Peptides & Antioxidants Advanced Firming Daily Treatment]].** Pairs a peptide blend with an antioxidant complex in a pricier firming treatment; the antioxidant side is its own case, and the peptide firming claim sits on the same class-level evidence.

## Copper peptide alone

**[[the-ordinary-multi-peptide-copper-peptides-serum|The Ordinary Multi-Peptide + Copper Peptides 1% Serum]].** Built around copper tripeptide-1 (GHK-Cu) with a smaller signal-peptide blend, in the same style of hyaluronic-acid and amino-acid base as the other two. Copper peptide's laboratory case for driving collagen and elastin synthesis is one of the stronger mechanistic stories among cosmetic peptides, but its human cosmetic evidence is the weakest of the three: it rests mainly on reviews from the peptide's own discoverer rather than independent trials, and copper peptide has limited permeation through the stratum corneum, so a leave-on serum reaching the dermis is not assured (both detailed on [[copper-peptides]]). The formula also excludes niacinamide, and the manufacturer directs against combining it with other direct acids, retinoids, or vitamin C, narrowing how it fits alongside other actives compared with the other two.

**[[niod-copper-amino-isolate-serum-3-1-1|NIOD Copper Amino Isolate Serum 3 1:1 (CAIS3)]].** A concentrated copper-peptide serum built around a manufacturer-declared 1% GHK-Cu with additional signal peptides. It carries the same split copper-peptide evidence, a strong laboratory story against a thin independent human case (see [[copper-peptides]]), at a higher price than The Ordinary's copper serum.

## Single-peptide serums

**[[the-ordinary-argireline-solution-10|The Ordinary Argireline Solution 10%]].** A cheap single-peptide serum whose only active is acetyl hexapeptide-8, marketed as needle-free "topical Botox." It ranks at the bottom here: the peptide's independent evidence is the weakest of the group, an independent toxicology study found almost none of it penetrates the skin, and small trials show only a modest surface wrinkle-depth effect (see [[argireline-acetyl-hexapeptide-8]]).
