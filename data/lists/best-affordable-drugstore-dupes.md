A "dupe" only earns the name when it discloses the same active at the same strength as the product it is copying, ideally at a matching pH for pH-dependent actives like L-ascorbic acid. Marketing copy calling something "clinically equivalent" is not evidence; a printed label percentage and a measured pH are. This list pairs pricier, better-studied products already on the site with cheaper alternatives, ranked by how completely each cheaper option discloses a real match, not by how loudly it claims to be one.

## The tier list

**Vitamin C: [[skinceuticals-c-e-ferulic|SkinCeuticals C E Ferulic]] and its budget alternatives.** The reference formula was clinically tested at 15% L-ascorbic acid, 1% vitamin E, 0.5% ferulic acid, at a low pH; the case for that combination is on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]. [[trader-joes-nourish-vitamins-c-e-facial-serum|Trader Joe's Vitamin C Serum]] tops this list because its label discloses all three of those exact percentages and independent pH-strip testing put it in range, the fullest disclosed match of the three at $9.99. [[maelove-the-glow-maker|Maelove The Glow Maker]] matches the 15% L-ascorbic acid figure and an independent teardown reports a pH close to the reference, but its vitamin E and ferulic acid percentages are undisclosed. [[timeless-20-vitamin-c-e-ferulic-serum|Timeless 20% Vitamin C + E Ferulic Serum]] runs the L-ascorbic acid stronger than the studied concentration and, per the same independent teardown, its ferulic acid runs roughly double the reference ratio, so it is a related formula rather than a disclosed match.

**Azelaic acid: prescription strength without the prescription.** [[finacea-azelaic-acid-15-gel|Finacea]] is FDA-approved at 15%, and [[cos-de-baha-az20-azelaic-acid-20-serum|Cos De BAHA AZ20]] is labeled 20%, matching the strength used in the foundational acne and melasma trials and in the prescription cream AZELEX, for about $12; the trial evidence is on [[azelaic-acid]]. [[the-ordinary-azelaic-acid-suspension-10|The Ordinary Azelaic Acid Suspension 10%]] is labeled at half that strength, a real but lower-dose version of the same active.

**Niacinamide: cheap and consistently disclosed.** Niacinamide is stable, inexpensive to formulate, and its evidence, covered on [[niacinamide]], does not hinge on a narrow pH window the way vitamin C's does, so budget versions are not really "dupes" of a pricier original so much as the same commodity active sold at a lower markup. [[the-ordinary-niacinamide-10-zinc-1|The Ordinary Niacinamide 10% + Zinc 1%]] discloses its concentration on the label at a low price and is the version most directly comparable to pricier niacinamide serums built on the same 10% figure.

**Retinoids: adjacent, not equivalent.** [[the-ordinary-granactive-retinoid-2-emulsion|The Ordinary Granactive Retinoid 2% Emulsion]] is priced like a drugstore product but is not a dupe of anything: its actual HPR and retinol fractions are undisclosed, and no published trial tests this exact blend. It sits at the bottom of this list for that reason, not because retinoids lack evidence, which is covered on [[retinoids]]; treat it as a low-cost way to try a retinoid family, not as a matched substitute for a studied formula.

None of these save you from sunscreen or from checking irritation tolerance individually; a disclosed-match dupe still needs to be introduced the same cautious way as the product it copies. Nothing here is medical advice.
