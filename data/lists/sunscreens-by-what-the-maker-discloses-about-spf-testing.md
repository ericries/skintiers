Every sunscreen sold in the US carries an SPF number that was tested somehow. Almost none of them tell you how. This page sorts by who published an actual measurement, which turns out to separate the field far more sharply than the SPF numbers do.

> **One product out of forty-seven.** Across the sunscreen pages on this site, exactly one maker publishes both its testing protocol and the numbers it got. KraveBeauty states that "For Beet The Sun we carried out a total of 6 tests: 2 FDA Broad Spectrum Tests, 2 FDA SPF Static Tests, 1 ISO 24444 (2019 version) and 1 ISO 24442", adds that "It's common to only perform these tests once, but we knew it was important for you to be extra clear", and reports that "we found the exact rating to be 42 & 43 which fell under our projected 40 SPF rating".[^krave] Labelling 40 on a measured 42 to 43 errs in the buyer's favour.

## The three rungs

**Rung 1: the maker publishes protocol and numbers.** Only [[kravebeauty-beet-the-sun-spf-40]]. ISO 24444 and ISO 24442 are the real in-vivo standards for SPF and UVA respectively, and naming them correctly is itself a signal, because a brand gesturing vaguely at clinical testing is saying nothing checkable.

**Rung 2: the maker publishes nothing, but somebody independent measured it.** [[beauty-of-joseon-relief-sun-rice-probiotics-spf50]] sits here because Consumer Reports lab-tested it and put its average tested SPF at 36 against a 50+ label.[^cr] Its SPF and PA figures are Korean regulatory label claims rather than FDA-tested ones. Independent measurement is weaker than full disclosure in detail and stronger in incentive.

**Rung 3: the label rating and nothing else.** Forty-four of the forty-seven.[^census] "Broad Spectrum" is a US monograph term every compliant sunscreen carries, and a PA rating is a result without a published method, so neither counts as disclosure here.

## The thing this page cannot tell you

Disclosure is not protection. The one brand that published its own testing came in above its label; the one measured independently came in well below. That is two products, which is not a pattern, and it would be a serious error to read this page as evidence that transparent brands protect better. What it does say is narrower and still useful: for one sunscreen here you can check the claim, and for forty-four you are taking it on trust.

The same Consumer Reports testing put another formula from the same brand, its US version, at an average tested SPF of 19.[^cr] Same brand, same marketing, very different measurement, which is the strongest argument on this page for wanting numbers at all.
