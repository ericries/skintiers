**Read this first if you arrived from the device ladder.** [[home-use-skincare-devices-by-evidence]] grades home intense pulsed light at Level D and not recommended, but that grade is **for acne**, from a review covering acne and facial rejuvenation only. It does not apply to hair removal, and the two home IPL products below are hair-removal devices, graded here on the hair question only.

## What the two literatures say

**The professional side is the stronger case, and it is independent.** A 2013 review of laser hair removal concluded that "The literature supports the use of the alexandrite, diode, Nd:YAG and IPL devices for long-term hair removal", and that "Current in-office laser hair removal devices effectively provide a durable solution for unwanted hair removal".[^gan] Its most practically useful line concerns skin tone: "Because of its longer wavelength, the Nd:YAG is the best laser system to use for pigmented skin."[^gan] That is a wavelength-specific recommendation no home device on this site offers, and it matters because burns and pigment change are the real risks in deeper skin tones.

**On home devices, the same 2013 review withheld judgement.** It stated that "Further research is needed regarding the safety and efficacy of home-use devices".[^gan] Thirteen years on, that caution is the honest starting point for the two products graded above.

**The warmer verdict on home devices comes with authorship worth knowing about.** A 2019 review reports that "Significant basic science and clinical evidence has been published to confirm the clinical efficacy and technical safety of many laser and IPL home-use devices for hair removal".[^town] It also makes a genuinely interesting mechanistic argument: home appliances are "different compared to professional systems both in terms of fluence per pulse and in terms of biological mechanisms", with low-fluence pulses inducing catagen transition rather than destroying the follicle outright, which the authors suggest could produce follicle miniaturisation cumulatively.[^town] And it reports that "long-term application of a commercially-available home-use IPL appliance resulted in persistent hair reduction (80%) one year after last treatment".[^town] Three of its seven authors are listed at Philips Research, PubMed records no conflict-of-interest statement for it, and Philips manufactures one of the two home devices graded above.[^town] None of that makes the 80% wrong. It does mean the figure should not be read as independent, and the review does not name the appliance, so the number cannot be attached to either product here.

**What nobody has published, as far as this page could establish.** A head-to-head trial of a named home IPL device against clinic laser. Both sources describe their categories; neither compares them directly, so the ranking above is a judgement about evidence quality rather than a measured difference in hair reduction.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Light-based hair removal carries burn and pigment-change risk that varies with skin tone, and the one skin-tone-specific recommendation found here points to an in-office wavelength rather than a home appliance.
