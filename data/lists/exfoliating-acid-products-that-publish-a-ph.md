pH governs how much of an exfoliating acid sits in its free, active form, which is why two products at the same labelled percentage can behave differently. What that means clinically is less settled than the internet suggests, and the one trial that varied pH directly points the other way: testing glycolic acid at pH 3.25, 3.80 and 4.40 on dry, scaly skin, it reported that "All pH levels and concentrations demonstrated significant improvement in the condition of the skin with trends implying that increasing the pH increases efficacy".[^dinardo] So treat a low number as a description of how the formula is built, not as a promise about results. The acids themselves are covered on [[exfoliating-acids-by-strength]]. This page answers a narrower question: which products tell you the number at all.

**Eleven do, out of forty-two.** Counting every product page on this site whose key actives include an exfoliating acid, 42 qualify and 11 publish a numeric pH. The other 31 give a percentage and leave the pH unstated, including well-known exfoliants from CeraVe, Pixi, Sunday Riley, Dr Dennis Gross and Farmacy. A percentage without a pH is half a specification.

## The tier list

The order is by published pH, lowest first, and the tiers mark bands rather than merit. Two entries sit here for honesty rather than comparability: [[lac-hydrin-ammonium-lactate-lotion-12]] is a prescription-strength ammonium lactate lotion whose job at pH 4.4 to 5.5 is humectancy and keratolysis rather than low-pH exfoliation, and [[cosrx-low-ph-good-morning-gel-cleanser]] is a rinse-off cleanser, where contact time is seconds and pH matters far less.

Note also that publishing a RANGE is not the same as publishing a value. [[the-ordinary-mandelic-acid-10-ha]] states 3.5 to 4.5, a full point wide, which spans a meaningful difference in free-acid availability. A narrow range such as 3.50 to 3.70 is a more useful disclosure than a wide one, and neither is as useful as a measured figure per batch, which no brand here provides.

## What This List Is Not

It is not a ranking of which exfoliant to buy, and a low pH is not a recommendation. A strong acid at pH 3.0 on skin that cannot tolerate it is worse than a gentler one at 3.9, and the DiNardo trial above found the efficacy trend running toward the HIGHER pH it tested, not the lower.[^dinardo] Choosing between these belongs to [[exfoliation]] and the acid pages; what this page adds is the disclosure, which is the part brands control and most of them withhold.

## How This Was Counted

Every product page on this site whose `key_actives` names an exfoliating acid (glycolic, salicylic, lactic, mandelic, ammonium lactate, malic, tartaric, citric, gluconolactone, lactobionic or polyhydroxy acids) was taken, giving 42 pages, and each tested for a published numeric pH. The test requires the token pH followed by a number and explicitly excludes the phrase pH adjuster, which appears in ingredient lists and is not a published figure. That exclusion changes the answer: without it the [[murad-rapid-dark-spot-correcting-serum]] counts as publishing a pH, when what its page records is sodium hydroxide listed as a pH adjuster. Every pH above is the manufacturer-stated figure already recorded and sourced on that product's own page, reproduced here rather than re-sourced; follow the linked page for its provenance.
