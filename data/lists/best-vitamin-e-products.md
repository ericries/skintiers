Topical [[tocopherol|vitamin E]] is a real antioxidant, but on its own the clinical case is thin: a review of the dermatology literature found "multiple human studies have shown no effects of vitamin E on the prevention or development of skin cancers," and studies of vitamin E for burns and scars "have been disappointing."[^keen] It is also a recognized contact allergen in some formulations. The one place vitamin E earns its keep is as a partner to vitamin C, where it measurably boosts photoprotection, covered in full on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]. This list ranks products by how closely they match that studied role rather than by scar-cream or anti-aging marketing.

## The tier list

**[[skinceuticals-c-e-ferulic|SkinCeuticals C E Ferulic]].** The formula behind the actual research: Murray and colleagues applied the same 15% L-ascorbic acid, 1% alpha-tocopherol, 0.5% ferulic acid combination to human skin before UV exposure and found it "provided significant and meaningful photoprotection," while Lin's earlier pig-skin work showed adding ferulic acid to vitamin C and E "doubled" the photoprotection.[^murray][^lin] Both studies came from the lab that developed and holds a patent on the formula.[^lin][^murray]

**[[trader-joes-nourish-vitamins-c-e-facial-serum|Trader Joe's Vitamin C Serum]].** One of the few drugstore-tier options with a fully disclosed concentration: 15% L-ascorbic acid, 1% alpha-tocopherol, 0.5% ferulic acid, matching the studied CEFer ratio almost exactly.

**[[timeless-20-vitamin-c-e-ferulic-serum|Timeless 20% Vitamin C + E Ferulic Acid Serum]].** The same three-ingredient concept, vitamin C plus vitamin E plus ferulic acid, at a much lower price; the manufacturer discloses the 20% L-ascorbic acid figure but not the vitamin E or ferulic acid percentages, so it is not a verified match to the studied ratio.

**[[medik8-c-tetra-vitamin-c-serum|Medik8 C-Tetra Vitamin C Serum]].** Uses a stabilized vitamin C ester rather than L-ascorbic acid, with tocopherol as a listed key active; the stabilized-derivative tradeoffs are covered on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]] and [[vitamin-c]].

**[[paulas-choice-c15-super-booster|Paula's Choice C15 Super Booster]].** Discloses the 15% vitamin C concentration but not the vitamin E or ferulic acid percentages; tocopherol is listed as a key active alongside the vitamin C.

**[[bio-oil-skincare-oil|Bio-Oil Skincare Oil]].** The best-known vitamin-E-branded product for scars and stretch marks, and the weakest fit here: it is a mineral-oil-based emollient with only cosmetic doses of vitamin A and vitamin E, and the evidence review behind [[tocopherol]] found vitamin E studies for burns and scars "have been disappointing." Any benefit here is occlusive moisturizing, not a vitamin E effect.

Nothing on this list is a vitamin E monotherapy worth seeking out for its own sake. If the goal is antioxidant photoprotection, pair vitamin C with vitamin E and sunscreen; if the goal is scar or stretch mark fading, the evidence does not support vitamin E doing that job.
