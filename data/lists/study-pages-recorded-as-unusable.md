Most of the 151 study pages on this site exist because a paper reports something worth grading. Three exist for the opposite reason: a reader or a later pass could mistake them for evidence, so the defect is the record.[^pages] They fail in three different ways, and the ways are more useful than the papers.

Nothing here says these papers are fraudulent or that their conclusions are false. It says that what was published cannot be weighed, which is a separate and more common problem.

## 1. Indexed as a trial, written as a case report

[[karimi-2025-neurocosmetic-post-procedure-cream]] tests an unnamed post-procedure cream after fully ablative erbium:YAG resurfacing of the lower eyelid. PubMed indexes its publication type as **Randomized Controlled Trial**. The abstract calls it a "pilot study" and never states that anyone was randomized, how participants were allocated, or whether anyone was blinded. It also reports no sample size and no statistic of any kind, and names neither the cream nor the "commonly used anhydrous topical" it is compared against.

**The transferable lesson:** a PubMed publication type is an indexing decision, not a design guarantee. If you are filtering a literature search on `Randomized Controlled Trial[pt]`, this paper passes the filter and answers nothing.

## 2. Significance reported without magnitude

[[amin-2025-oral-hyaluronic-acid-meta-analysis]] pools 7 randomized trials of oral hyaluronic acid supplements. Its abstract gives no total participant count, no per-trial sample sizes and no effect size anywhere, while its own conclusion refers to "the study's limited sample size" without saying what it was. Its search covered two sources, PubMed and Google Scholar, one of which is a general search engine. And it narrates its three null outcomes as "a general trend of improvement".

**The transferable lesson:** p-values without magnitudes cannot tell you whether anything happened that a person would notice, and a conclusion that absorbs null results into a "trend" is doing rhetorical work the data did not do. Note also that oral supplementation is a different question from the topical ingredient, which is graded on [[hyaluronic-acid]].

## 3. Direction and p-values, no numbers behind them

[[wang-2025-encorelane-sensitive-skin]] is the most carefully executed of the three, and still unusable for grading magnitude. Its clinical half is a 6-week double-blind half-face study in 23 sensitive-skin subjects, which is a sound design at a small size. The abstract then reports that the blend "significantly improved TEWL, R2, F4, and crow's feet wrinkles (p < 0.05)" and gives no magnitudes, no confidence intervals and no baseline-to-endpoint values for any of them.

**The transferable lesson:** a good design does not rescue an abstract that withholds its numbers. This paper also has a disclosure problem, since all seven authors work for the company that owns the ingredient while the paper declares no conflict, but that axis belongs to [[manufacturer-authored-studies-by-what-they-disclosed]] and is ranked there rather than repeated here.

## Why there is no tier list on this page

The tier vocabulary used across this site grades evidence strength, and applying it to three papers that cannot be graded would invert its meaning. These three are not ranked against each other, because a missing sample size, an absent effect size and a withheld magnitude are not points on one scale.
