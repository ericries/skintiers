In 2019 the FDA proposed sorting every US sunscreen filter into three groups, and the result is not the one a shelf implies: of the sixteen filters legally sold in American sunscreens, the agency proposed that exactly two had enough data behind them.[^fr]

> **Read this as history, not as law.** The 2019 document was a *proposed* rule, and it never took effect as written. Sunscreen regulation moved to the OTC monograph order process the following year, and the live vehicle is now Monograph M020, with a proposed order published in September 2021 and amendments as recently as 2026.[^m020] What the 2019 proposal is still good for is the question it answers plainly and nothing else does: which filters the FDA had actually seen enough safety data on. Being on sale and being well-evidenced turned out to be different things, and two filters in the same bottle can sit on opposite sides.

## Category I: enough data to be GRASE

Two filters, both mineral. FDA found the record for [[zinc-oxide]] and [[titanium-dioxide]] sufficient "to support a proposal that sunscreen products containing these ingredients (at concentrations of up to 25 percent) would be GRASE".[^fr] GRASE means generally recognised as safe and effective.

## Category II: proposed as not GRASE

Two filters, both effectively gone from the US market already. FDA wrote that its review of aminobenzoic acid (PABA) and trolamine salicylate "has caused us to conclude that the risks associated with use of these active ingredients in sunscreen products outweigh their benefits".[^fr] Neither has a page here, because neither is something a reader will find to buy.

## Category III: the other twelve, all of them

Everything else. FDA proposed Category III for "cinoxate, dioxybenzone, ensulizole, homosalate, meradimate, octinoxate, octisalate, octocrylene, padimate O, sulisobenzone, oxybenzone, or avobenzone".[^fr] That is every chemical filter the US allows, including the ones most sunscreens are built from: [[avobenzone]], [[octocrylene]], [[homosalate]], [[octisalate]], [[octinoxate]], [[oxybenzone]] and [[ensulizole]]. Meradimate is the same filter as [[menthyl-anthranilate]].

**Category III is a statement about the file, not about harm.** It means the data in front of the agency were insufficient to classify the ingredient either way. The category says nothing about whether a filter is dangerous, and reading it as a safety verdict is the most common mistake made with this document.

**Ten of the twelve, not all twelve.** FDA named a shorter list where "most significant gaps in the administrative record exist": cinoxate, dioxybenzone, ensulizole, homosalate, meradimate, octinoxate, octisalate, octocrylene, padimate O, and sulisobenzone.[^fr] Oxybenzone and avobenzone are Category III but are not on it, which is worth knowing given how much attention oxybenzone attracts.

## What this list is not

It is not an evidence ranking and carries no tier list. Regulatory status and protective performance are different axes, and a filter can sit in Category III while protecting well; for how these filters compare on what they actually block, see [[uv-filters-by-uva-protection]]. What has since become of these proposals, which for most filters is nothing, is tracked on [[what-otc-monograph-m020-settled-and-what-it-left-open]].
