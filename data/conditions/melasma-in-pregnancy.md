Chloasma, also called "the mask of pregnancy," is melasma triggered or worsened by pregnancy hormones. It is extremely common, with pregnancy prevalence estimates ranging from 36.4% to 75% across studies, and it typically appears as symmetric brown patches across the cheeks, forehead, nose, and upper lip, the same centrofacial pattern described on [[melasma]], which covers the condition's mechanism and full non-pregnancy treatment tier list in detail.[^zhao]

**The skeptical bottom line:** the single highest-yield move is rigorous photoprotection, including visible light, and [[azelaic-acid]] is the main active considered compatible with pregnancy. [[hydroquinone]] and [[retinoids]] like [[tretinoin]], melasma's usual first-line actives, are best set aside until after pregnancy and breastfeeding. A meaningful share of chloasma fades on its own after delivery, so treating it aggressively during pregnancy is often unnecessary in the first place.

## How to know you have this

Chloasma follows the same recognition pattern as melasma generally, described in full on [[melasma]], with a few pregnancy-specific patterns clinicians and reviews note:

- **Usually shows up in the second trimester.** A 2024 review states melasma "typically appears during the second trimester of pregnancy," which is why it recommends starting photoprotection early rather than waiting for pigment to appear.[^zhao]
- **More likely with a personal or family history.** The same review reports women with melasma in a previous pregnancy were about 44 times more likely to develop it again, and that 37.6% of affected pregnant women had a mother, and 25.9% a sister, with the condition too.[^zhao]
- **More common in darker skin tones and with more sun exposure.** The review describes chloasma as primarily affecting Fitzpatrick phototypes III through VI, with higher rates among women who spent more time outdoors.[^zhao]
- **Same symmetric, centrofacial pattern as melasma generally.** The patches cluster on the cheeks, forehead, nose, and upper lip, as described in full on [[melasma]]; only a clinician can confirm the diagnosis over other pregnancy-era pigment changes.

Pregnancy skin changes should be discussed with an OB-GYN or dermatologist, who can weigh trimester, dosage, and personal health history in ways a general page cannot. Nothing here should be used to delay or replace appropriate prenatal or dermatologic care.

## Why the usual melasma playbook changes here

[[melasma]] ranks hydroquinone and retinoid-containing combinations among the strongest topical options, but both are the actives pregnancy-safety reviews flag as the ones to set aside. A 2011 review found 35% to 45% of topical hydroquinone is absorbed into the bloodstream, the highest absorption rate among the topicals it evaluated, and advises minimizing exposure until more data exist.[^bozzo] Topical retinoids, including [[tretinoin]], are minimally absorbed, and two prospective studies of roughly 100 pregnant women each found no increased malformation risk, but the same review still advises against encouraging their use, citing case reports of birth defects consistent with retinoid embryopathy that those larger studies have not fully ruled out.[^bozzo] Oral isotretinoin, the same drug class, carries an FDA boxed warning identifying it as a human teratogen and is contraindicated in pregnancy.[^isotretinoin] Full detail on both actives' pregnancy evidence, plus how the rest of skincare's common actives are graded, lives on [[pregnancy-safe-skincare]].

With those two set aside, photoprotection and azelaic acid carry most of the load. A 2024 review focused on melasma prevention during pregnancy recommends physical (mineral) sunscreens over chemical ones for pregnant patients, on the grounds that chemical filters are more systemically absorbed, and names iron-oxide and titanium-dioxide tinted formulas specifically because they also block visible light on top of UV.[^zhao] Visible light is a documented melasma trigger outside pregnancy too, covered in [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and in the tinted-sunscreen evidence on [[melasma]] itself. [[azelaic-acid]] is the active with the clearest pregnancy safety data: a 2023 review of acne treatment during pregnancy and lactation reports only 4% to 8% systemic absorption and no reported adverse fetal effects at concentrations up to 20% in any trimester.[^ly]

Niacinamide and vitamin C appear on melasma's general tier list as moderate options, but neither has a dedicated pregnancy-safety study behind it; [[pregnancy-safe-skincare]] leaves both off its own tier list for the same reason, and this page does the same rather than assert a safety it cannot source.

## It often fades on its own

Chloasma is frequently self-limited. The same 2024 review states that melasma arising during pregnancy typically regresses within a year of delivery, though it also reports that up to 30% of cases persist even a decade later, and that recurrence in a later pregnancy is common.[^zhao] That mixed picture, usually improves, sometimes does not, is why reviewers frame pregnancy-era treatment around the "cause no harm" principle: the same review notes that in most cases, active treatment of melasma is not recommended during pregnancy at all, with photoprotection carrying most of the weight until after delivery.[^zhao] Postpartum, once breastfeeding plans are settled with a clinician, the full range of melasma's usual first-line options, including hydroquinone and retinoids, becomes available again on [[melasma]].

## Honest limits

Controlled trials that deliberately expose pregnant women to a treatment are considered unethical, so pregnancy-specific melasma evidence is thin and mostly precautionary: absorption data, case reports, and general dermatoses-of-pregnancy reviews rather than the randomized comparisons used on [[melasma]] itself. This page only grades the actives that already have a page on this site and a source discussing their pregnancy standing; anything else is left ungraded rather than guessed at. Lasers and other in-office procedures, and prescription decisions generally, belong to an OB-GYN or dermatologist managing the individual case, not to a general page like this one.

## The Evidence

There is no controlled trial base specific to treating melasma during pregnancy, since deliberately testing treatments on pregnant women is considered unethical. The epidemiology, trimester timing, risk factors, and postpartum course here come from a single 2024 review focused specifically on melasma prevention during pregnancy.[^zhao] The pregnancy-appropriateness calls for hydroquinone and topical retinoids come from a 2011 review of skin care product safety in pregnancy that tabulates systemic absorption across common actives,[^bozzo] the oral isotretinoin call comes from that drug's FDA-approved label,[^isotretinoin] and the azelaic acid call comes from a 2023 review of acne treatment during pregnancy and lactation that reports its absorption and fetal-outcome data.[^ly] Both of those reviews, and the full grading of every other common active, live on [[pregnancy-safe-skincare]]; this page only restates the calls relevant to melasma rather than re-deriving them.
