Scars left by surgery or injury, and what the evidence says about caring for one while it heals. Scarring from acne is a different problem with a different literature and is on [[acne-scars]].

> **The two things people are told most often are both less settled than they sound.** Silicone is the standard recommendation, and a 2026 systematic review of randomised trials says in its own opening that while silicone gel fluid "is widely recognised as a leading non-invasive treatment, there is inconsistent evidence of superiority".[^silicone] Vitamin E is the standard folk remedy, and a systematic review of six studies split three to three on whether it helps at all, with two reporting adverse reactions.[^vite] Neither finding means do nothing. They mean the confident version you have heard is more confident than the papers.

## How to know you have this

You do not need to identify a surgical scar; you have one because you had a wound. What is worth recognising is when a healing wound stops being ordinary, and that is a question for the clinician who treated you rather than for a skincare page.

The reasons to make contact rather than wait are the usual wound ones: spreading redness, increasing pain, heat, discharge or fever, or a wound edge that opens. Separately, a scar that becomes raised, itchy and thickened is worth showing to someone, because treatment decisions differ once it does. The 2026 review describes hypertrophic scars as "elevated, pigmented scarring confined within the wound boundaries", and notes they can cause "pruritus, pain, stiffness and contractures" as well as psychological effects, so an itchy raised scar is a recognised clinical entity rather than a cosmetic complaint to dismiss.[^silicone]

## What the evidence supports

**Silicone, with the caveat stated.** The 2026 review searched Medline, Embase, CINAHL and the Cochrane Library under PRISMA, included 35 papers and meta-analysed 20, restricting itself to randomised controlled trials and comparing silicone gel fluid against placebo, no treatment, silicone sheets and other treatments.[^silicone] That is a serious evidence base. What this site cannot give you from it is the size of the benefit: the published abstract describes the methods and the gap it set out to address without reporting the pooled results, so the numbers are not quoted here. The honest summary is that silicone is the most studied option and the review's own framing of the evidence for superiority is "inconsistent".

**Vitamin E, which is not supported as a solo treatment.** A systematic review in Aesthetic Surgery Journal found six studies. Three reported significant improvement in scar appearance, one of those as monotherapy in children and two as combination therapy; the other three "showed no significant improvement in scar appearance when using topical vitamin E as monotherapy".[^vite] Two of the six reported adverse events, "contact dermatitis and increased incidence of itching and rash".[^vite] Its conclusion is the useful sentence: "there is not yet sufficient evidence that monotherapy with topical vitamin E has a significant beneficial effect on scar appearance to justify its widespread use".[^vite] The verdict is about monotherapy specifically, and says nothing either way about vitamin E used alongside something else.

**What this page does not grade.** There is no tier list here. Grading would mean scoring interventions for this condition specifically, and this site has evidence for two of them, one of which does not report its effect size. The other things commonly advised during healing, keeping the wound moist, minimising tension across it, and sun protection, are not assessed on this page because no source was read for them here; [[petrolatum]] covers occlusion generally and makes no scar claim.
