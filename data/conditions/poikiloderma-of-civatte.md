Poikiloderma of Civatte is "a benign, common and chronic condition" in which sun-damaged skin develops the combination that gives poikiloderma its name: mottled reddish-brown pigment (hyper- and hypopigmentation) alongside dilated fine blood vessels, or telangiectasia, with some skin atrophy.[^dermnet] It sits in the melanoderma (pigmented skin disorder) group and was first described by the French dermatologist Civatte in 1923.[^dermnet] The honest framing up front: DermNet is blunt that "the results of the treatment may be disappointing," and it is "a chronic and irreversible condition" that "responds poorly to available treatments."[^dermnet] Topicals here are damage control, not a cure.

## How to know you have this

The pattern is distinctive: "confluent reddish-brown patches with atrophy that symmetrically involve sun-exposed areas such as the sides of the neck and lateral aspect of the cheeks," and it "usually spares the shaded area under the chin," the classic sign that points to chronic UV exposure rather than another cause.[^dermnet] It is usually asymptomatic, though some people notice mild burning, itching, episodic flushing, or sensitivity in the affected skin, and there is no systemic involvement.[^dermnet] It is most common in middle-aged and elderly fair-skinned people (Fitzpatrick I and II) with significant sun exposure, and it is seen most often in postmenopausal women.[^dermnet] Diagnosis is clinical; a biopsy, if done, shows epidermal atrophy, pigment incontinence, telangiectasia, and solar elastosis, the microscopic signature of chronic photoaging.[^dermnet]

## What is happening in the skin

The exact cause is unknown, but long-term sun exposure is considered the main driver, with fair skin, photosensitizing ingredients in cosmetics and perfumes, hormonal changes around menopause or low estrogen, and a genetic predisposition listed as contributing factors.[^dermnet] The result is decades of cumulative UV damage to the dermal blood vessels and pigment-producing cells of exposed neck and cheek skin, while the shielded skin under the chin is spared, which is why the distribution itself is diagnostic.[^dermnet]

## What actually works

DermNet's own treatment list is worth reading plainly, because it separates what works from what merely helps at the margins. Sun protection, "daily broad-spectrum SPF 50+ sunscreen," and avoiding perfumes on or near the area (including in soap) come first, aimed at preventing further worsening rather than clearing existing marks.[^dermnet] Hydroquinone-containing preparations "may help fade the pigmentation," hydrocortisone cream (a mild topical steroid) is used twice daily for 2 to 4 months, topical retinoids "could be beneficial if used for about a year," and alpha hydroxy acids "can reduce brown pigmentation."[^dermnet] These are modest, slow adjuncts; general pigment-lightening options are covered on [[hyperpigmentation]] and retinoid basics on [[retinoids]]. DermNet is explicit that the treatments actually reported to reduce the telangiectasia and pigmentation are procedural: pulsed dye laser and intense pulsed light (IPL) are called "the best way," and fractional nonablative laser may improve the vascular, pigmented, and textural components together.[^dermnet] Those are in-office procedures outside this site's topical scope. For the surrounding neck and chest skin, gentler versions of the same actives applied more broadly are covered in [[neck-chest-decolletage-care]].

## What's overhyped

No cream clears established poikiloderma of Civatte. Products marketed as fading or erasing the mottling on the neck are, at best, offering the same modest adjuncts DermNet lists, hydroquinone, retinoids, AHAs, and none of them approach what in-office laser or IPL can do for the vessels.[^dermnet] The realistic topical goal is preventing further sun-driven progression, not reversal.

## The Evidence

The clinical description, cause, presentation, and treatment list above are drawn from DermNet's clinical reference page, itself built on a small procedural literature: DermNet's own reference list cites intense pulsed light and pulsed dye laser case series (Goldman & Weiss 2001; Weiss, Goldman & Weiss 2000; Wheeland & Applebaum 1990) as the evidence behind the laser and IPL recommendation, which shows the strongest data here is procedural, not topical.[^dermnet] There is no dedicated randomized-trial evidence behind the topical adjuncts (hydroquinone, retinoids, AHAs) specifically for poikiloderma of Civatte; DermNet presents them as standard dermatologic practice rather than as trial-backed for this condition.

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. The diagnosis of poikiloderma of Civatte is made clinically by a dermatologist, and mottled reddish pigmentation on the neck and chest is not always this benign condition: DermNet lists cutaneous lupus erythematosus, dermatomyositis, Rothmund-Thomson syndrome, Riehl melanosis, and erythromelanosis follicularis faciei et colli among the differential diagnoses, some of which are serious and need medical management.[^dermnet] The interventions with the strongest evidence here, pulsed dye laser and IPL, are in-office procedures, and hydroquinone and topical steroids may require a prescriber depending on where you live. Nothing here should be used to self-diagnose or to delay appropriate medical care.
