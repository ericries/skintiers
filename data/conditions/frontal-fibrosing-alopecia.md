Frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA) is a scarring form of hair loss that pulls the frontal and temple hairline backward over years, most often in women past menopause.[^statpearls] It destroys the hair follicle itself, so the hair it takes does not grow back, and it is a genuine medical disease rather than a skincare concern. This page covers what FFA is and how it is recognized; the treatment that actually affects it is prescription-led and belongs with a dermatologist, not a topical routine.

> FFA is a permanent, scarring hair-loss disease with no fully effective treatment and no controlled-trial evidence behind any of its options.[^statpearls] What limited benefit exists comes from prescription drugs, oral 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, corticosteroid injections, topical calcineurin inhibitors, and antimalarials, not skincare, and they sit outside this site's scope. Because the hairline damage is permanent once scarring sets in, getting to a dermatologist early matters more than what is applied at home.

## How to know you have this

FFA shows up as a band of hairline recession across the front and temples of the scalp. In advanced cases the recessed skin looks pale and smooth, with no visible follicle openings, in contrast to the sun-damaged, hyperpigmented forehead skin just above it.[^statpearls] Scattered single hairs are sometimes left standing inside that band, and the edge of the affected area can show redness or small bumps around the remaining follicles while the disease is active.[^statpearls] Eyebrow loss, partial or complete, affects most patients and can show up before the hairline recession does.[^statpearls] Unlike lichen planopilaris elsewhere on the scalp, itching, burning, and pain are uncommon in FFA.[^statpearls] Without treatment the hairline typically recedes somewhere between about 0.2 and 2 centimeters a year.[^statpearls] FFA is told apart from ordinary female pattern (androgenetic) hair loss, covered on [[hair-loss-thinning]], by scarring and follicle loss at the hairline and by eyebrow involvement; androgenetic thinning spares the eyebrows, does not scar, and leaves fine vellus hairs along the front border, which FFA erases.[^statpearls] A 2026 systematic review of menopause and skin disease found that alopecia, and FFA in particular, showed the strongest and most consistent association with the postmenopausal period of any skin condition it examined, with earlier or surgical menopause linked to greater risk.[^roster]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Only a dermatologist can diagnose FFA, distinguish it from other causes of hairline recession, and confirm it on biopsy where needed. Nothing here should be used to delay evaluation of new or worsening hair loss.

## What actually treats it

The realistic treatment ladder for FFA is prescription-led and sits outside this site's topical-skincare scope. A 2024 clinical review states plainly that "no treatment is fully effective in frontal fibrosing alopecia," and notes that the lack of controlled trials, combined with the disease's own tendency to sometimes stabilize on its own, makes it hard to confirm what is actually working.[^statpearls] Within that limited toolbox, dermatologists use drug classes named here only for orientation, not as a ranked or recommended list: oral 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride), which the same review describes as giving the most promising results for stabilizing the scalp; intralesional or potent topical corticosteroids, which produced eyebrow regrowth in the large majority of patients treated early but are far less reliable on the scalp itself; topical calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus and pimecrolimus; and antimalarials such as hydroxychloroquine, reported as ineffective in several patient series.[^statpearls] None of these is a skincare product, and none of it is within this site's scope; it belongs with a dermatologist, sometimes alongside an endocrinologist.[^statpearls]

Timing matters here more than the choice of any one drug. Because FFA destroys the hair follicle through scarring, damage already done cannot be reversed, only halted, which is why the same clinical review frames early detection and treatment as the way to limit permanent hair loss rather than a way to restore it.[^statpearls] The cause of FFA is still unknown. The same review lists sun exposure and sunscreens, alongside dietary and environmental chemical exposures, medications, and viral infections, as proposed triggers in people who are already genetically predisposed, but it states this as a hypothesis about possible triggers, not as an established cause, and draws no conclusion that sunscreen itself causes the disease.[^statpearls]

## The Evidence

The clinical description, recognition features, epidemiology, and treatment summary on this page come from a 2024 StatPearls clinical review of frontal fibrosing alopecia, a continuing-education reference for clinicians that synthesizes the published case series and trial literature on the condition.[^statpearls] The statement that FFA shows the strongest postmenopausal association of any skin condition studied comes from a 2026 systematic review of 40 studies on menopause and common dermatoses, which also covers other conditions; see [[anti-aging-perimenopause]] for the broader skin-aging picture of that life stage.[^roster]
