Bullous pemphigoid is an autoimmune blistering disease, described in a 2026 meta-analysis as "the most common autoimmune subepidermal bullous disease of the skin".[^dupi] It is a medical condition treated by a dermatologist, not a skincare problem, and it is on this site for one reason: [[dupixent-dupilumab-injection|Dupixent]] now carries an FDA indication for it, which means a drug readers may meet in an eczema context is also licensed for this.[^label]

> **This page will not help you work out whether you have it, and that is deliberate.** Blistering skin has many causes, several of them serious and several of them treated in opposite ways. Bullous pemphigoid is confirmed by a clinician taking a skin biopsy and running immunofluorescence on it. Nothing you can do at home distinguishes it from the conditions it resembles, so the useful thing this page can offer is what the evidence says about treating it once a diagnosis exists.

## How to know you have this

You do not establish this one yourself. New, unexplained blistering on the skin is a reason to see a doctor promptly rather than to start a routine, and the diagnosis rests on laboratory work on a biopsy specimen rather than on appearance alone.

That caution is the substance of this section, not a disclaimer around it. This site grades cosmetic and over-the-counter products, and nothing in that catalogue treats an autoimmune blistering disease. If you are reading this because your skin is blistering, the next step is a clinician.

## What the evidence supports

**A potent topical steroid, not an oral one, is the established first line.** A review in The Journal of Dermatology summarises a large randomized trial as showing that clobetasol propionate, a "super-potent" topical corticosteroid, was associated with "a significant decrease in severe complications and mortality of BP patients" and was "more effective than oral prednisone", and concludes that strategies "should include topical clobetasol propionate as the first line treatment".[^review] That review is from 2003 and this page cites its characterisation of the trial rather than the trial itself, which was not read. The general properties of the drug class are on [[topical-corticosteroids]].

**Dupilumab has an FDA indication and a large single-arm evidence base.** The DUPIXENT label carries bullous pemphigoid in adults as indication 1.8.[^label] A 2026 systematic review pooled 587 patients across 24 studies and reported a complete response rate of 68% (95% CI 60% to 78%) and a disease control rate of 95% (95% CI 92% to 98%), with complete response still 63% (95% CI 49% to 81%) in those taking dupilumab without another systemic drug.[^dupi]

Read those numbers with the design in mind. It is a **single-arm** meta-analysis: every patient in it received dupilumab and there is no control group, so the figures describe what happened to treated patients rather than what dupilumab added over anything else. The review reported 112 adverse events in 97 patients and says most were mild and did not lead to stopping treatment.[^dupi] The drug itself is covered on [[dupilumab]] and [[dupixent-dupilumab-injection]].

## What this site does not grade

There is no tier list on this page. The site grades treatments where it has disease-specific evidence, and for bullous pemphigoid it has that for one drug. A grade earned by an ingredient in a different disease does not transfer: dupilumab holds tier best here on its atopic dermatitis evidence, and that grade says nothing about blistering disease. The other drugs used for this condition have no pages here and are deliberately left ungraded rather than ranked on borrowed evidence.
