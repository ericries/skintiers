A reaction described after prolonged topical corticosteroid use, most often on the face. **Two things are true at once:** the published evidence is seven studies, nearly all case reports, and in 2026 a panel of physicians and patient advocates nonetheless agreed a first set of management points. What topical steroids do in normal use is on [[topical-corticosteroids]].

## How to know you have this

There is no settled answer, and that is the finding rather than an evasion. The most recent systematic review states plainly that "Inconsistent diagnostic criteria, variable definitions, and the primarily descriptive nature of the currently available evidence complicate accurate diagnosis and treatment of TCSW".[^review] The Delphi panel opens from the same place, calling it "a poorly defined condition thought to be related to prolonged use of topical corticosteroids".[^delphi]

What the pooled case material describes is a recognisable picture. Across the seven studies, reported symptoms were burning in 89.8 percent, itching in 85.6 percent and skin hypersensitivity in 82.3 percent, with erythema in 89.6 percent and desquamation in 88.7 percent.[^review] The people described were overwhelmingly adult women, 90 percent, treating atopic dermatitis, 99.5 percent, on the face, 90 percent.[^review]

**Read those percentages for what they are.** They describe who appears in a small set of published case reports, not how common the condition is or who gets it. A case-report series cannot give a prevalence, and the review does not claim one.

## The Evidence

**Seven studies, and the review says what kind.** A systematic search of Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed and the Cochrane Library covering October 2020 to June 2024 found "Seven studies were included in the analysis, including five case reports, one case series, and one qualitative cross-sectional survey, reflecting the limited and descriptive nature of the currently available evidence on TCSW".[^review] There is no trial here to grade, which is why this page carries no tier list.

**A first consensus exists anyway, and it is honest about its own status.** A modified Delphi study put 72 management strategies to an "expert panel of 21 physicians and patient advocates" over two rounds, with consensus defined as at least 70 percent agreement or disagreement.[^delphi] Twelve outcomes reached it, "primarily focused on cessation of topical and systemic corticosteroids and psychosocial support", and the panel also "met consensus for dupilumab as a first-line systemic treatment".[^delphi] The authors describe the result as "initial guidelines for the management and treatment of TSW until further therapies can be investigated for future evidence-based care".[^delphi]

**Why there is no tier list on this page.** Two of the three consensus pillars are not interventions this site grades: stopping a drug, and psychological support. The third, [[dupilumab]], has its own page and its own evidence, none of which was generated in TSW. Grading a ladder on that basis would invent precision the literature does not have.

**The contested part, stated as contested.** The review's own subtitle asks whether TSW is "Steroid Addiction or Adverse Drug Effect?" and it is not answered.[^review] The same paper notes that steroid phobia "has been gaining attention on social media platforms" and may affect whether people use prescribed treatment.[^review] Both of those can be true alongside patients having a real and severe reaction; nothing in the evidence read here settles the mechanism, and a page that pretended otherwise would be guessing.
