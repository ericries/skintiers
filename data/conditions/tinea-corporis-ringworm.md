Ringworm of the body is a dermatophyte infection of the skin, and the name is a leftover from before anyone knew what caused it. A 2025 review co-authored by the CDC's Mycotic Diseases Branch puts it plainly: "Tinea infections (often called ringworm) are caused by dermatophyte fungi and classified by the body site involved".[^afp] The foot version is on [[athletes-foot]] and the scalp version, which behaves very differently, on [[tinea-capitis]].

> **The hard part is not the treatment, it is being sure that is what you have.** The same review warns that "Clinical diagnosis without testing may be unreliable because other conditions can resemble tinea infections", and the example it gives first is that tinea corporis can be confused with eczema.[^afp] That matters because the two are treated in opposite directions, and because the most common way people make ringworm worse is reaching for a steroid cream.

## How to know you have this

The classic description is specific enough to be useful. A 2020 review describes it as "a well-demarcated, sharply circumscribed, oval or circular, mildly erythematous, scaly patch or plaque with a raised leading edge", adding that "Mild pruritus is common".[^leung] The raised leading edge with relative central clearing is the feature that gives the ring its name, and it is where a clinician takes a sample.

**Certainty needs a test, not a look.** The 2020 review notes that diagnosis "can be difficult with prior use of medications, such as calcineurin inhibitors or corticosteroids", that it can be confirmed "by microscopic examination of potassium hydroxide wet-mount preparations of skin scrapings from the active border of the lesion", and that "Fungal culture is the gold standard".[^leung] If an OTC antifungal has not worked, the next step is a clinician rather than a stronger cream.

## The Evidence

**Topicals are the standard treatment.** "The standard treatment of tinea corporis is with topical antifungals", and the 2025 review says tinea corporis, tinea cruris and tinea pedis "generally respond to inexpensive topical antifungal agents".[^leung][^afp] Oral treatment is reserved: the 2025 review lists "extensive disease, lack of response to topical treatment, immunocompromise, or hair follicle involvement".[^afp]

**Do not use a combined antifungal and steroid.** The 2025 review is explicit that "Antifungal stewardship, including avoiding the use of combination antifungal-corticosteroids, should be emphasized to optimize outcomes and help prevent resistance".[^afp] A steroid calms the redness and itch while the infection continues underneath, which is why a plain [[topical-corticosteroids]] cream on suspected ringworm is a bad idea.

**Resistant tinea is now a real category.** The review records that "Emerging tinea infections may be more severe than classic tinea infections and generally do not improve with first-line topical or oral antifungals", and may need "prolonged oral antifungal therapy and specialized diagnostic testing".[^afp] This is the clearest reason not to keep self-treating a rash that is not responding.

**A gap worth stating about this site's own evidence.** The pooled randomised evidence quoted across the antifungal pages here, the Crawford Cochrane review, covers the skin and nails of the **foot**. The ringworm indications below come from the product labels and from the reviews cited on this page, not from that pooled trial data. Where an OTC label claims to cure ringworm, that claim rests on its regulatory status, which is set out on [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Scalp involvement needs oral treatment and a prescriber, and a rash that is spreading, not improving, or on a child's head should be seen rather than self-treated.
