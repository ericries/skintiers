## How to know you have this

Darkening of the skin in the armpit, usually brown to grey-brown, developing gradually rather than appearing overnight. It is a cosmetic complaint rather than a disease, and the broader mechanism is at [[hyperpigmentation]] and [[post-inflammatory-hyperpigmentation]], which this page does not restate.

The thing worth ruling out first is **acanthosis nigricans**, which also darkens the armpit but is velvety and thickened rather than flat, and which can be a marker of insulin resistance or other systemic disease. That is a question for a doctor, not a serum, and this page makes no attempt to help you tell them apart.

> **The entire published literature is ten studies.** A 2025 systematic review searched PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Web of Science and Embase and found "Ten studies on AH", which it sorted into "topical treatments, light/laser-based therapies, and comparative approaches".[^review] Its own framing of the problem is that axillary hyperpigmentation "is a common dermatologic complaint, with no standard treatment."[^review] Nothing on the tier list above rests on more than a few trials.

## The Evidence

One systematic review, read from its abstract, and it is candid. On topicals it reports that "niacinamide, desonide, sweet orange extract, Perilla frutescens leaf extract, Cyperus rotundus oil, glycolic acid, and novel serums demonstrated varying degrees of pigmentation reduction, with desonide and C. rotundus oil showing significant effectiveness and minimal side effects."[^review]

On devices it reports that Q-switched Nd:YAG laser "was effective across three studies", that both it and intense pulsed light "showed significant improvement with minimal adverse events; though pain was more reported with the laser", and that one comparative study "found IPL superior to alpha-hydroxy acid in skin lightening and texture improvement."[^review]

No pooled effect size, no confidence interval and no number of participants appears anywhere in the abstract, which is why the ladder above ranks the strength of the review's wording rather than a measured result.

**What is missing is as informative as what is there.** Two of the treatments the review found most effective, desonide and Perilla frutescens, have no page on this site, and neither does Q-switched Nd:YAG. Desonide is a topical corticosteroid, so the obvious question about long-term use on thin axillary skin is one the abstract does not address.
