Hyperpigmentation is a darkening of the skin caused by excess melanin, and its common facial forms are melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and pigmentation driven by sun exposure.[^handel][^davis] The color change is usually the only symptom, the skin stays flat and is neither itchy nor sore, and the same person can have more than one form at once. Among the topical options with controlled evidence, photoprotection is the foundation with the strongest support, the depigmenting creams are real but modest, and without ongoing sun protection the pigment tends to come back.[^liu][^castanedo][^delrosario]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Most hyperpigmentation is benign, but a new, changing, or single dark spot can be something other than melasma or a sun spot, so a board-certified dermatologist is the person who can tell them apart. Several of the treatments with the best evidence are prescription drugs or in-office procedures: the fixed triple-combination cream with hydroquinone, oral tranexamic acid, prescription retinoids, chemical peels, and lasers.[^liu][^delrosario] They require a prescriber or a procedure. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians use to recognize the common forms of hyperpigmentation. They are for recognition, not self-diagnosis: the patterns overlap, they can hide other conditions, and only a clinician can make the diagnosis, often with a Wood's lamp (a near-ultraviolet lamp of 340 to 400 nm) or dermoscopy to judge how deep the pigment sits.[^handel]

- **Melasma looks symmetric and blotchy.** The clinical picture is described as "irregular brown macules symmetrically distributed on sun-exposed areas," most often on the face.[^handel] The patches are flat, tan to grey-brown, and tend to be roughly mirror-image on both sides. They cluster in two patterns: a central pattern across the forehead, nose, cheeks, upper lip, and chin, and a peripheral pattern along the cheekbones and jawline.[^handel] Melasma "affects mainly women," especially during the reproductive years, and more often those with medium-to-deep skin tones (Fitzpatrick skin types III to V).[^handel] It commonly starts or worsens in pregnancy or on hormonal contraception, and it darkens in summer and fades in winter.[^handel]
- **Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation follows an earlier flare.** It is "a common sequelae of inflammatory dermatoses," meaning flat dark marks that appear where a previous spot healed: an acne pimple, a bug bite, eczema, a burn, or a scratch.[^davis] The tell is that each mark matches the site and shape of the thing that came before it, rather than forming a symmetric pattern of its own. It "tends to affect darker skinned patients with greater frequency and severity," and in deeper skin tones it can last months to years.[^davis]
- **Sun-driven pigmentation tracks where the light lands.** Ultraviolet exposure "directly induces the increase of melanogenic activity," so chronically exposed skin darkens overall and discrete brown spots build up on the face and the backs of the hands.[^handel] The same mechanism is why melasma flares after sun and why treated pigment relapses without daily photoprotection.[^handel][^castanedo]
- **How to tell the forms apart.** Melasma is symmetric and has no single preceding injury; post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is patchy and always traces back to an earlier lesion at that exact spot.[^handel][^davis] All three are flat: if an area is raised, scaly, itchy, growing, or bleeding, that points away from ordinary hyperpigmentation and toward something a clinician should examine.[^handel]

## What It Is and Who Gets It

Pigment problems are one of the most common reasons people see a dermatologist, and the burden falls hardest on skin of color. A 2006 Brazilian Society of Dermatology survey of 57,343 diagnoses found that pigmentary disorders (grouped as melanodermias, among them melasma) were the third largest group of complaints, at 8.4% of all diagnoses, ranging from 5.9% to 9.1% across regions.[^handel] In a 2010 Brazilian population survey of 1,500 adults, pigmentation disorders were the leading reason people sought dermatological care, cited by 23.6% of men and 29.9% of women.[^handel] For darker-skinned patients specifically, dyschromias including post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation are among the most common reasons they seek a dermatologist's care.[^davis]

Melasma has a strong constitutional streak. It is "a chronic acquired hypermelanosis of the skin" that concentrates in women during their reproductive years and in medium-to-deep skin tones, and more than 40% of patients report a relative with the same condition, pointing to genetic predisposition.[^handel] Its recognized triggers are sun exposure, pregnancy and sex hormones, skin inflammation, some cosmetics, and photosensitizing drugs.[^handel] Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is different in origin: it is a consequence of inflammation or injury rather than a standalone disease, which is why acne, eczema, and even aggressive cosmetic procedures are among its common precipitants in pigment-prone skin.[^davis]

## What Is Happening in the Skin

All three forms share a final common step: melanocytes deposit more melanin than normal, either in the upper skin (epidermal pigment) or deeper in the dermis, and Wood's-lamp examination is used to estimate which.[^handel] Melasma that stands out more sharply under Wood's light is largely epidermal and tends to respond better to topical treatment; pigment sitting deeper responds less.[^handel]

- **Ultraviolet light drives melanin production.** UV radiation "directly induces the increase of melanogenic activity," and in melasma this happens more intensely in the affected patches than in the neighboring skin, which is why the condition worsens in summer and improves in winter.[^handel]
- **Visible light matters beyond UV.** A double-blind randomized trial found that adding an iron-oxide pigment (which absorbs visible light) to a broad-spectrum UV sunscreen improved melasma more than the UV-only sunscreen, evidence the authors read as "a role for VL in melasma pathogenesis."[^castanedo]
- **Melasma behaves like photoaged skin.** Beyond the extra pigment, the current view is that "melasma might be a photoaging skin disorder affecting genetically predisposed individuals," with hormonal stimulation on a genetic background as long-recognized contributors and added factors such as vascular and dermal changes.[^passeron][^handel]
- **Post-inflammatory pigment is the aftermath of injury.** When the skin is inflamed or injured, melanocytes can be stimulated to overproduce and disperse melanin, leaving a flat stain once the original lesion clears; this happens more readily and lasts longer in darker skin.[^davis]

## What Actually Works

Graded against controlled evidence, and framed relative to named comparators. The efficacy anchor is a network meta-analysis of 59 randomized trials that ranked 14 melasma therapies on the Melasma Area and Severity Index (MASI); it is a ranking with real uncertainty rather than a precise scoreboard.[^liu] The health-leading step is photoprotection, because it both treats and prevents relapse; the depigmenting agents work on top of it.

**1. Photoprotection, including visible-light coverage. Effect: notable (foundational) · Evidence: solid.** Every melasma trial is run on a base of daily sunscreen, and the quality of that sun protection changes the result. In a double-blind randomized trial, 68 melasma patients (61 completed) all used 4% hydroquinone, and the group whose SPF 50+ sunscreen also contained iron oxide to block visible light showed 15% greater MASI improvement, 28% greater colorimetric improvement, and 4% greater improvement on measured melanin than the group using a UV-only sunscreen at 8 weeks; the authors concluded "UV-VL sunscreen enhances the depigmenting efficacy of hydroquinone."[^castanedo] Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is also one of the few skincare practices with randomized-trial support against hard clinical endpoints; learn more on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

**2. Hydroquinone and the fixed triple-combination cream. Effect: modest (monotherapy) to notable (triple combination) · Evidence: solid.** Hydroquinone is the long-standing reference depigmenting drug. In the network meta-analysis it beat placebo but ranked low as monotherapy (13th of 14), while the prescription fixed triple-combination cream (hydroquinone with a retinoid and a corticosteroid) was rated "the most favorable selection among the topical drugs for melasma."[^liu] Hydroquinone is the reference depigmenting drug, strongest as that FDA-approved triple combination and restricted to short-term use; learn more on [[hydroquinone]].

**3. Azelaic acid. Effect: modest to notable · Evidence: solid.** In the ranking it placed above hydroquinone monotherapy, and head-to-head evidence puts it ahead of hydroquinone 2% and roughly level with the stronger 4%.[^liu] Azelaic acid is a well-tolerated depigmenting agent available over the counter and by prescription; learn more on [[azelaic-acid]].

**4. Tranexamic acid. Effect: modest to notable, and durable only while taken · Evidence: solid (oral).** A double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in moderate-to-severe melasma found oral tranexamic acid cut the modified MASI by 49% versus 18% for placebo at 3 months, and both topical and oral forms ranked above hydroquinone in the network meta-analysis.[^delrosario][^liu] The benefit largely relapses after stopping. Oral tranexamic acid is one of the better-evidenced melasma options, though its skin use is off-label; learn more on [[tranexamic-acid]].

**5. Topical vitamin C and niacinamide. Effect: modest · Evidence: mixed.** Topical vitamin C ranked above hydroquinone in the melasma network meta-analysis.[^liu] Topical vitamin C is a well-characterized antioxidant with modest evidence for brightening and for supporting sun protection; learn more on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]. Niacinamide is well tolerated with modest evidence for lightening facial hyperpigmentation; learn more on [[niacinamide]].

**6. Topical retinoids. Effect: modest · Evidence: solid.** Tretinoin ranked above hydroquinone monotherapy in the network meta-analysis and is the retinoid inside the top-ranked triple-combination cream.[^liu] Tretinoin is the trial-validated benchmark retinoid and improves melasma on its own; learn more on [[tretinoin]].

**For post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation specifically,** the review by Davis and Callender describes first-line treatment as "topical depigmenting agents in addition to photoprotection including a sunscreen," with tyrosinase inhibitors such as hydroquinone, azelaic acid, kojic acid, arbutin, and licorice extracts able to "effectively lighten areas of hypermelanosis," alongside retinoids, ascorbic acid, and niacinamide; it also stresses treating the original inflammatory condition first.[^davis]

## What's Overhyped

- **"Natural" lightening with lemon or other citrus.** There is no controlled evidence that citrus juice fades pigment, and it carries a specific hazard that is self-defeating here: citrus contains furocoumarins that cause phytophotodermatitis, a phototoxic reaction whose blistering heals into exactly the kind of hyperpigmentation the person was trying to remove.[^phyto]
- **Hydroquinone as the strongest possible fix.** In the 59-trial network meta-analysis, hydroquinone monotherapy ranked 13th of 14 melasma treatments for efficacy.[^liu] It is the long-used reference agent, not the most effective option, and its full grade and its safety limits are on [[hydroquinone]].
- **Scrubs and aggressive exfoliation to "buff away" dark spots.** In pigment-prone skin, irritation is itself a trigger for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and the Davis and Callender review cautions to "use caution with all of the above treatments to prevent irritation and worsening of postinflammatory hyperpigmentation."[^davis] A harsh physical scrub adds inflammation rather than removing pigment.
- **A cream that "erases it permanently."** Melasma is chronic and sun-sensitive: it "improves in the winter and worsens in the summer," and even effective drugs relapse without ongoing photoprotection.[^handel] In the oral tranexamic acid trial the 49% pigment reduction on treatment had fallen back toward placebo three months after stopping.[^delrosario]

## The Evidence

**The ranking anchor: Liu 2021 network meta-analysis.** A systematic review and network meta-analysis pooled 59 randomized controlled trials using MASI and ranked 14 melasma therapies against placebo.[^liu] Ranked above hydroquinone monotherapy were the Q-switched Nd:YAG laser, intense pulsed light, ablative fractional laser, the triple-combination cream, topical vitamin C, oral tranexamic acid, chemical peeling, azelaic acid, microneedling, topical tranexamic acid, tretinoin, and the picosecond laser; hydroquinone placed 13th of 14 for efficacy while having one of the lower side-effect rates (18.2%). The authors concluded the triple-combination cream "was found to be the most favorable selection among the topical drugs for melasma." The certainty limits matter: this is a broad network of heterogeneous trials, so the fine ordering among the topicals is soft.[^liu]

> The prescription triple-combination cream ranks as the strongest topical; single agents including azelaic acid, tranexamic acid, vitamin C, and tretinoin all outrank hydroquinone alone, which beats placebo but sits near the bottom.[^liu]

**Photoprotection is active treatment, not background alone: Castanedo-Cazares 2014.** A double-blind randomized trial assigned 68 melasma patients (61 completed) to an SPF 50+ sunscreen either with iron oxide (absorbing visible light) or UV-only, on top of 4% hydroquinone for all, over 8 weeks.[^castanedo] The visible-light-absorbing group showed 15%, 28%, and 4% greater improvements than the UV-only group on MASI, colorimetry, and melanin assessments. The trial is small and short, but it isolates one variable and shows sun protection quality changes the outcome.

> Adding visible-light protection to a UV sunscreen improved melasma more than UV protection alone, evidence that sunscreen choice is part of the treatment itself, not prevention alone.[^castanedo]

**Oral tranexamic acid: Del Rosario 2018.** A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in moderate-to-severe melasma found oral tranexamic acid reduced the modified MASI by 49% versus 18% for placebo at 3 months.[^delrosario] Three months after treatment stopped, the reductions had converged toward 26% versus 19%, showing the effect depends on continued treatment.

> Oral tranexamic acid roughly halved melasma severity while taken, but much of the gain was lost within three months of stopping.[^delrosario]

**Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation: Davis and Callender 2010.** This review of PIH in skin of color describes the condition as "a common sequelae of inflammatory dermatoses" that "tends to affect darker skinned patients with greater frequency and severity," sets first-line treatment as topical depigmenting agents plus photoprotection, and emphasizes managing the initial inflammatory condition and avoiding treatment-induced irritation.[^davis]

**Pathophysiology: Handel 2014 and Passeron 2018.** The clinical and epidemiological review establishes melasma's presentation, its concentration in women with medium-to-deep skin tones, its genetic loading, and UV's direct stimulation of melanin.[^handel] The Passeron and Picardo synthesis reframes melasma as a photoaging disorder in genetically predisposed people, integrating hormonal, vascular, and dermal contributors beyond the melanocyte alone.[^passeron]

Across these, the practical convergence is consistent: sun exposure (UV and visible light) drives the pigment, photoprotection is the one step that both treats and prevents relapse, and the depigmenting drugs produce modest gains that fade if the light exposure and, for melasma, the underlying tendency are not managed.[^liu][^castanedo][^delrosario][^handel]
