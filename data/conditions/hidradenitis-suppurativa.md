Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory disease, not an acne variant or a hygiene problem, that produces painful, recurrent nodules, abscesses, and sinus tracts (tunnels under the skin) in areas that carry apocrine sweat glands: the armpits, groin, genital and perineal skin, under the breasts, and the buttocks.[^nesbitt][^koumaki] It is a genuine medical disease, and it is primarily managed with systemic drugs and surgery, not skincare. This page covers only the narrow, limited role topical treatment plays for mild disease; it is not a substitute for a dermatologist.

> HS is managed medically and surgically. Systemic antibiotics, hormonal therapy, and biologics such as adalimumab and secukinumab, plus procedures from incision and drainage to deroofing and wide excision, are the real mainstays and sit outside this site's topical-skincare scope. Topical treatment only helps at the mild end of the disease, and even there the evidence is a single old trial. If you think you have HS, see a dermatologist.

## How to know you have this

HS is diagnosed clinically, not by a skincare routine's response to it. Features that support the diagnosis include typical deep-seated, inflamed nodules or sinus tracts in the typical locations (axilla, inguinal, genital, perineal, and inframammary regions, often on both sides of the body), plus a chronic, relapsing course.[^nesbitt] Disease severity is commonly staged with the Hurley system: Stage I is abscess formation without sinus tracts or scarring, Stage II is recurrent abscesses with sinus tracts and scarring, and Stage III is diffuse involvement with multiple interconnected sinus tracts across an entire area.[^nesbitt]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. HS is a clinical diagnosis and its management, especially beyond mild disease, requires a dermatologist. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## What actually treats it

The real treatment ladder for HS sits mostly outside topical skincare. A 2026 review of medical and surgical management concludes that "HS is best managed by matching treatment to disease phenotype," with "medical therapy... essential for inflammatory control" and "surgery... essential for persistent tunnels, fibrosis, and scarred regional disease," and describes "the strongest overall clinical position" as "an integrated, multidisciplinary model in which systemic therapy reduces inflammatory load and surgery definitively treats irreversible tissue damage."[^frew] On the medical side, that means oral antibiotics (commonly doxycycline, or clindamycin combined with rifampin for non-responders), hormonal therapy, and biologics; the same review reports trial evidence for adalimumab (PIONEER trials), secukinumab (SUNSHINE/SUNRISE), and bimekizumab (BE HEARD I/II) in moderate-to-severe disease.[^frew][^nesbitt] On the surgical side, wide excision has the strongest evidence for structurally advanced disease, with meta-analytic data showing lower recurrence than after local excision or incision and drainage alone.[^frew] None of that is within this site's scope; it belongs with a dermatologist or HS specialist.

Topical treatment is reserved for mild, localized, predominantly inflammatory disease.[^frew] The only topical with actual randomized-controlled-trial evidence in HS is [[clindamycin-topical]]: a 1983 double-blind trial randomized 30 patients with recurrent HS to clindamycin 1% solution or placebo, and for every outcome measured (numbers of abscesses, inflammatory nodules, and pustules), "clindamycin 1% solution was significantly superior to placebo" (p < .01).[^clemmensen][^nesbitt] That trial is small, dated, and specific to mild, inflammatory-abscess-stage disease, but a 2019 clinician's guide still describes topical clindamycin as "often the first-line therapy for mild HS."[^nesbitt] Antibiotic resistance is a documented, ongoing concern in HS bacterial management generally, which is part of why clindamycin is used during flares rather than as an indefinite standalone routine.[^amr] Antiseptic hygiene washes such as [[benzoyl-peroxide]] are sometimes used alongside treatment to reduce surface bacterial load, but they carry no dedicated HS trial evidence of their own, only their general antibacterial profile. A third topical, resorcinol 15% peel, also appears in the mild-disease literature as an alternative to clindamycin, but it does not have a page on this site.[^nesbitt]

## The Evidence

The clinical definition, typical lesion sites, and Hurley staging come from a 2019 clinician's guide to HS therapy, which also documents the Clemmensen 1983 double-blind trial of topical clindamycin, the only randomized-controlled-trial evidence behind any topical HS treatment on this page.[^nesbitt][^clemmensen] The statement that medical therapy and surgery, not topicals, are the real treatment ladder, along with the trial evidence for adalimumab, secukinumab, and bimekizumab and the recurrence data for wide excision, comes from a 2026 narrative review of medical and surgical HS management that searched PubMed/MEDLINE, the Cochrane Library, and major dermatology guidelines through May 2026.[^frew] The general statement about ongoing antibiotic resistance concerns in HS bacterial management comes from a 2024 cross-sectional study of antimicrobial resistance patterns in HS lesions.[^koumaki][^amr]
