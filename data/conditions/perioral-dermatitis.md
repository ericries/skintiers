Perioral dermatitis is a common facial rash of small, red, sometimes scaly papules and pustules clustered around the mouth, and often the eyes and nose too, where it is also called periorificial dermatitis.[^statpearls] It affects young adult women most often, typically between ages 20 and 45, though it also occurs in children.[^statpearls] The rash characteristically spares a thin rim of skin right at the vermilion border of the lips.[^statpearls] It is distinct from the clogged-follicle comedones that define [[acne]] and from the persistent central-facial flushing of [[rosacea]], though it shares several of rosacea's topical treatments. It is benign but tends to recur.[^statpearls]

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians look for. They are for recognition, not self-diagnosis: perioral dermatitis is diagnosed clinically, and only a clinician can make that call.[^statpearls]

- **Small red bumps clustered around the mouth, eyes, or nose.** StatPearls describes "characteristic erythematous grouped papules that are often bilateral but may be unilateral, surrounding the mouth, eyes, and nose," sometimes alongside scaling, vesicles, or pustules.[^statpearls]
- **A clear strip of unaffected skin right at the lip line.** "The vermilion borders of the lips are typically spared of involvement," a pattern that helps separate it from other facial rashes.[^statpearls]
- **Burning or sensitivity more than itch.** Patients "often report that the areas of involvement are associated with symptoms of burning or sensitivity, although pruritus can also occur."[^statpearls]
- **A history of topical steroid use on the face.** "For many patients, an association between topical corticosteroid use and perioral dermatitis is often noted," and chronic use raises the risk of a more severe rash.[^statpearls]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. StatPearls lists rosacea, acne vulgaris, seborrheic dermatitis, allergic contact dermatitis, and Demodex folliculitis among the look-alikes clinicians rule out, and recommends a skin biopsy when the presentation is atypical or does not respond to treatment.[^statpearls] Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## Management

Topical corticosteroid use on the face commonly precedes the rash, and StatPearls calls discontinuing it "a primary recommendation for treatment," while warning that "abrupt cessation of corticosteroids can cause rebound flaring; therefore, a gradual taper may be necessary."[^statpearls] Clinicians are also advised to have patients avoid other likely irritants: StatPearls instructs that they "must emphasize discontinuing all topical steroids and avoiding other potential irritants such as fluorinated toothpaste, heavy cosmetics, and unnecessary skincare products."[^statpearls] In some patients the rash resolves once the offending product is stopped; in others it becomes a chronic, relapsing condition that needs ongoing treatment.[^statpearls]

The drug options graded above are used on top of that step, not in place of it. A 2014 evidence-based review rated oral tetracycline as having "the best valid evidence," and topical metronidazole, erythromycin, and pimecrolimus as further "effective treatment choices with good evidence," while noting that topical corticosteroids themselves can improve the picture short-term, so "the question of whether it is a good treatment or a cause remains unanswered."[^tempark] A more recent 2022 systematic review that pooled 11 randomized trials (733 participants) and graded each finding with the GRADE framework reached a more cautious conclusion, that "the body of evidence to inform treatment of POD currently consists of low and very low certainty evidence for important outcomes," across every intervention it assessed.[^gray] Topical corticosteroid misuse itself carries the strongest evidence as a cause: a separate systematic review "found the strongest evidence to support topical corticosteroid misuse as the principal causative factor in the pathogenesis of perioral dermatitis," while noting further research is still needed on the treatment side, including tetracyclines, topical metronidazole, and topical azelaic acid.[^searle]

## The Evidence

The clinical picture, epidemiology, and causal role of topical corticosteroids come from the StatPearls chapter.[^statpearls] The treatment evidence is thin and comes from two reviews that disagree on how strong it is: a 2014 narrative review graded oral tetracycline and several topicals as having good evidence using US Preventive Services Task Force criteria,[^tempark] while a 2022 GRADE systematic review of the same drug classes, working from 11 randomized trials, found only low- or very-low-certainty evidence for every outcome it assessed, including a null finding for azelaic acid gel and a non-significant confidence interval for pimecrolimus.[^gray] A third systematic review converged on topical corticosteroid misuse as the best-evidenced cause, while flagging the treatment literature itself as still needing more research.[^searle]
