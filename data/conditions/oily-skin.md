Oily skin (seborrhea) is the everyday name for skin that produces more sebum than average, which Sakuma and Maibach describe as oversized sebaceous glands that "produce excessive amounts of sebum giving the appearance of shiny and greasy skin."[^sakuma] It is a skin type and a cosmetic complaint rather than a disease, though the same excess sebum feeds acne and seborrhoeic dermatitis. There are two things people mean by "controlling oil": actually reducing how much sebum the glands make (a change in skin biology), and mopping up or hiding the oil already on the surface (appearance only). Only one intervention, oral isotretinoin, durably lowers production, and it is a prescription drug used for acne, not for oiliness alone; almost everything sold for oily skin works on the surface.[^landthaler][^endly][^draelos]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Oily skin itself is not dangerous, but persistent oiliness with inflammatory bumps can be acne, and greasy, flaking, itchy patches on the scalp, brows, or sides of the nose can be seborrhoeic dermatitis, both of which a board-certified dermatologist can evaluate and treat. Several of the interventions named below (oral isotretinoin, spironolactone, and combined oral contraceptives) are prescription drugs used off-label or on-label for related conditions; they require a prescriber, carry their own risks and monitoring, and are named here so readers know they exist. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians and cosmetic scientists associate with oily skin. They describe a recognizable pattern, not a diagnosis, and only a clinician can confirm what is behind any one person's skin.

- **Midday shine and a greasy feel, worst in the T-zone.** Sebum pools fastest on the forehead, nose, and chin (the "T-zone"), where sebaceous glands are largest and densest, so skin that looked matte in the morning turns shiny and feels slick there by afternoon.[^sakuma][^endly]
- **Pores that look larger.** Endly and Miller's review links oily skin to "larger facial pores and an 'unclean' or 'greasy' appearance," because a pore filled and stretched by sebum reads as bigger.[^endly]
- **Makeup and sunscreen that slide or break up.** Surface oil loosens products applied over it, a practical sign people notice long before any measurement.[^endly]
- **The blotting-paper test.** Pressing a tissue or blotting paper to the T-zone and lifting away visible oil is the informal version of what dermatologists measure with instruments; the Sebumeter and similar devices put a number on the same surface sebum.[^endly]
- **A measurable threshold.** Endly and Miller report that when "sebum production exceeds 1.5mg/10cm² every three hours, it is considered excessive" and defines seborrhea, roughly one and a half times the normal adult rate.[^endly]

Oiliness is a spectrum, it changes with heat, humidity, hormones, and age, and the same face is often oily in the T-zone and normal or dry on the cheeks ("combination" skin).[^sakuma] None of these signs on its own means a medical problem; they describe a skin type, and persistent bumps, flaking, or irritation alongside the oil are what warrant a clinician's assessment.

## What Oily Skin Is

Sebum is the oily secretion of the sebaceous glands, which sit alongside hair follicles across the face and scalp and empty their contents (an entire cell's worth of lipids at a time) into the pore. Sakuma and Maibach's overview frames oily skin as the visible result when those glands are oversized and overproductive.[^sakuma] Sebum coats the skin surface, and while excess is a cosmetic nuisance, the same lipids contribute to the skin's water barrier, which is one reason aggressively removing all surface oil has trade-offs.[^sakuma]

The main driver of sebum output is hormonal. Sebaceous glands carry androgen receptors, and androgens (testosterone and its more potent skin metabolite dihydrotestosterone) stimulate the sebocytes to proliferate and make lipids, which is why oiliness rises at puberty and why men, with higher testosterone, tend to have higher sebum output than women.[^endly][^zouboulis] The acne literature calls this androgen-driven excess "hyperseborrhoea (that is, increased sebum production)," one of the core mechanisms Zouboulis and colleagues place at the start of acne.[^zouboulis] On top of hormones, Sakuma and Maibach note that skin oiliness varies with genetics, age (it falls after the sebaceous glands' peak in early adulthood and drops further after menopause), sex, ethnicity, and hot, humid climates, which raise sebum output and the sensation of greasiness.[^sakuma]

That biology sets up the honest dividing line for everything that follows. Sebum production is a continuous, hormonally governed process happening below the surface; wiping, washing, or powdering the face changes what is on top of the skin without touching the glands underneath.[^sakuma][^draelos]

## What Reduces Sebum Production (health effects)

These interventions act on the glands or the hormones that drive them, so they change how much sebum the skin actually makes. Most are prescription drugs used for acne or hormonal conditions rather than for oiliness on its own, and they are named here, not recommended, because the decision and the risks belong with a prescriber.

**Oral isotretinoin. Effect: strong and durable. Evidence: solid (but it is an acne drug, not an oiliness drug).** Isotretinoin is the one intervention shown to shrink the sebaceous glands themselves. Landthaler and colleagues measured "a marked decrease of size of sebaceous glands of up to 90%" after 12 weeks of treatment, and Endly and Miller summarize that "sebum production decreases by 90 percent during oral isotretinoin therapy," with the effect outlasting the course: "sebum excretion rates have been found to remain significantly suppressed" a year after stopping.[^landthaler][^endly] It is a systemic retinoid approved for severe acne, is a potent teratogen requiring pregnancy prevention and monitoring, and is not a cosmetic treatment for shine. Named for completeness; graded, where in scope, on [[acne]].

**Hormonal and anti-androgen therapy (spironolactone, combined oral contraceptives). Effect: notable in appropriate patients. Evidence: moderate.** Because androgens drive sebum, blocking them lowers output. In a double-blind trial, oral spironolactone (50 to 200 mg daily) "reduced sebum excretion in all female subjects," with a dose-dependent clinical response.[^goodfellow] Combined oral contraceptives lower sebum by suppressing ovarian androgens; Endly and Miller's review reports one study finding a 60% relative reduction on the cheeks and 30% on the forehead after six cycles.[^endly] These are prescription options for appropriate patients (typically women), with their own risks, and are used for acne and hormonal signs rather than for oiliness alone.

**Topical retinoids. Effect: modest and mainly on pores, not clearly on production. Evidence: mixed.** In the lab, retinoids "significantly reduce sebocyte proliferation, differentiation, and synthesis of sebum," but Endly and Miller are candid that clinical "evidence has yet to exclusively illustrate that topical retinoids are sebosuppressive."[^endly] What is better documented is a smaller effect on the look of pores: they report 42% of subjects on tazarotene once daily for 24 weeks achieved a reduction in pore size.[^endly] Topical vitamin A derivatives are well evidenced for acne and photoaging; that broader case is on [[retinoids]], and [[differin-adapalene-0-1-gel]] is a nonprescription example.

**Niacinamide. Effect: modest, mostly on surface oil. Evidence: preliminary.** In Draelos and colleagues' trials, a 2% niacinamide moisturizer "demonstrated significantly lowered SER" (sebum excretion rate) after two and four weeks in Japanese subjects, while in a separate Caucasian group casual sebum on the surface fell but the excretion rate did not.[^draelos] So the signal is real but small and inconsistent across populations, and it sits below the hormonal and isotretinoin evidence. Niacinamide is well tolerated and its full evidence base is on [[niacinamide]]; [[the-ordinary-niacinamide-10-zinc-1]] is a common serum form.

**Azelaic acid and salicylic acid (BHA). Effect: modest, on acne and pores rather than on production. Evidence: mixed.** Neither is established as reducing sebum output. Salicylic acid is oil-soluble, so it penetrates the sebum-filled pore and acts as a keratolytic; it is a legitimate second-tier acne active that the American Academy of Dermatology recommends conditionally, covered on [[salicylic-acid]] ([[paulas-choice-skin-perfecting-2-bha]] is a widely used version). Azelaic acid is a well-tolerated dicarboxylic acid with strong evidence for inflammatory acne and rosacea rather than for oiliness, graded use by use on [[azelaic-acid]].

## Surface Oil and Shine (cosmetic effects)

These products change how the skin looks and feels without changing what the glands produce. Draelos and colleagues make the distinction plainly: most sebum-control products work by "absorbing sebum from the face rather than modulating its production."[^draelos] That makes them a matte finish that lasts until the glands refill the surface, which is a legitimate cosmetic effect, not a treatment for the underlying oiliness.

- **Sebum-absorbing powders (silica, starch, and "no-sebum" mineral powders).** Finely milled silica and starch soak up surface oil and scatter light for a matte look. They absorb what is already there and do nothing to production.[^draelos]
- **Clay and kaolin masks.** Clays adsorb surface oil and give a temporary tightened, matte result; the effect is cosmetic and short-lived.[^draelos]
- **Blotting papers.** They lift visible oil off the T-zone on the spot, the same removal the blotting-paper test uses to detect oiliness, with no effect below the surface.[^endly]
- **Mattifying primers and oil-free or gel moisturizers.** These add a matte or oil-free layer and avoid depositing more oil, again altering finish rather than sebum output.[^draelos]

## Korean Sebum-Control Approaches

K-beauty routines aimed at oily and combination skin lean on light textures and a set of botanical and vitamin ingredients marketed for "sebum control." The honest picture is that most of the effect demonstrated for these is on surface sebum, in short trials, rather than on gland output.

- **Niacinamide serums** are a mainstay, resting on the same modest, preliminary sebum-excretion data described above.[^draelos]
- **Green tea (its catechin EGCG) and L-carnitine.** These appear in "anti-sebum" creams and toners and have the best controlled Korean-style data among the botanicals. In a randomized trial of 90 people with seborrhea, creams containing 5% EGCG, 2% L-carnitine, or both cut sebum from baseline significantly ("mean sebum reduction from baseline was statistically significant in all treatment groups," P<0.01), with EGCG outperforming L-carnitine.[^detudom]
- **Oil-free and birch-sap "no-sebum" lotions and mineral powders** are formulated for a matte, low-oil finish; like other surface products they manage shine rather than production.[^draelos]
- **Heartleaf (Houttuynia cordata) and other botanicals** are popular in soothing, "sebum-balancing" products, but controlled evidence that they reduce sebum output is thin, so they are best read as gentle, well-liked cosmetics rather than proven sebosuppressants.

## The Evidence

**Oily skin, defined and characterized (Sakuma and Maibach 2012; Endly and Miller 2017).** Sakuma and Maibach's overview describes oily skin as oversized sebaceous glands producing excess sebum and maps how oiliness varies with diet, age, sex, ethnicity, and hot humid climates.[^sakuma] Endly and Miller's treatment review sets the quantitative threshold (sebum production above 1.5 mg/10cm² every three hours defines seborrhea, versus about 1 mg/10cm² normally) and reviews each treatment by mechanism and evidence.[^endly] Both are narrative reviews, and the magnitude claims below are anchored to the primary trials they draw on.

**Isotretinoin shrinks the glands (Landthaler 1980; King 1982; Cowley 1990).** Landthaler and colleagues, using histology and planimetry, found sebaceous gland size fell by up to 90% after 12 weeks of 13-cis-retinoic acid.[^landthaler] King and colleagues' double-blind dose-response study of 48 acne patients found "marked clinical improvement with a concomitant reduction in sebum excretion rate."[^king] In a separate condition, Cowley and colleagues found six weeks of isotretinoin "reduced the mean sebum excretion rate by 70%" in 10 men with seborrhoeic dermatitis, improving the rash.[^cowley] Three independent studies converge on the same point: isotretinoin lowers production substantially, unlike surface treatments.

**Hormonal reduction of sebum (Goodfellow 1984; Walton 1986).** In a double-blind study of 36 patients, oral spironolactone reduced sebum excretion in all female subjects with a dose-dependent clinical benefit at 150 to 200 mg.[^goodfellow] The route matters: Walton and colleagues' trial was titled for its result, "Lack of effect of topical spironolactone on sebum excretion," so the anti-androgen works taken by mouth, not rubbed on.[^walton]

**Topical sebum reducers (Draelos 2006; Detudom 2023).** Draelos and colleagues ran a placebo-controlled trial of 100 Japanese subjects and a split-face trial of 30 Caucasian subjects; 2% niacinamide significantly lowered the sebum excretion rate in the Japanese group but not in the Caucasian group, where only surface (casual) sebum fell.[^draelos] Detudom and colleagues randomized 90 people with seborrhea to 5% EGCG, 2% L-carnitine, or the combination, and all three cut sebum significantly from baseline over four weeks (P<0.01).[^detudom] These are short trials measuring surface sebum, a smaller and less durable effect than the hormonal and isotretinoin data.

## What's Overhyped

- **Powders, clays, and blotting papers that "control oil."** These absorb or hide surface sebum; Draelos and colleagues note such products work by "absorbing sebum from the face rather than modulating its production," so the matte finish lasts only until the glands refill the surface.[^draelos] Useful cosmetically, not a change to oiliness.
- **Topical spironolactone creams for oil.** The anti-androgen reduces sebum taken orally, but Walton and colleagues found a "lack of effect of topical spironolactone on sebum excretion."[^walton] A cream version is not equivalent to the oral drug.
- **Scrubbing or stripping oil away to make skin less oily.** Production is a continuous, hormonally driven process below the surface; removing surface lipids does not reduce gland output, and those same lipids are part of the skin's water barrier, so harsh removal has trade-offs without lowering production.[^sakuma][^draelos]
- **Botanical "sebum-balancing" claims beyond the tested ingredients.** Among K-beauty botanicals only green tea catechins and L-carnitine have controlled sebum data, and that is on surface sebum over weeks;[^detudom] broader "balancing" or "purifying" claims for heartleaf, birch sap, and similar extracts are not backed by controlled sebum trials.
