Contact dermatitis is a group of eczematous skin reactions caused by direct contact with a causative substance, split mainly into irritant contact dermatitis (skin damage from a substance that would irritate anyone with enough exposure) and allergic contact dermatitis (a delayed immune reaction to a specific allergen in someone who has become sensitized to it).[^dermnet-cd] It is "the most common cause of occupational skin disease," especially among cleaners, healthcare workers, food handlers, and hairdressers.[^dermnet-cd]

## How to know you have this

Both forms produce an eczematous rash, redness, blistering, dryness, scaling, and sometimes cracking, usually confined to the skin that touched the trigger and often asymmetrical (one hand worse than the other).[^dermnet-cd] Irritant contact dermatitis is more often reported as burning or painful than itchy, can follow a single strong exposure or build up from repeated mild ones, and is the most common cause of hand dermatitis.[^dermnet-icd] Allergic contact dermatitis is typically intensely itchy, appears 24 to 72 hours after contact with an allergen the person has previously tolerated, and can spread beyond the area of direct contact.[^dermnet-acd] Common allergy triggers include nickel in jewelry, fragrance, rosin in adhesive plasters, paraphenylenediamine in hair dye, rubber-glove accelerators, and the preservative methylisothiazolinone in wash-off products and wipes.[^dermnet-acd] Common irritants include water, soap, detergents, solvents, and, with long-term use, retinoids and benzoyl peroxide.[^dermnet-icd]

## What is happening in the skin

Irritant contact dermatitis happens when a chemical or physical agent damages the skin surface faster than it can repair itself, stripping oils and natural moisturizing factor and letting more irritant penetrate; it can affect anyone with enough exposure, though people with atopic dermatitis are especially susceptible.[^dermnet-icd] Allergic contact dermatitis is a type IV (delayed) hypersensitivity reaction: CD4+ T-lymphocytes recognize an allergen on the skin and release cytokines that drive the inflammation, a process that only happens in people who have already become sensitized to that specific substance, sometimes after years of prior, symptom-free contact.[^dermnet-acd] The two can coexist, and patch testing is the gold-standard way to tell them apart in a persistent or severe case.[^dermnet-icd][^dermnet-acd]

## What actually works

For both forms, identifying and avoiding the specific irritant or allergen is the principal strategy, and for allergic contact dermatitis, formal patch testing is how the trigger is confirmed.[^dermnet-acd] Because the skin barrier is damaged either way, DermNet's general management for contact dermatitis includes switching to a pH-balanced, soap-free cleanser, drying skin carefully after washing, and applying emollients frequently while the rash is active and for some weeks after; general barrier-support evidence is covered on [[skin-barrier-repair]].[^dermnet-cd] Active flares are treated with a short course of a topical corticosteroid, and severe cases sometimes need a short course of oral corticosteroids, phototherapy, or an immunosuppressant such as methotrexate or ciclosporin.[^dermnet-cd] DermNet also lists topical calcineurin inhibitors, [[tacrolimus-topical|tacrolimus]] ointment and [[pimecrolimus]] cream, among the treatments.[^dermnet-acd] For occupational irritant hand dermatitis specifically, moisturizer use showed a plausible protective effect in a Cochrane review of workplace prevention trials, described in [The Evidence](#the-evidence) below.[^cochrane-oihd]

## The Evidence

The most relevant controlled-trial evidence here is preventive rather than curative: a 2018 Cochrane review pooled 9 randomized controlled trials (2,888 workers without hand dermatitis at baseline) testing barrier creams, moisturizers, or skin-protection education against occupational irritant hand dermatitis.[^cochrane-oihd] Moisturizer use alone showed a possible clinically important protective effect: 13% of the moisturizer group developed signs of the condition versus 19% of controls (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.46 to 1.09; 507 participants, 3 studies), rated low-quality evidence.[^cochrane-oihd] Combining barrier creams with moisturizers showed a similar pattern (8% vs 13%, RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.33 to 1.42; 474 participants, 2 studies), also low-quality.[^cochrane-oihd] Barrier creams alone performed worse: 29% of users developed signs versus 33% of controls (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.72 to 1.06; 999 participants, 4 studies), a difference the reviewers judged not clinically important.[^cochrane-oihd] Skin-protection education alone showed an imprecise, very-low-quality effect (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.54 to 1.08).[^cochrane-oihd] *Bottom line: in workers without existing hand dermatitis, regularly moisturizing may cut the risk of developing occupational irritant hand dermatitis, but the trials are small, low-quality, and cannot rule out no effect; barrier cream alone is the weaker option of the two.* The review's authors concluded there is "insufficient evidence to confidently assess the effectiveness" of any of these primary-prevention measures, which is why avoidance of the identified trigger, not a skincare product, remains the core of management once dermatitis has already developed.[^cochrane-oihd]
