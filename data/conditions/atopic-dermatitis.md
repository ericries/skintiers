Atopic dermatitis, the form of eczema meant when people say "eczema" without qualification, is a chronic, intensely itchy inflammatory skin disease that relapses and remits over years and has no cure, where treatment "at best achieves symptom control rather than cure."[^nutten2015] The American Academy of Dermatology guideline describes it as "a chronic, pruritic inflammatory dermatosis that affects up to 25% of children and 2% to 3% of adults."[^eichenfield2014] Among **topical skincare** options for atopic dermatitis, the intervention with the strongest evidence is plain moisturizing, which reduces flares and the amount of prescription steroid needed rather than clearing the disease, and no moisturizer has been shown to beat another.[^cochrane2017]

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians look for, described so a reader can recognize them. They are not a self-diagnosis: the AAD guideline states that "The diagnosis of atopic dermatitis is made clinically" from history, the look and location of the rash, and associated signs, and only a clinician can make that diagnosis.[^eichenfield2014]

The single feature the U.K. Working Party found most discriminating is a **history of an itchy skin condition**; in its validated criteria, atopic dermatitis is recognized by that itch plus several of the following: a history of the rash in the skin creases, a history of generally dry skin, onset before age 2, a personal history of asthma or hay fever, and visible dermatitis in the skin folds.[^williams1994] In everyday terms:

- **Itch is the core.** The itch tends to come before the visible rash and drives much of the distress; scratching then worsens the skin. A rash without itch is unlikely to be atopic dermatitis by these criteria.[^williams1994]
- **The skin looks and feels eczematous.** The AAD lists the typical findings as "erythema, edema, xerosis, erosions/excoriations, oozing and crusting, and lichenification," meaning redness, swelling, dryness, scratch marks, weeping and crusting in flares, and thickened leathery skin where it has been rubbed for a long time; these "vary by patient age and chronicity."[^eichenfield2014] Generally dry skin between flares is common.[^williams1994]
- **Where it sits changes with age.** In infants and young children it often appears on the cheeks, face, and the outer surfaces of the limbs; in older children and adults it tends to settle into the skin folds (the flexures): the fronts of the elbows, behind the knees, the fronts of the ankles, and around the neck, which is the flexural pattern the U.K. criteria treat as characteristic.[^williams1994][^eichenfield2014]
- **It relapses and remits.** It is chronic and comes and goes over months and years rather than clearing in days, usually beginning in early childhood.[^nutten2015][^williams1994]
- **Personal or family atopy is common.** A personal or family history of asthma or hay fever supports the pattern, because atopic dermatitis is part of the same atopic tendency.[^williams1994][^nutten2015]

How it is told apart from look-alikes follows from those features. Atopic dermatitis is defined by itch, an eczematous (not comedonal) rash, a flexural distribution, early onset, and a chronic relapsing course with atopy, a combination that distinguishes it from conditions defined by other lesions, such as the blackheads and whiteheads of [[acne]] or the centrofacial flushing and visible vessels of [[rosacea]].[^williams1994][^eichenfield2014] Distinguishing overlapping rashes still requires clinical examination.[^eichenfield2014]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Atopic dermatitis is a *medical* condition that can become infected, disturb sleep, and carries measurably higher rates of anxiety and depression, so evaluation by a board-certified dermatologist is appropriate, especially for widespread, weeping, or poorly controlled disease.[^chiesafuxench2019] The anti-inflammatory treatments with the strongest evidence are prescription drugs rather than topical skincare, including topical corticosteroids, the topical calcineurin inhibitors tacrolimus and pimecrolimus, topical crisaborole, and, for more severe disease, the injectable biologic dupilumab and oral drugs such as JAK inhibitors and ciclosporin.[^eichenfield2014] These require a prescriber. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## What Atopic Dermatitis Is

Atopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease, not a temporary rash, and it is common. The AAD guideline puts it at "up to 25% of children and 2% to 3% of adults."[^eichenfield2014] A review of the global epidemiology found it "affects up to 20% of children and up to 3% of adults," with prevalence "still increasing, especially in low-income countries," and noted that the first signs usually appear early in childhood and "often precede other allergic diseases such as asthma or allergic rhinitis."[^nutten2015]

The most rigorous recent estimate in U.S. adults comes from the Atopic Dermatitis in America Study, a cross-sectional population survey (1,278 adults) using modified U.K. diagnostic criteria, which found an adult prevalence of "7.3% (5.9-8.8)," of whom 60.1% had mild, 28.9% moderate, and 11% severe disease.[^chiesafuxench2019] The same study measured the burden: adults with atopic dermatitis had a worse quality-of-life index (mean 4.71 vs 0.97 in controls, P < 0.001) and higher anxiety and depression scores, which the authors read as "a worse impact on quality of life and an increased likelihood of anxiety or depression."[^chiesafuxench2019]

## What Is Happening in the Skin

Atopic dermatitis arises from a damaged skin barrier and a misdirected immune response acting together, rather than from one cause. The epidemiology review frames it as "the interaction of a dysfunctional epidermal barrier in genetically predisposed individuals with harmful effects of environmental agents."[^nutten2015]

- **A skin-barrier defect.** The clearest genetic evidence is for filaggrin, a protein that helps build the outer barrier. Palmer and colleagues showed that two loss-of-function variants in the filaggrin gene are "very strong predisposing factors for atopic dermatitis," carried by "approximately 9% of people of European origin," and concluded the work "establishes a key role for impaired skin barrier function in the development of atopic disease."[^palmer2006] A leaky barrier loses water and lets in irritants and allergens, which is the mechanistic reason repairing and moisturizing the barrier is a mainstay of care.[^palmer2006][^nutten2015]
- **Immune and allergic involvement, as consequence more than trigger.** Atopic dermatitis has long been called an allergic disease, but the epidemiology review notes "the contribution of allergic reactions to the initiation of AD is challenged," proposing instead that allergy "is rather a consequence of AD" once the barrier is breached in an atopic person.[^nutten2015] This is one reason food-elimination diets are a poor primary treatment (see What's Overhyped).

## What Actually Works

The best-evidenced topical **skincare** for atopic dermatitis rests on a Cochrane systematic review of 77 randomized trials (6,603 participants) of emollients and moisturizers for eczema.[^cochrane2017] The prescription anti-inflammatory drugs are more potent but are not topical skincare; each now has its own page here: the corticosteroid class ([[topical-corticosteroids]]), the topical calcineurin inhibitors [[tacrolimus-topical]] ([[protopic-tacrolimus-ointment|Protopic]]) and [[pimecrolimus]] ([[elidel-pimecrolimus-cream|Elidel]]), the steroid-free [[tapinarof]] ([[vtama-tapinarof-cream|Vtama]], now approved for eczema), and the topical JAK inhibitor [[opzelura-ruxolitinib-cream|ruxolitinib (Opzelura)]].

**1. Moisturizing and emollients (the barrier-repair category) - Effect: modest to notable (flare prevention and steroid-sparing) - Evidence: solid.** This is the best-evidenced skincare step for eczema. In the Cochrane review, moisturizer use produced lower investigator-assessed severity than control (SMD -1.04, 95% CI -1.57 to -0.51; 12 studies, 1,281 participants; high-quality evidence) and fewer flares (RR 0.33, 95% CI 0.17-0.62; moderate quality), and against no moisturizer at all it cut flares (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.23-0.70), prolonged time to flare (median 180 vs 30 days), and reduced the topical corticosteroid needed (mean difference -9.30 g, 95% CI -15.3 to -3.27).[^cochrane2017] Adding a moisturizer to an active drug beat the active drug alone (SMD -0.87, 95% CI -1.17 to -0.57; moderate quality).[^cochrane2017] Moisturizing is a well-evidenced barrier intervention for eczema that reduces flares and spares steroids; how it works and how to read the evidence is covered on [[moisturizing]]. The honest ceiling: the review "did not find reliable evidence that one moisturiser is better than another."[^cochrane2017]

**2. [[colloidal-oatmeal]] - Effect: modest - Evidence: mixed.** Colloidal oatmeal is an FDA-recognized skin protectant with randomized-trial support for relieving itch and improving the barrier in mild-to-moderate eczema, where a 1% cream matched a prescription barrier cream; its full, indication-by-indication grade is on [[colloidal-oatmeal]]. Set against the category evidence, though, in the Cochrane review oat-containing moisturizers showed "no significant differences" versus control on investigator-assessed severity (SMD -0.23, 95% CI -0.66 to 0.21), while still reducing flares (RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.12-0.7), and one study reported more adverse events in the oat group (Peto OR 7.26, 95% CI 1.76-29.92), consistent with oat proteins being an occasional contact allergen.[^cochrane2017] A legitimate, well-tolerated anti-itch emollient, positioned as an adjunct rather than a flare treatment.

**3. Ceramide and physiologic-lipid moisturizers - Effect: modest (barrier measures) - Evidence: mixed.** Eczema-prone skin is measurably short of ceramides, the skin's own barrier lipids, and ceramide moisturizers improve barrier readings such as water loss; the mechanism and trial detail are on [[ceramides]], which is a physiologically reasonable way to replace a lipid the skin genuinely lacks. What the evidence does not establish is superiority over a plainer moisturizer: in the trial cited on that page a ceramide cream lowered water loss but did not reduce eczema severity more than a matched base, and the Cochrane review found no reliable evidence any one moisturizer beats another.[^cochrane2017] A humectant-and-occlusive base such as [[petrolatum]] with [[glycerin]] carries much of the same category benefit.

## What's Overhyped

- **"This specialty barrier cream outperforms plain moisturizers."** The Cochrane review of 77 trials concluded "We did not find reliable evidence that one moisturiser is better than another."[^cochrane2017] The demonstrated benefit belongs to moisturizing as a practice, not to any premium ceramide, oat, or "eczema" formulation over a cheaper one. A specialty cream may be pleasant and effective, but the evidence does not support paying more for a superior outcome versus a plainer moisturizer.[^cochrane2017]
- **A "cure," whether cream or diet.** The epidemiology review states plainly that treatment "at best achieves symptom control rather than cure."[^nutten2015] No skincare product resolves the disease; the best-evidenced options reduce flares and symptoms and are used on an ongoing basis.[^cochrane2017][^nutten2015]
- **Food-elimination diets as a primary treatment.** Because "the contribution of allergic reactions to the initiation of AD is challenged" and allergy is framed as a consequence rather than the cause of the barrier breach, broad dietary elimination is not a substitute for barrier care and anti-inflammatory treatment.[^nutten2015]

## The Evidence

**The skincare anchor: van Zuuren 2017 (Cochrane).** A systematic review of 77 randomized controlled trials (6,603 participants; mean age 18.6 years; mean duration 6.7 weeks) assessed emollients and moisturizers for eczema.[^cochrane2017] Its GRADE conclusions: moisturizer use lowered investigator-assessed severity (SMD -1.04, 95% CI -1.57 to -0.51; high-quality evidence) and reduced flares (RR 0.33, 95% CI 0.17-0.62; moderate quality); versus no moisturizer it reduced flares (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.23-0.70), prolonged time to flare (median 180 vs 30 days), and reduced corticosteroid use (MD -9.30 g, 95% CI -15.3 to -3.27); and combining a moisturizer with an active drug beat the active drug alone (SMD -0.87, 95% CI -1.17 to -0.57).[^cochrane2017] The authors concluded that "Most moisturisers showed some beneficial effects," working best with active treatment and "prolonging time to flare, and reducing the number of flares," while "We did not find reliable evidence that one moisturiser is better than another."[^cochrane2017] The takeaway is that the effect is a class effect of moisturizing, and the head-to-head differences the marketing turns on are not established.

**Why barrier care makes mechanistic sense: Palmer 2006 (Nature Genetics).** This genetic study identified two loss-of-function filaggrin variants as "very strong predisposing factors for atopic dermatitis," carried by "approximately 9% of people of European origin," establishing "a key role for impaired skin barrier function."[^palmer2006] A barrier that leaks water and admits allergens is the physical target that moisturizing and occlusion address.[^palmer2006][^nutten2015]

**Recognition rests on validated criteria: Williams 1994 (U.K. Working Party).** From 224 patients examined blind by two observers, multiple logistic regression derived a minimum discriminating set: a history of a pruritic skin condition plus a history of flexural involvement, a history of dry skin, onset under age 2, a personal history of asthma, and visible flexural dermatitis; the set held up in a further community sample of 150.[^williams1994] These are the features that ground the recognition section above, not a home test.

**Burden and prevalence: Chiesa Fuxench 2019 and Nutten 2015.** The U.S. population survey put adult prevalence at "7.3% (5.9-8.8)" with a worse quality-of-life index (4.71 vs 0.97, P < 0.001) among those affected, and the global epidemiology review reported it "affects up to 20% of children and up to 3% of adults" and is "still increasing."[^chiesafuxench2019][^nutten2015]

These sources are methodologically independent: a Cochrane synthesis of treatment trials, a genetic study of barrier biology, a diagnostic-criteria derivation, and two epidemiology studies, each cited for the claim it actually supports.[^cochrane2017][^palmer2006][^williams1994][^chiesafuxench2019][^nutten2015]
