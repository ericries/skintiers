Jock itch is tinea cruris, the same dermatophyte infection as [[athletes-foot]] and [[tinea-corporis-ringworm]] in a different place. A 2025 clinical review groups it with them, noting that "tinea cruris, tinea pedis, and tinea unguium (most common type of onychomycosis) are more likely in adolescents and adults" and that all three "generally respond to inexpensive topical antifungal agents".[^afp] The general antifungal evidence is on those pages and not repeated here; what this page adds is what the labels say about the groin specifically.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice. A groin rash that does not clear with an over-the-counter antifungal, spreads, weeps, or occurs in someone with diabetes or a weakened immune system is worth a doctor's evaluation, partly because several non-fungal rashes look similar.

## How to know you have this

The honest answer is that you often cannot be certain by looking. The same review warns that "Clinical diagnosis without testing may be unreliable because other conditions can resemble tinea infections", giving the example that "tinea corporis can be confused with eczema".[^afp] In the groin that ambiguity matters more than elsewhere, because inverse psoriasis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, candidal intertrigo and simple friction rash all occupy the same folds. A rash that is itchy, scaly and spreading outward from the crease is consistent with tinea cruris; persistent uncertainty is a reason to have it looked at rather than to keep trying creams.

## Jock itch gets a shorter course than athlete's foot

**The same tube, a different regimen.** This is the practical thing the labels tell you and the marketing does not. [[terbinafine]] directs "for jock itch and ringworm: apply once a day (morning or night) for 1 week", against twice daily for one to two weeks on the foot depending on location.[^terbinafine-label] [[butenafine]] directs "for jock itch and ringworm: apply once a day to affected skin for 2 weeks", while its foot course runs either one week twice daily or four weeks once daily.[^butenafine-label] [[clotrimazole]] is blunter still: "for athlete's foot and ringworm, use daily for 4 weeks; for jock itch, use daily for 2 weeks", applied "twice daily, morning and night".[^clotrimazole-label]

**What the shorter course does and does not mean.** Groin skin is thinner and occluded, so drug stays in contact differently than on a sole. What the labels record is what each sponsor studied and chose to claim, not a measured comparison between body sites. No groin-specific head-to-head sits behind any of this.

**One claim narrows and one does not.** Butenafine limits its athlete's foot claim to "between the toes" and says "Effectiveness on the bottom or sides of foot is unknown", yet its jock itch claim carries no such qualifier.[^butenafine-label] Reading a label's limits is more informative than reading its promises.

## The evidence is borrowed from the foot

The tier list above inherits its grades, and that is worth stating plainly rather than hiding in a footnote. The pooled randomised evidence separating these drug classes comes from a Cochrane review of "fungal infections of the skin and nails of the foot", quoted in full on [[athletes-foot]].[^cochrane] Nothing in it was measured in the groin. The 2025 review treats the sites together for treatment purposes, which is a reasonable clinical position and still not the same as groin trial data.[^afp]

**Do not reach for a combination steroid cream.** The same review ends on stewardship, advising that "Antifungal stewardship, including avoiding the use of combination antifungal-corticosteroids, should be emphasized to optimize outcomes and help prevent resistance."[^afp] That advice lands hardest in the groin, where combination antifungal-steroid products are commonly prescribed for an itchy fold and where the steroid can blunt the appearance of an infection it is not curing.
