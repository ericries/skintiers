Giant hogweed (Heracleum mantegazzianum) is "an invasive flowering weed that can reach a height of 13 feet" whose sap, combined with sunlight, causes "severe phytophotodermatitis".[^cutis] The mechanism is the same furanocoumarin reaction described on [[phytophotodermatitis]]; what sets hogweed apart is severity, so it gets its own page rather than a line there.

> **This is the serious end of the plant-plus-sunlight burn.** Lime juice on a wrist leaves brown marks; hogweed sap can produce "cutaneous, full-thickness, and long-lasting dermatitis, and in extreme cases, massive skin necrosis".[^burns] Two practical facts are worth carrying: the reaction is **delayed**, appearing roughly a day after contact in a reported case, so people do not connect it to the gardening they did yesterday; and the people most exposed are those **cutting or clearing the plant**.[^cutis][^burns] If you have blistering after contact with a large unfamiliar weed, that is a medical problem, not a skincare one.

## How to know you have this

The giveaway is the combination of a big unfamiliar plant, outdoor work, and a delayed blistering rash on sun-exposed skin. A 2021 case report describes "a 27-year-old man who presented with a blistering rash on the neck and arms that developed 18 to 24 hours after trimming giant hogweed plants without photoprotection".[^cutis] The authors advise that "A diagnosis of giant hogweed phytophotodermatitis should be considered for patients presenting with acute erythema and bullae in sun-exposed areas after exposure to the plant".[^cutis] Bullae means large fluid blisters, which is a step beyond the redness-and-pigment course typical of citrus exposure.

Because the delay runs to a day or more, the cause is easy to miss. That lag is also why it is worth knowing the plant by sight before clearing overgrown ground.

## Why It Is More Severe Than Lime Burn

Same chemistry, much higher dose. Hogweed sap is loaded with furanocoumarins and clearing the plant coats skin in it, rather than the incidental splash that causes a margarita burn. A 2015 study in the journal *Burns* describes exposure to giant hogweeds as leading to "phototoxicity and even chemical burns manifested by cutaneous, full-thickness, and long-lasting dermatitis, and in extreme cases, massive skin necrosis".[^burns] Full-thickness means the full depth of the skin, the same language used for serious thermal burns.

Two species carry this risk and are often discussed together: Heracleum mantegazzianum and Heracleum sosnowskyi, both described as "aggressive invaders that are successfully spreading in different parts of the world".[^burns] Reported US cases of hogweed phytophotodermatitis are increasing.[^cutis]

## Who Is Most At Risk

This is substantially an **occupational** problem. Forestry workers are flagged as higher risk "because of the outdoor nature of their work and their active involvement in managing invasive species".[^burns] A survey of 1563 employees across 367 Polish forest districts found that "More than 20% of the surveyed individuals had been exposed to these plants at least once in their lives, but only less than half of them were aware of proceeding afterward", and that fewer than 35% knew anything about controlling the plants.[^burns] Stands were reported in over 50% of the forest districts surveyed.[^burns]

The gap that study identifies is not exposure alone but knowing what to do after it, which is the argument for recognising the plant before you cut it.

## The Evidence and What To Do

**Prevention is the whole game, and it is clothing rather than a product.** Cover skin when clearing unfamiliar large weeds, and wash sap off promptly and out of sunlight. The general preventive case, including the role of covering up, is on [[phytophotodermatitis]] and is not repeated here. The 2021 case involved trimming the plant "without photoprotection".[^cutis]

**Blistering is a reason to see a doctor, not to treat at home.** Nothing on this site is a treatment for a chemical burn, and the severity language in the cited literature is the reason. Where a case settles into lasting brown marks, that aftermath is ordinary post-inflammatory pigment and is covered on [[hyperpigmentation]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Extensive blistering, facial involvement, or any burn in a child needs urgent medical assessment.
