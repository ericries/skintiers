Actinic keratosis (also called solar keratosis) is a rough, scaly patch that forms on chronically sun-exposed skin and is considered a precancer: a lesion the National Cancer Institute describes as "the related noninvasive lesion actinic keratosis (viewed by some pathologists as a variant of in situ [squamous cell carcinoma])."[^nci] It is a physician-diagnosed and physician-treated condition, not something over-the-counter skincare treats, and the one skincare-relevant lever is prevention through daily [[sunscreen-uv-filters|sun protection]].

> The honest framing is two-sided. Actinic keratosis is a marker of accumulated sun damage and a potential precursor of [[squamous-cell-carcinoma]], so lesions are evaluated and treated by a clinician rather than self-managed. At the same time, the National Cancer Institute is clear that "the rate of progression is extremely low, and most do not become SCCs."[^nci] So it warrants medical attention without warranting alarm, and the skincare contribution is upstream: preventing the sun damage that causes it.

## How to know you have this

Actinic keratoses show up as "typically red, scaly patches [that] usually arise on areas of chronically sun-exposed skin and are likely to be found on the face and dorsal aspects of the hand," in the National Cancer Institute's description.[^nci] Because they are a clinical marker of sun damage and a possible precursor to skin cancer, a rough or persistent scaly spot on sun-exposed skin is something for a clinician to examine rather than to diagnose or treat at home.

## The Evidence

Actinic keratoses are "potential precursors of SCC, but the rate of progression is extremely low, and most do not become SCCs."[^nci] They still matter clinically: a squamous cell carcinoma that arises from an actinic keratosis on sun-exposed skin is, per the National Cancer Institute, "less likely to metastasize" and carries "a better prognosis than those who develop de novo SCCs or SCCs on non-sun-exposed skin."[^nci] The presence of actinic keratoses is therefore treated as a signal of cumulative sun damage and skin-cancer risk.

Treatment is physician-directed. For actinic keratosis specifically, the National Cancer Institute lists topical agents (fluorouracil, imiquimod cream, diclofenac sodium 3% gel, ingenol mebutate), chemical peels, surgery, photodynamic therapy, and laser therapy, chosen by a clinician according to the number and location of lesions.[^nci] None of these is an over-the-counter skincare step, so this page does not grade at-home products for treating actinic keratosis.

Where skincare does have an evidence-backed role is prevention. Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is the best-supported way to reduce the cumulative ultraviolet exposure that drives actinic keratosis and other sun-damage lesions; the filter evidence and how sunscreen is rated are on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a diagnosis or treatment plan. A rough, scaly, or persistent spot on sun-exposed skin, and any lesion that bleeds, grows, or does not heal, should be evaluated by a dermatologist or physician; actinic keratosis and its treatment are managed by a clinician.
