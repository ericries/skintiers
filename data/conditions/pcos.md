Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal condition in which excess androgens drive a recognizable set of skin and hair changes, the most prominent being a distinctive, hormonally patterned acne.[^3][^4][^5] Estimates of how many reproductive-age women are affected vary with the diagnostic criteria used: a 2025 narrative review put it at "affecting 5%-10% of reproductive-aged women," while a StatPearls survey of the literature found reported prevalence "ranges between 5% and 26%, depending on the diagnostic criteria applied."[^4][^2] This page focuses on the skin: what PCOS-related acne looks like, how it differs mechanically from ordinary clogged-pore acne, and which topical actives the evidence actually supports for each pattern.

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians look for, described so a reader can recognize the pattern rather than self-diagnose; only a clinician can make the diagnosis. Most guidelines diagnose PCOS when two of three criteria are present: "chronic oligo-anovulation, clinical or biological hyperandrogenism, and PCOM [polycystic ovarian morphology] in the absence of any other pathology," per the Rotterdam criteria.[^2][^1] The skin and hair signs below reflect the hyperandrogenism arm of that definition.

- **A distinctive acne pattern rather than a heavier version of ordinary acne.** A guide to adult female acne co-authored by five acne specialists found the lesions "located mainly on the lower part of the face, including the mandibular region, the perioral region and the chin, conferring a U-shape," and described as "inflammatory lesions, papules and pustules, of mild to moderate intensity, with the presence of few closed comedones or microcysts."[^3] A separate review of PCOS skin findings similarly reports that "PCOS-related acne predominantly presents with inflammatory papules, pustules, and nodules on the lower face, jawline, chest, and upper back."[^4] The comedonal pattern of ordinary teenage acne, blackheads and whiteheads across the whole face, is covered on [[acne]].
- **A cycle tied to hormones.** The same guide to clinical practice reports "worsening of the disease in the premenstrual period in 60 to 70% of women," along with flares in pregnancy and with progestin-only contraception.[^3]
- **A StatPearls review of the condition advises that clinicians "should be particularly vigilant for underlying hyperandrogenism in females in their mid-20s to 30s who have persistent or worsening acne,"** since acne appearing or worsening in adulthood, rather than settling as it does after adolescence, is itself a clue to an androgen driver.[^2]
- **Hirsutism, as context.** The same androgen excess can also produce hirsutism, defined as "coarse, dark terminal hairs in a pattern typically associated with males."[^2] It is named here as part of the same hormonal picture; hirsutism treatments are outside this page's skincare focus.
- **Oily skin.** Excess androgen stimulates the sebaceous glands directly; a 2025 review of PCOS skin findings states that "elevated androgen levels, such as testosterone and DHT, stimulate sebaceous glands, leading to excessive sebum production."[^4] The mechanism and what actually reduces sebum output, as opposed to just mopping it up, is covered in depth on [[oily-skin]].

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. PCOS has health implications well beyond the skin, including menstrual and fertility effects, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk factors, so evaluation by a clinician, whether a primary care physician, gynecologist, or endocrinologist, is appropriate for anyone with these signs. The treatments with the strongest evidence for the underlying androgen driver, combined oral contraceptives and spironolactone, are prescription drugs that fall outside this page's topical-skincare scope. They are named here so readers know they exist; they require a prescriber, carry their own risks and monitoring, and are not graded on this page beyond the topical adjunct role azelaic acid can play alongside them. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## Two acne mechanisms, two different treatments

The central distinction for PCOS-related skin is that "acne" covers two different processes, and they do not respond to the same things.

**Comedonal (clogged-pore) acne** starts as a mechanical problem: dead skin cells and sebum plug the follicle, forming the open comedones (blackheads) and closed comedones (whiteheads) that define ordinary acne, sometimes followed by bacterial overgrowth and inflammation once a follicle is blocked. The full mechanism and its graded topical treatments are covered on [[acne]].

**Hormonal or cystic acne** starts upstream, with the androgen excess itself. A 2025 review of the mechanism explains that insulin resistance, common in PCOS, "augments ovarian/adrenal androgen synthesis and lowers sex hormone-binding globulin levels, increasing free testosterone and sebum production," while "IGF-1 signaling further amplifies sebogenesis and follicular hyperkeratinization."[^5] In practice this produces the deep, tender nodules concentrated on the jawline, chin, and lower face described above, rather than a face full of surface blackheads.[^3][^4]

That mechanistic difference is why the two patterns respond differently to treatment. A topical retinoid or acid can unclog a follicle or kill surface bacteria, but it does not lower how much androgen reaches the sebaceous gland or how much sebum the gland makes; only oral isotretinoin and hormonal therapy have been shown to durably reduce sebum production itself, detailed on [[oily-skin]]. Topical skincare therefore tends to help the comedonal component of PCOS-related acne more reliably than it helps the deep hormonal nodules, where the evidence-backed treatments for the driver itself are systemic.

## What helps the clogged-pore component

For the comedonal, follicular-plugging part of PCOS-related acne, the evidence base is the same one that applies to acne generally. [[adapalene]] and [[tretinoin]] are topical retinoids that normalize follicular shedding and clear plugged follicles, with equivalent efficacy to each other and solid trial support, detailed on [[acne]]. Over-the-counter [[retinol]] shares the same vitamin-A pathway, but its own controlled trials are for skin aging rather than acne; any acne benefit is extrapolated from the prescription retinoids, not tested in retinol itself, see [[retinoids]]. [[salicylic-acid]] is oil-soluble and penetrates sebum-filled pores; a 500-patient randomized trial found a 2% leave-on formulation matched adapalene gel for improvement (51.01% vs 43.10%, p=0.0831), detailed on [[acne]]. [[azelaic-acid]] is equally effective to tretinoin 0.05% for comedonal acne and better tolerated. For the inflamed, pus-filled lesions rather than the plugged follicles themselves, [[benzoyl-peroxide]] is antimicrobial rather than comedolytic and is the evidence-backed topical option for that inflammatory component.

## What helps the hormonal, cystic component

Topical skincare has a smaller role once the driver is androgen excess itself. The systemic, hormone-directed options carry the strongest evidence for that driver rather than anything applied to the skin. A 2025 review of PCOS-associated acne states that "first-line therapy includes combined oral contraceptive pills with antiandrogenic progestins; spironolactone is an effective adjunct," alongside "judicious use of standard acne modalities such as topical retinoids, benzoyl peroxide, and oral/topical antibiotics."[^5] A Cochrane systematic review of 31 trials (12,579 participants) found combined oral contraceptives "reduced acne lesion counts, severity grades and self-assessed acne compared to placebo" across six progestin formulations, detailed on [[arowojolu-2012-cochrane-coc-acne]]. A 410-woman, placebo-controlled UK trial found oral spironolactone improved acne-related quality of life and investigator-rated clearance versus placebo, detailed on [[santer-2023-safa-spironolactone-acne]].

Both act by reducing circulating or effective androgen exposure at the follicle, not by treating the skin surface, and both are prescription drugs with their own contraindications and monitoring (spironolactone requires kidney-function and potassium checks; combined oral contraceptives carry their own risk profile). Whether either is appropriate for a given person is a decision for a clinician who can weigh a person's full hormonal, reproductive, and medical picture, not something topical skincare can substitute for. [[azelaic-acid]] remains a reasonable topical adjunct alongside those systemic options: it is comedolytic and antimicrobial with its own solid acne evidence, but it does not lower androgen levels or sebum output the way the systemic options do.

## The Evidence

**Diagnostic criteria.** The Rotterdam ESHRE/ASRM-Sponsored consensus workshop defined PCOS as "a syndrome of ovarian dysfunction along with the cardinal features hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovary (PCO) morphology," while cautioning that "no single diagnostic criterion... is sufficient for clinical diagnosis."[^1] A StatPearls synthesis of subsequent guidelines describes the working rule as two of three features: chronic oligo-anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology.[^2]

**Clinical pattern.** Two independent groups converge on the same acne distribution. Bagatin and colleagues' guide to clinical practice, drawing on "the first studies" of adult female acne, describes a U-shaped, lower-face and mandibular distribution with few comedones and a premenstrual flare in 60 to 70% of women.[^3] Farhan and colleagues' 2025 review of PCOS cutaneous manifestations independently reports inflammatory papules, pustules, and nodules concentrated on "the lower face, jawline, chest, and upper back."[^4]

**Mechanism.** Damoulaki and colleagues' 2025 review traces the pathway from insulin resistance to acne: IR "augments ovarian/adrenal androgen synthesis and lowers sex hormone-binding globulin levels, increasing free testosterone and sebum production," while "IGF-1 signaling further amplifies sebogenesis and follicular hyperkeratinization," and identifies combined oral contraceptives with antiandrogenic progestins as first-line therapy, with spironolactone as an effective adjunct.[^5]
