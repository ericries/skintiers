Pityriasis versicolor (also called tinea versicolor, though "tinea" strictly refers to dermatophyte infections) is "a common yeast infection of the skin, in which flaky discoloured patches appear on the chest and back."[^dermnet] It is caused by an overgrowth of Malassezia yeast, the same genus behind [[fungal-acne]] and [[seborrheic-dermatitis]], and topical antifungals are first-line treatment for all three.[^dermnet]

## How to know you have this

Pityriasis versicolor shows up as multiple round or oval patches on the trunk, neck, and upper arms, in areas dense with sebaceous glands.[^dermnet][^gupta] The patches can be coppery brown, paler than surrounding skin, or pink, and pale (hypopigmented) patches are more common in darker skin, an appearance called pityriasis versicolor alba.[^dermnet] Fine surface scale is often visible, more obvious when the skin is scratched. The condition is usually asymptomatic though sometimes mildly itchy, and it is not more prone to sunburn, but because the yeast blocks normal tanning the patches become far more visible once surrounding skin tans in summer.[^dermnet] It is diagnosed clinically, sometimes confirmed with a Wood's lamp (yellow-green fluorescence) or a KOH skin scraping showing the classic "spaghetti and meatballs" pattern of hyphae and yeast cells.[^dermnet]

## What is happening in the skin

Malassezia yeast lives on normal skin as part of the resident microbiota, particularly in oily areas, without causing a rash.[^dermnet] Under conditions that favor it (heat, humidity, heavy sweating), it switches to a mycelial (filamentous) growth form on the skin surface.[^dermnet] The color change comes from two different mechanisms depending on type: brown patches involve the yeast inducing enlarged pigment granules in melanocytes, while pale patches are thought to result from a chemical the yeast produces that diffuses into the skin and impairs melanocyte function.[^dermnet] It is a surface overgrowth, not an invasive infection, which is why it responds well to topical antifungals and why relapse is common once conditions favor the yeast again: one review found relapse "can be as high as 80% within 2 years."[^gupta]

## What actually works

Topical antifungals are first-line and clear the yeast reliably. [[ketoconazole]] (as a 2% cream or shampoo) has the strongest trial base; [[selenium-sulfide|selenium sulfide]] shampoo performs comparably and is cheap and widely available; [[ciclopirox]] cream is a hydroxypyridone antifungal (a different chemical class from the azoles) shown to outperform clotrimazole head-to-head.[^gupta] [[zinc-pyrithione|Zinc pyrithione]] and [[piroctone-olamine|piroctone olamine]] work the same anti-Malassezia way and are used in the same wash-on, leave-on-a-few-minutes, wash-off routine, though with less pityriasis-versicolor-specific trial data.[^gupta] DermNet's applied protocol: apply from the jawline down, leave on 5 to 15 minutes, daily for one week then weekly for a month, then monthly as maintenance to prevent recurrence.[^dermnet] For extensive or treatment-resistant cases, a short course of an oral antifungal (itraconazole or fluconazole, prescribed) is often used and works well; oral terbinafine does not work for this yeast infection despite treating dermatophyte fungi effectively.[^dermnet][^gupta]

## What's overhyped

Clearing the surface scale is not the same as normal skin tone returning. The fine scale is the marker of active yeast, and once it is gone the infection is over, but altered pigmentation, especially the pale patches, "may persist for weeks or months" afterward and is not a sign the treatment failed.[^dermnet] Because relapse is common when heat and humidity return, a single course rarely means the problem is solved for good; monthly maintenance with a topical antifungal is the realistic way to keep it from recurring.[^dermnet]

## The Evidence

DermNet NZ's clinical overview is the descriptive backbone for cause, features, and standard treatment protocol.[^dermnet] For efficacy, a placebo-controlled trial found 2% ketoconazole cream produced mycological cure in 43 of 51 patients (84%) versus 11 of 50 (22%) on placebo.[^gupta] A head-to-head trial found 2% ketoconazole shampoo and 2.5% selenium sulfide shampoo performed similarly (95% versus 85% cure at 4 weeks, not statistically different).[^gupta] Another trial found ciclopirox olamine 1% cream significantly outperformed clotrimazole 1% cream (77% versus 45% mycological cure).[^gupta] A 2015 systematic review of the PubMed literature concluded topical antifungals (zinc pyrithione, ketoconazole, terbinafine) are first-line, oral itraconazole and fluconazole are reserved for severe or recalcitrant disease, and oral terbinafine and oral ketoconazole are not appropriate treatments for this condition.[^gupta]

This page describes a common, benign, treatable skin condition, not a substitute for a clinician's diagnosis; persistent, spreading, or diagnostically uncertain patches should be evaluated in person, and oral antifungals require a prescription.
