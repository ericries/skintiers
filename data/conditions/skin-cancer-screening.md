This is the entry point for skin cancer on this site, and a short account of what a routine whole-body skin check has and has not been shown to achieve. The individual diseases have their own pages: [[melanoma]], [[basal-cell-carcinoma]], [[squamous-cell-carcinoma]], and the precancer [[actinic-keratosis]].

> **Two true things that sit awkwardly together.** Skin cancer is extraordinarily common: the NCI estimates 5.4 million keratinocyte carcinomas diagnosed among 3.3 million people in the US in 2012, a figure that "exceeds all other cases of cancer estimated by the American Cancer Society for 2025, which is about 2 million".[^nci] And yet, in the NCI's own words, "There is insufficient evidence that population screening for skin cancer reduces skin cancer mortality."[^nci] Those are both accurate. The resolution is in the word **population**: the evidence is weak for routinely examining people with no symptoms, which is a different question from what to do about a mole that is actually changing.

## How to know you have this

Screening is not a condition, so the practical question is what to look at and when to escalate. The pattern checklist for a suspicious pigmented lesion is on [[melanoma]], which sets out the ABCDE criteria and is not repeated here. The rough, scaly sun-damage lesions that can precede squamous cell carcinoma are on [[actinic-keratosis]], and the two commonest cancers themselves are on [[basal-cell-carcinoma]] and [[squamous-cell-carcinoma]].

**Nothing on this page is a substitute for having a lesion looked at.** A new, changing, bleeding, or non-healing spot is a reason to see a clinician, and the evidence discussed below is about screening people who have noticed nothing, not about ignoring something you have noticed.

## The Evidence: what screening has and has not been shown to do

**Benefit: not established.** The NCI's health-professional summary states that "The evidence is inadequate to determine whether visual examination of the skin in asymptomatic individuals leads to a reduction in mortality from melanomatous skin cancer", and that "in asymptomatic populations, the effect of visual skin examination on mortality from nonmelanomatous skin cancers is unknown".[^nci] It grades the magnitude of effect as "Unknown", with direct evidence "limited to a single ecological study" of "Poor" internal validity.[^nci] This is an absence of good evidence, not a demonstration that screening fails.

**Harms: real, though unquantified.** The same summary finds that "Based on fair-though unquantified-evidence, visual examination of the skin in asymptomatic individuals may lead to adverse consequences", and names them: "complications of diagnostic or treatment interventions (such as poor cosmetic or functional outcomes) and the psychological effects of being labeled with a potentially fatal disease", plus "overdiagnosis, leading to the detection of biologically benign disease that would otherwise go undetected, and possible misdiagnosis of a benign lesion as malignant".[^nci]

**Why the incidence figures are shakier than they look.** The NCI notes that "A precise estimate of the total number and incidence rate of keratinocyte carcinoma is not possible because reporting to cancer registries is not required", and that of the observed rise, "At least some of this increase may be attributable to increased skin cancer awareness and resultant increasing investigation and biopsy of skin lesions".[^nci] More looking finds more disease, which is the same mechanism as the overdiagnosis harm above.

**What this does not settle.** None of this speaks to people at elevated risk, who are a different population from the asymptomatic general public, and the screening question is decided with a clinician rather than from a page like this one. There is no tier list on this page because the graded interventions here are preventive rather than diagnostic.

## Prevention is where the graded evidence is

The intervention with real controlled evidence is sun protection, not screening. The filter-by-filter case is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], and the trial evidence that sunscreen reduces precancerous and cancerous lesions is set out on [[actinic-keratosis]] and the studies linked from it. For the one topical drug approved to treat field sun damage, see [[fluorouracil-topical]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Skin cancers are diagnosed by examination and biopsy, not by appearance alone or by an app, and any lesion that worries you is worth a real assessment.
