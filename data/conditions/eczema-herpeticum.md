Eczema herpeticum is herpes simplex virus spreading across skin already affected by eczema. A 2026 case report describes it, using its other name, as "a dermatologic emergency characterized by the abrupt onset of painful monomorphic vesiculopustules with potential for rapid dissemination", with atopic dermatitis "the main predisposing condition due to skin barrier dysfunction and impaired antiviral immunity".[^case] The underlying disease is on [[atopic-dermatitis]].

> **This is the one page on this site where the right action is to stop reading and get seen today.** Nothing here is a skincare problem. The cited guidance is that "Current recommendations emphasize immediate systemic acyclovir based on clinical suspicion, without awaiting laboratory confirmation", and the reason given for urgency is that delay "may result in avoidable complications, including ocular involvement and systemic disease".[^case] If you have eczema and a painful, rapidly spreading blistering rash with fever, that is an urgent medical assessment, not a product decision.

## How to know you have this

The pattern that distinguishes it from an ordinary eczema flare is **pain, speed, and uniformity**. The general description above is "abrupt onset of painful monomorphic vesiculopustules", monomorphic meaning the lesions all look alike, unlike the mixed appearance of eczema itself.[^case]

In the reported case, a 4-year-old with moderate atopic dermatitis presented after "a 6-day history of fever, malaise, and a rapidly progressive vesiculopustular eruption involving both eczematous and previously unaffected skin", and examination found "widespread painful monomorphic umbilicated vesiculopustules with hemorrhagic crusts and mild bilateral conjunctival injection".[^case] Two details in that case are worth carrying. The rash crossed onto **skin that had not been eczematous**, and the eye involvement was present but mild, which is the complication the urgency is about.

**The diagnostic trap is being treated for the wrong infection.** In that case the child "had initially received oral amoxicillin-clavulanate for presumed bacterial superinfection without improvement" before the viral cause was recognised.[^case] Infected-looking eczema is commonly bacterial, so an antibiotic is a reasonable first thought; a flare that does not respond to one, and hurts, deserves reconsideration rather than a second antibiotic.

## The Evidence

**Why eczema specifically.** A 2025 review of infections in atopic dermatitis explains that patients "have an increased risk of bacterial, viral and fungal infections of the skin, mainly due to impaired barrier function, altered immune response and frequent scratching", and names the two commonest pathogens as "Staphylococcus aureus and herpes simplex virus", which between them cause "impetigo, folliculitis, abscesses, eczema herpeticum and other complications".[^napolitano] So this is not a rare curiosity: the virus behind it is one of the two organisms that most often infect eczematous skin.

**Treatment choice for the eczema itself can matter.** The same review states that "Certain systemic treatments for AD, such as mycophenolate mofetil and Janus kinase inhibitors, increase the risk of viral infections", and concludes that "Preventing and limiting the development of infections are important considerations in choosing an AD treatment".[^napolitano] **That review was funded by Sanofi and two of its authors are Sanofi employees**, and Sanofi markets a competing non-JAK biologic for atopic dermatitis, so the finding deserves independent corroboration rather than acceptance on its own.[^napolitano]

**It has that corroboration, from a regulator rather than a sponsor.** The FDA label for the topical JAK inhibitor on [[delgocitinib]] warns that the drug "may increase the risk of infection" and records that "Eczema herpeticum was observed in a subject treated topically with ANZUPGO".[^anzupgo] A manufacturer's own approved labelling naming this specific complication is stronger evidence of the link than a sponsored review, and the two agree.

**No tier list on this page.** There is nothing to rank: the treatment is systemic antiviral therapy started on suspicion by a clinician, not a choice among graded skincare options. The eczema-management options that do get graded are on [[atopic-dermatitis]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. The sources cited here call eczema herpeticum a dermatologic emergency and advise starting treatment on clinical suspicion; if that describes your situation, seek care the same day rather than researching further.
