Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory condition of the central face, described in the Cochrane systematic review as "a common chronic skin condition affecting the face" whose features include flushing, persistent redness, visible blood vessels, and inflammatory bumps.[^cochrane2015] It relapses and remits over years, it clusters on the cheeks, nose, chin, and forehead, and it has no cure: the treatments with the best evidence reduce specific features rather than resolve the disease, and which treatment fits is decided by which features a person actually has.[^vanzuuren2019][^schaller2017] This page maps the condition at a high level. The deep clinical and treatment detail lives on the subtype pages linked below.

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians look for, described so a reader can recognize the pattern rather than self-diagnose. The global ROSacea COnsensus (ROSCO) panel is explicit that rosacea is identified from a pattern of features on the central face rather than from any single lesion or test, and only a clinician can make the diagnosis.[^tan2017]

- **Lasting redness across the middle of the face is the hallmark.** The one feature the ROSCO panel judged able to point to rosacea on its own is "persistent, centrofacial erythema associated with periodic intensification": a background redness over the cheeks, nose, chin, and forehead that periodically flares darker.[^tan2017] Skin thickening (phyma), most familiar as an enlarged, bumpy nose, is the other feature the panel counted as independently diagnostic.[^tan2017]
- **Flushing that comes and goes.** Easy blushing or flushing, a transient wave of warmth and redness that settles and returns, is one of the common associated features.[^tan2017][^cochrane2015]
- **Visible small blood vessels.** Fine dilated vessels (telangiectasia) can show through the skin of the cheeks and nose.[^tan2017]
- **Small inflammatory bumps.** Some people develop red bumps (papules) and pus-topped spots (pustules) on the red background.[^tan2017][^cochrane2015]
- **Burning, stinging, or dryness.** The affected skin can burn or sting and can look dry; the National Rosacea Society's standard classification counts burning or stinging among rosacea's secondary features.[^wilkin2002]
- **The eyes can be involved.** Dryness, irritation, and inflamed lid margins (ocular rosacea) can occur with or without the skin changes.[^tan2017]

Rosacea tends to relapse and remit over years rather than clear in days, and it stays clustered on the central face. The Cochrane reviewers' plain description gathers the range as flushing, redness, pimples, pustules, dilated blood vessels, eye involvement, and, in some people, thickening of the skin with enlargement of the nose.[^cochrane2015]

**Telling it apart from look-alikes.** The comparison people most often need is with acne. A StatPearls review states that "rosacea lacks comedones, helping to differentiate it from true acne," so the clearest difference is what rosacea does not have: blackheads and whiteheads.[^statpearls] Rosacea also sits on the background of persistent central-face redness described above, which ordinary acne does not.[^tan2017] The blackheads and whiteheads of ordinary acne are covered on [[acne]]. Because the features overlap and no single sign is decisive, separating rosacea from its look-alikes still calls for clinical examination.[^tan2017]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Rosacea is a *medical* condition: it can involve the eyes (ocular rosacea), and the thickening of skin known as phyma can be disfiguring, so evaluation by a board-certified dermatologist is appropriate, particularly for eye symptoms or skin thickening. Several of the interventions with the strongest evidence are *prescription drugs that fall outside this page's skincare scope*, including topical metronidazole, topical ivermectin, topical brimonidine, topical oxymetazoline, and oral doxycycline and isotretinoin.[^vanzuuren2019] They are named here so readers know they exist; they require a prescriber and are graded, where in scope, on the linked subtype pages rather than here. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## Who Gets It

The most rigorous prevalence estimate comes from a 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis that pooled 41 populations totaling about 26.5 million people.[^gether2018] It found that "5·46% of the adult population is affected" in the general population (95% CI 4.91-6.04), with a lower figure of 2.39% (95% CI 1.56-3.39) among dermatology outpatients.[^gether2018] Prevalence was higher in women (5.41%, 95% CI 3.85-7.23) than men (3.90%, 95% CI 3.04-4.87), and rosacea affected "mostly those aged 45-60 years."[^gether2018]

Two caveats belong with those numbers. The estimate depends heavily on how rosacea is ascertained: the authors found that questionnaire-based self-report gave higher figures than diagnosis by clinical examination, "suggesting a low specificity of questionnaires based on symptoms."[^gether2018] And the underlying data are geographically skewed, with 22 of the 41 populations drawn from Europe and comparatively few from Africa, Asia, or South America, so the global figure rests mainly on lighter-skinned European populations.[^gether2018]

## How Rosacea Is Classified: Subtypes Versus Phenotypes

The classification of rosacea changed in a way that matters for how the condition is described, and older and newer sources use different language for the same disease.

**The 2002 subtype system.** As the National Rosacea Society Expert Committee recounts, "In 2002, the National Rosacea Society assembled an expert committee to develop the first standard classification of rosacea."[^gallo2018] That original scheme (Wilkin and colleagues) sorted rosacea into four subtypes plus a variant:[^wilkin2002][^gallo2018]

- **Subtype 1, erythematotelangiectatic:** persistent central-face redness with flushing and visible vessels. This is the "Type 1" a reader will see referenced. Covered in depth on [[rosacea-erythematotelangiectatic]], which maps the redness-and-flushing presentation and what the controlled evidence supports for it (Type 1).
- **Subtype 2, papulopustular:** the inflammatory-bump presentation, with papules and pustules on a background of redness. This is "Type 2." Covered in depth on [[rosacea-papulopustular]], which maps the acne-like inflammatory presentation and its best-evidenced topical and oral options (Type 2).
- **Subtype 3, phymatous:** skin thickening and irregular surface changes, most familiar as rhinophyma of the nose.
- **Subtype 4, ocular:** eye and eyelid involvement, which can occur with or without the skin features.

A granulomatous variant sat outside the four subtypes.[^wilkin2002] No deep-dive pages exist yet for the phymatous and ocular presentations.

**The 2017 shift to phenotypes.** The subtype system had a practical problem: real patients often carry features that span more than one subtype at the same time, so a rigid box does not fit them. Two independent expert groups moved away from fixed subtypes in 2017 toward classifying by *phenotype* (the individual features a person actually has). The ROSCO panel reported that "Consensus was achieved for transitioning to a phenotype-based approach to rosacea diagnosis and classification."[^tan2017] The National Rosacea Society's own 2017 update likewise proposed a classification "based on phenotypes linked to our increased understanding of disease pathophysiology," on the reasoning that "a consistent multivariate disease process underlies the various clinical manifestations of this disorder."[^gallo2018] The Cochrane reviewers summarized the whole trajectory in one line: "Classification of rosacea has evolved from subtyping to phenotyping."[^vanzuuren2019]

The two schemes are not in conflict so much as different lenses. The subtype names remain a useful shorthand, and they are how many products and readers still refer to rosacea, which is why the Type 1 and Type 2 pages above use them. The phenotype approach is how current consensus guides diagnosis and treatment: assess each feature a person has and treat those features.[^tan2017][^schaller2017]

## What Is Happening in the Skin

Rosacea's cause is not settled, and consensus now frames it as "a consistent multivariate disease process," meaning several interacting mechanisms rather than one.[^gallo2018] This page gives the overview; the subtype pages go deeper on the mechanisms relevant to each presentation.

- **Innate immune activation.** The best-characterized molecular finding is an overactive innate immune response. Yamasaki and colleagues showed that "individuals with rosacea express abnormally high levels of cathelicidin in their facial skin," and that these cathelicidin peptides arise from "an increase in stratum corneum tryptic enzyme (SCTE) in the epidermis" (the protease also known as kallikrein-5).[^yamasaki2007] In mouse skin the same peptides and protease drove inflammation, leading the authors to conclude that "an exacerbated innate immune response can reproduce elements of this disease."[^yamasaki2007] This cathelicidin and kallikrein-5 cascade is the mechanism that topical azelaic acid appears to interrupt, detailed on [[azelaic-acid]].
- **Neurovascular dysregulation.** The flushing, persistent erythema, and visible vessels reflect dysregulated blood-vessel and nerve signaling in facial skin, one of the elements the National Rosacea Society groups under the multivariate disease process.[^gallo2018]
- **Demodex mites.** A meta-analysis of 23 case-control studies (1,513 patients) found rosacea patients far more likely to carry facial *Demodex* mites than controls (odds ratio 9.039, 95% CI 4.827-16.925) and to have higher mite density.[^chang2017] The authors were careful that "a causal relationship could not be established by case-control studies," but concluded "Demodex mites may play a role in both erythematotelangiectatic rosacea and papulopustular rosacea."[^chang2017] This is part of why an antiparasitic topical (ivermectin) has strong evidence for the inflammatory form.[^vanzuuren2019]

## What Guidelines Recommend, at a High Level

Rosacea management follows from the phenotype approach: the ROSCO treatment consensus concluded that "Rosacea diagnosis and treatment should be based on clinical presentation," matching a treatment to each feature a person has rather than to a subtype label.[^schaller2017] The panel identified first-line treatments for the individual features (transient and persistent erythema, papules and pustules, telangiectasia, and phyma) and stated that these are "underpinned by general skincare measures."[^schaller2017] The National Rosacea Society's 2019 management update frames the same individualized principle, encouraging clinicians to "assess and treat the disorder as it may occur in each individual."[^thiboutot2020] Because features can coexist, the ROSCO panel noted that multiple features in one patient can be treated with more than one agent at the same time.[^schaller2017]

On which specific agents carry the evidence, the reader is best served by the feature-specific pages. For the inflammatory (papulopustular) form, azelaic acid is one of the few topicals with high-certainty evidence, and it is graded in full, indication by indication, on [[azelaic-acid]] (a well-tolerated dicarboxylic acid with strong controlled evidence for inflammatory rosacea).[^vanzuuren2019] The general principle of daily broad-spectrum photoprotection, one of the few skincare practices with randomized-trial support against hard endpoints, is covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

## What's Overhyped

Quarantined and discounted. Treat each claim below as unsupported unless it is backed by the cited evidence.

- **A "cure."** Nothing in the two Cochrane-lineage reviews resolves rosacea; the interventions with the best evidence reduce specific features and are used on an ongoing basis.[^cochrane2015][^vanzuuren2019] The persistent redness responds to topical vasoconstrictors only while they are in use: the strongest such evidence is for reducing what the reviewers call "temporarily persistent erythema."[^vanzuuren2019]
- **A "rosacea diet" that cures the disease.** The interventions with the strongest evidence in the systematic reviews are specific topical drugs, oral drugs, and light-based therapy; no dietary regimen appears among the effective interventions the reviews pooled.[^cochrane2015][^vanzuuren2019] Individual triggers vary from person to person, which is one reason consensus stresses assessing each patient individually rather than prescribing a universal regimen.[^schaller2017]
- **Over-the-counter "redness relief" creams that fix rosacea.** The topical agents shown to reduce persistent facial redness with high-certainty evidence, brimonidine and (at moderate certainty) oxymetazoline, are prescription vasoconstrictors, and they reduce redness temporarily rather than modify the disease.[^vanzuuren2019] A cosmetic redness cream is not equivalent to those drugs and does not act on the underlying inflammation.

## The Evidence

Two systematic reviews by the same Cochrane team anchor the top-level evidence and reach the same conclusion from different vintages of trials.

**Cochrane 2015 (van Zuuren and colleagues).** The full Cochrane review included 106 randomized controlled trials comprising 13,631 participants and concluded there was "high quality evidence to support the effectiveness of topical azelaic acid, topical ivermectin, brimonidine, doxycycline and isotretinoin for rosacea," with moderate-quality evidence for topical metronidazole and oral tetracycline.[^cochrane2015]

**Cochrane-lineage 2019 update (van Zuuren and colleagues).** The updated systematic review added 46 new studies for a total of 152 trials and 20,944 participants, and reorganized its findings around the phenotype approach.[^vanzuuren2019] Its GRADE conclusions separate the evidence by feature. For persistent facial redness there was "high-certainty evidence for topical brimonidine" and moderate certainty for topical oxymetazoline; for erythema and telangiectasia there was low-to-moderate-certainty evidence for laser and intense pulsed light.[^vanzuuren2019] For papules and pustules there was "high-certainty evidence for topical azelaic acid and topical ivermectin," moderate-to-high certainty for doxycycline 40 mg modified release and for isotretinoin, and moderate certainty for topical metronidazole.[^vanzuuren2019] For ocular rosacea there was moderate-certainty evidence that oral omega-3 fatty acids were effective.[^vanzuuren2019]

The synthesis to carry away is that the evidence is feature-specific: a treatment earns its certainty rating for a particular feature (redness, or bumps, or eye symptoms), not for "rosacea" as a whole, which is exactly why current consensus treats by phenotype.[^vanzuuren2019][^schaller2017] The treatment specifics for each presentation are on the subtype pages: the redness-and-flushing form on [[rosacea-erythematotelangiectatic]] (Type 1) and the inflammatory bump form on [[rosacea-papulopustular]] (Type 2).
