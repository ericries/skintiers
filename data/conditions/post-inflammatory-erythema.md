Post-inflammatory erythema (PIE) is the flat pink or red mark left behind after an acne lesion or other skin inflammation heals. DermNet describes it as "more common in lighter skin" and explains it is "thought to relate to microvascular changes and epidermal thinning of the skin during wound healing."[^dermnet] That is the key distinction from its counterpart, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH): PIE is a vascular mark (dilated or damaged capillaries showing through thin skin), not a pigment mark, and creams built to block melanin are aimed at the wrong target.

## How to know you have this

PIE looks like flat pink-to-red spots where an acne lesion, cut, or other inflamed spot used to be. It blanches (pales) under pressure because it comes from blood vessels near the surface, not from deposited pigment. It sits alongside PIH and true atrophic scarring as one of the three things people lump together as "acne scars," and DermNet groups it explicitly as one of the "postinflammatory skin colour changes" that "may arise prior to or in conjunction with acne scar formation," alongside hyperpigmentation and hypopigmentation.[^dermnet] It is most visible on lighter skin tones, where the pink or red reads clearly against the surrounding skin; on darker skin the same inflammation more often produces the brown marks of PIH instead.[^lopez] A recent clinical review notes both marks "can persist for months or even years" and can weigh on quality of life "sometimes to a greater extent than active acne itself."[^lopez]

## What is happening in the skin

The redness comes from the wound-healing response itself: inflamed acne damages and dilates the small vessels in the upper dermis, and the epidermis over the healing lesion also thins, which lets that vascular redness show through more than usual.[^dermnet] This is a fundamentally different mechanism from PIH, where inflammatory mediators trigger melanocytes to overproduce and deposit melanin in the surrounding keratinocytes.[^dermnet] Because PIE is vascular, not pigmented, the in-office fix that actually targets the mechanism is a vascular (pulsed-dye or similar) laser that closes down the abnormal vessels, not a topical brightener. That procedure sits outside this site's topical scope, so it isn't tier-listed here.

## What actually works

Left alone, PIE usually fades on its own as the vessels normalize and the epidermis thickens back up, typically over a period of months.[^dermnet][^lopez] The most useful thing a topical routine can do is protect that natural fading process and stop new marks from forming: daily [[sunscreen-uv-filters|sunscreen]], since UV exposure worsens and prolongs post-inflammatory redness, and active treatment of ongoing [[acne]] so new lesions stop creating new marks. Beyond that, [[azelaic-acid]] has genuine anti-inflammatory and anti-redness evidence from rosacea, and [[retinoids]] have some support for post-acne marks generally by speeding turnover, though the published evidence for both leans more toward PIH than PIE specifically.[^lopez] [[niacinamide]] is a gentler adjunct with the weakest evidence of the group. None of these close down a dilated vessel; that is what makes persistent PIE, after conservative treatment has been given months, a candidate for vascular laser referral rather than another cream.

## The Evidence

DermNet's acne scarring topic page is the anchor for the clinical definition and mechanism: PIE is "thought to relate to microvascular changes and epidermal thinning of the skin during wound healing," distinct from PIH's melanin deposition.[^dermnet] A 2026 practical review in the American Journal of Clinical Dermatology frames post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and post-inflammatory erythema together as common, often-lingering sequelae of acne, noting they "can persist for months or even years" and listing topical treatment options (azelaic acid, retinoids, antioxidants, hydroquinone) alongside photoprotection as central to management, while flagging energy-based devices such as lasers as additional therapeutic avenues.[^lopez] That review is written primarily around the pigmented mark (PIH); it is the best recent citation available for PIE but its topical evidence base leans toward PIH, which is why the topicals below are graded moderate-to-minimal rather than strong.

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. PIE itself is a benign cosmetic mark that usually fades on its own, but a red spot that is new, growing, changing, or that you are not certain is a post-acne mark should be looked at by a board-certified dermatologist rather than assumed. The in-office treatment that actually targets the dilated vessels behind persistent PIE is a vascular laser (such as pulsed-dye), a procedure that requires a clinician and falls outside this page's topical scope; it is named here so readers know it exists. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.
