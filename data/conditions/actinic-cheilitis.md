Actinic cheilitis is precancerous sun damage on the lip, almost always the lower one. What it looks like, why it matters and how to avoid it are on [[lip-care]], which also carries the malignant transformation figures; this page covers the part that page stops at, which is what the treatments actually achieve.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Every intervention below is physician-administered or prescription-only, and none of it is a skincare step. A persistently dry, scaly or eroded lower lip in someone with a lot of sun exposure is a reason to be examined, not to try another balm.

## How to know you have this

Not by reading. The appearance, the risk factors and the warning signs of progression are set out on [[lip-care]], and the honest summary there is that cheilitis from many different causes looks alike. The sibling condition on ordinary sun-exposed skin is [[actinic-keratosis]], and the examination case is on [[skin-cancer-screening]].

## The Evidence

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis is the best available synthesis, registered in advance as "A pre-defined protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021225182)", searching Medline, Embase and Central, with GRADE applied to the randomised evidence.[^sr] It pooled "36 studies facilitated an evaluation of 614 participants for PCR, and 430 patients for PRR", meaning participant clearance rate and participant recurrence rate.[^sr]

**Clearance ranges from about half to nearly all, depending on the method.** "Diclofenac showed the lowest PCR (0.53, 95% confidence interval (CI) [0.41; 0.66]), while CO2 laser showed the highest PCR (0.97, 95% CI [0.90; 0.99])."[^sr] On recurrence the ordering changes: "For PRR, Er:YAG laser showed the highest rates (0.14, 95% CI [0.08; 0.21]), and imiquimod the lowest (0.00, 95% CI [0.00; 0.06])."[^sr] So the treatment that clears the most and the treatment that recurs the least are not the same treatment, which is the single most useful thing this review says.

**Read those clearance figures as single-arm pools, not as a race.** The review states its own method: "The generalized linear mixed model was used to pool proportions for uncontrolled studies."[^sr] A 0.97 clearance rate drawn from uncontrolled studies is not the same kind of number as a result from a head-to-head trial, and nothing in the review puts CO2 laser and diclofenac in the same randomised comparison. The authors say as much, that "Due to qualitative data, comparability was limited, highlighting the need for individualized treatment."[^sr]

**One genuine randomised comparison came out of it.** Priming photodynamic therapy with an ablative fractional laser beat photodynamic therapy alone on recurrence: the odds ratio was 0.22 (95% CI 0.06 to 0.82) for Er:YAG laser-primed methyl-aminolevulinate PDT against MAL-PDT by itself.[^sr] That is a pairwise meta-analysis of randomised trials rather than a pooled proportion, and it is the firmest finding on the page.

**Side effects separate the two lasers.** "The CO2 laser showed fewer local side effects than the Er:YAG laser, while PDTs caused more skin reactions."[^sr] Adverse events were analysed qualitatively rather than pooled, so there is no rate to quote alongside that ordering.

**A conflict worth naming.** One author of the review declares honoraria for lectures from Biofrontera, a company in the photodynamic therapy business, described as outside the submitted work; several others declare consulting and speaking relationships with pharmaceutical companies.[^sr] The review is methodologically careful and PROSPERO-registered, and that disclosure belongs next to a result that assesses PDT.

**Nothing here is a product you can buy.** None of the treatments named above has a page on this site yet, because all of them are clinic procedures or prescription drugs rather than the over-the-counter products this site grades. The one thing a reader controls is upstream: lip photoprotection, on [[lip-care]].
