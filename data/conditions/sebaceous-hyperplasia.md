Small soft yellowish bumps, usually on the forehead, nose and cheeks, where an oil gland has enlarged around a central dip. Harmless, persistent, and not treatable with anything you can buy. **Bottom line:** the decision is whether to have them removed by a procedure, and the honest backdrop is that specialists describe the treatment landscape as thin.

> **The researchers say so themselves.** A 2025 Chinese treatment study opens by stating that sebaceous hyperplasia "is a prevalent comorbidity in dermatology" but that "there is a notable absence of an effective and safe treatment modality in current clinical practice".[^rf] That is the field's own assessment, published in the course of trying to improve on it.

## How to know you have this

Soft, yellow to skin-coloured papules a few millimetres across, often with a dimple in the middle, appearing from middle age onward. In the study below, the mean age of onset was "42.9 ± 9.6 years" and men outnumbered women at a ratio of 1.8 to 1.[^rf] They persist rather than resolve.

The lesions this site covers alongside it are [[seborrhoeic-keratosis]], [[skin-tags]], [[milia]] and [[cherry-angioma]]. A bump you cannot confidently identify is a reason to be examined rather than to match it against a web page.

## The medical point most people miss

A lesion that looks like sebaceous hyperplasia on the surface is not guaranteed to be only that underneath. A 2026 case report describes a nasal arteriovenous malformation whose superficial biopsy showed sebaceous hyperplasia, and the authors draw the general lesson: "Persistent facial lesions with benign superficial histopathology should prompt suspicion of deeper vascular anomalies", because a superficial biopsy "may fail to detect underlying arteriovenous malformations, leading to delayed diagnosis and inappropriate management".[^avm]

That is one case and not a reason for alarm about an ordinary bump. It is a reason to take a persistent, changing or unusual facial lesion to a clinician rather than deciding for yourself that it is cosmetic.

## Treatment

**Nothing on a shelf.** A search of the FDA's National Drug Code directory on 2026-11-17 for products whose brand name contains `sebaceous` returned no records.[^ndc] No topical is sold for this under a name that advertises the condition.

**Procedural, with one recent study worth reading carefully.** The radiofrequency study above reported that "Forty-four patients (97.8%) achieved optimal outcomes", on an average of "2.2 ± 0.8" treatments, with one relapse over six months.[^rf] That number will look conclusive and should not. It is a retrospective single-arm series of 45 patients at one hospital, with no control group and no comparison treatment, and "optimal" is the authors' own category for more than 90% lesion regression judged by dermoscopy rather than by how the patient thinks their face looks. Every patient got redness, and 8.9% got crusting.[^rf]

**Oral isotretinoin appears in the literature.** A 2026 case series reports panfacial Fordyce-like sebaceous hyperplasia "successfully treated with oral isotretinoin".[^iso] PubMed carries no abstract for it, so this page can report the title's claim and nothing further: no patient count, no dose, no duration, no relapse data. [[isotretinoin]] covers the drug, which is not a casual prescription.

No tier list appears here, because no product on this site is graded for treating sebaceous hyperplasia and there is nothing to rank.

## The Evidence

Three papers, all read from PubMed on 2026-11-17: one retrospective treatment series with numbers, one case report about a diagnostic pitfall, and one case series for which only a title is available.[^rf][^avm][^iso] That is a thin literature, which matches what the first of them says about the field.
