Psoriasis in a skin fold looks nothing like psoriasis on an elbow. The armpit, groin, under-breast and genital versions are smooth, shiny and red rather than thick and scaly, because the moisture and friction of a fold prevent the scale that makes plaque psoriasis recognisable. The general disease is on [[psoriasis]], which does not cover this variant, so this page sticks to what differs.

> **The treatment evidence for this variant is thin, and its own systematic review says so.** The one systematic review found for this page opens by noting that inverse psoriasis "often requires a targeted treatment strategy due to the inherent sensitivity, thinness, and occlusion of flexural areas. However, most treatment recommendations are based on anecdotal evidence."[^review] Its conclusion is blunter still, that "There is a paucity of high-quality studies on which to base treatment recommendations, especially with regard to the role of systemic and biologic therapies."[^review]

## How to know you have this

Not by looking, and that is the practical point of this page. A red, moist, sharply bordered patch in a fold is the shared presentation of at least three different problems: this, [[candidal-intertrigo]], and [[tinea-cruris-jock-itch]]. They are a psoriasis, a yeast infection and a dermatophyte infection respectively, and they are treated with three different drug classes.

Scale is the usual clue for psoriasis and it is the clue a fold removes. The review describes the affected regions as axillary, inframammary, facial and anogenital.[^review] A case report in the literature illustrates how hard this can be: an 84-year-old woman with chronic lesions across the lower abdomen, external genitals, perianal region, groin and inner thighs was examined repeatedly, and neither colposcopy with a dermatovenerological consultation nor histopathology reached a conclusion; only specialist dermatopathology arrived at inverse psoriasis.[^case] One case is not evidence of how often that happens, and it is cited here only as an illustration that the diagnosis can resist ordinary workup.

If you have a persistent fold rash, the useful question for a clinician is which of the three it is, because treating the wrong one does nothing.

## The Evidence

One systematic review, searched in October 2018 across PubMed, Scopus, Embase and the Cochrane Library, screened 340 results and included 14 studies covering more than 1,000 patients with mild to severe psoriasis in flexural and genital sites. Inclusion required a prospective design and at least 10 patients.[^review]

What those 14 studies supported, by count: topical immunomodulators in 7, vitamin D analogs in 4, topical corticosteroids in 3, antiseptics in 2 and biologics in 1.[^review] The review reports no pooled effect size, no comparison between classes and no numeric result of any kind in its abstract, which is why the ranking above is by weight of supporting studies rather than by how well anything worked. Its own verdict on those studies is that they are "few" and "many of which are of low evidence quality", and that "more randomized controlled trials are needed".[^review]
