Papulopustular rosacea (PPR), historically called Type 2 or subtype 2 rosacea, is the inflammatory form of the disease: crops of small red bumps (papules) and pus-topped spots (pustules) sitting on a background of persistent redness across the central face. It produces no blackheads or whiteheads, which is the clearest way it separates from acne, and it adds visible inflammatory lesions on top of the flushing and redness that define the Type 1 (erythematotelangiectatic) form.

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians look for, described so a reader can recognize them, not a way to self-diagnose. Rosacea is diagnosed clinically, from the pattern of features on the central face, and only a doctor can make that call.[^wilkin][^rosco]

Papulopustular rosacea is recognized by inflammatory bumps on a red background. The 2002 National Rosacea Society classification defined this subtype as persistent central-facial redness with transient papules or pustules, or both, in a central-facial distribution.[^wilkin] In everyday terms:

- **Red bumps and pus-filled spots.** Small dome-shaped red bumps (papules) and whitehead-like spots topped with a bead of pus (pustules) come up in crops, usually on the cheeks, nose, chin, and forehead.[^wilkin][^yamasaki]
- **On a flushed, red background.** The bumps sit on skin that stays pink or red between flares. That persistent central-facial redness is the feature the global ROSCO consensus treats as the anchor of a rosacea diagnosis, and fine visible vessels (telangiectasia) may show through it.[^rosco][^wilkin]
- **How it feels.** The affected skin often burns or stings and is easily irritated by products that other people tolerate; the National Rosacea Society lists these sensory symptoms among the features of the condition.[^wilkin]
- **It comes and goes, and does not clear on its own.** Rosacea is chronic: the redness persists with periodic intensification rather than settling in a few days, which is part of why the ROSCO panel treats persistent centrofacial erythema as diagnostic on its own.[^rosco]

**How it differs from acne.** The most useful distinction is what is *absent*: papulopustular rosacea produces papules and pustules with no comedones, and it sits on a base of fixed central-face redness and flushing that acne does not have. Acne, by contrast, is built on the comedo (the clogged follicle that becomes a blackhead or whitehead) and tends to spread across the jaw, chest, and back; that comedonal disease and its evidence-ranked topicals are set out, with their sources, on [[acne]].

**How it differs from Type 1 rosacea.** The erythematotelangiectatic form (Type 1) is dominated by flushing, persistent redness, and visible small vessels, with few or no inflammatory lesions; papulopustular rosacea adds the papules and pustules on top. The distinction guides treatment, because the drugs that clear inflammatory lesions do little for background redness, and the drugs that reduce redness do little for the lesions. The redness-and-flushing subtype, and what topical skincare and light devices can do for it, is covered on [[rosacea-erythematotelangiectatic]].

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Rosacea is a *medical* condition, and papulopustular rosacea that is moderate, severe, or not responding to topicals is generally evaluated by a board-certified dermatologist. Several of the most effective options named here are prescription-only: topical ivermectin, azelaic acid, and metronidazole, oral doxycycline, and (for severe or refractory disease) oral isotretinoin, which carries its own risks and monitoring. Rosacea can also involve the eyes (ocular rosacea) and, in some people, progress to thickening of the skin (phymatous change); both are reasons to see a clinician. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

> The inflammatory lesions of papulopustular rosacea are among the more treatable parts of rosacea. A Cochrane systematic review of 106 randomized trials rated topical ivermectin and azelaic acid effective on high-quality evidence, topical metronidazole on moderate-quality evidence, and low-dose oral doxycycline on high-quality evidence.[^cochrane] A separate lever is worth naming up front: the vasoconstrictor drugs sold for rosacea redness (brimonidine, oxymetazoline) act on the erythema, not on the bumps and pustules.[^cochrane][^oracea]

## Why It Happens

The papulopustular lesions are understood as an over-active innate immune response in facial skin, and two threads of evidence anchor that model.

**The cathelicidin and serine-protease cascade.** In a foundational mechanistic study, people with rosacea were shown to "express abnormally high levels of cathelicidin in their facial skin," and the cathelicidin fragments found in rosacea differed from the normal ones because of an increase in a skin serine protease the authors called stratum corneum tryptic enzyme (later identified as kallikrein-5, KLK5).[^yamasaki] In mice, adding those rosacea-type cathelicidin peptides or raising protease activity each increased skin inflammation, and the authors concluded that "an exacerbated innate immune response can reproduce elements of this disease."[^yamasaki] A separate study of azelaic acid confirmed the same actors from the treatment side: it found that "excess cathelicidin and kallikrein 5 (KLK5) have been hypothesized to play a role" and that both fall during treatment, reporting that "cathelicidin and KLK5 decrease in association with AZA exposure."[^coda] Cathelicidin (its active peptide is LL-37) and KLK5 form a self-amplifying loop that drives the visible inflammation.

**Demodex mites.** The follicular mite *Demodex folliculorum* is denser in rosacea skin. A meta-analysis of 23 case-control studies (1,513 patients with rosacea) found they had "significantly higher prevalence and degrees of Demodex mite infestation" than controls (odds ratio for infestation 9.039, 95% CI 4.827 to 16.925), and the raised mite density held specifically for the papulopustular subtype (standardized mean difference 2.804, 95% CI 1.464 to 4.145).[^demodex] The authors concluded that "Demodex mites may play a role in both erythematotelangiectatic rosacea and papulopustular rosacea," while cautioning that case-control data cannot prove the mites cause the disease rather than thriving in already-inflamed skin.[^demodex] This mite association is part of why an anti-mite drug (ivermectin) turns out to be one of the most effective topicals.

These inflammatory mechanisms overlap with the neurovascular abnormality behind the flushing and redness, which is why the erythema and the lesions travel together but respond to different drugs.

## What Actually Works

The anchor is the abridged updated Cochrane review of interventions for rosacea, which pooled 106 RCTs (13,631 participants) and attached GRADE certainty ratings to each treatment.[^cochrane] The head-to-head RCTs and FDA labels below fill in the effect sizes.

**1. Topical ivermectin 1% cream (Soolantra). Effect: notable · Evidence: solid.** The best-supported topical for the inflammatory lesions, combining anti-mite and anti-inflammatory action. The Cochrane review found "topical ivermectin was more effective than placebo based on two studies (high-quality evidence)," and "slightly more effective than metronidazole in one study."[^cochrane] In the two phase 3 vehicle-controlled trials, once-daily ivermectin 1% reduced inflammatory lesions by 76.0% and 75.0% versus 50.0% for vehicle, and reached treatment success (Investigator's Global Assessment of "clear" or "almost clear") in 38.4% and 40.1% of patients versus 11.6% and 18.8% for vehicle (both P<.001).[^ivm-vc] In a separate 962-patient head-to-head trial, ivermectin 1% once daily beat metronidazole 0.75% twice daily on lesion reduction (83.0% vs 73.7%, P<0.001) and on the share of patients rated clear or almost clear (84.9% vs 75.4%, P<0.001).[^ivm-mtz] It is FDA-approved "for the treatment of inflammatory lesions of rosacea."[^soolantra]

**2. Topical azelaic acid 15%. Effect: notable · Evidence: solid.** Azelaic acid is a well-evidenced first-line topical for the inflammatory lesions of rosacea, on par with or better than metronidazole in head-to-head trials, and the Cochrane review rated the azelaic-acid-versus-placebo evidence high-quality.[^cochrane][^king][^elewski] Its full, indication-by-indication grade, its FDA approval for papulopustular rosacea, and the mechanism study linking it to the KLK5 and cathelicidin cascade are on [[azelaic-acid]]. In the head-to-head comparison it cut inflammatory lesions by 72.7% versus 55.8% for metronidazole 0.75% gel (P<.001), a "significant superiority over using 0.75% metronidazole gel."[^elewski]

**3. Topical metronidazole 0.75-1%. Effect: modest to notable · Evidence: moderate.** The longest-established topical, and still a common first-line choice. The Cochrane review pooled it as "more effective than placebo," but graded that evidence moderate-quality, a notch below ivermectin and azelaic acid, and the head-to-head trials place it behind both of those on lesion reduction.[^cochrane][^ivm-mtz][^elewski]

**4. Oral doxycycline 40 mg modified-release (Oracea). Effect: notable · Evidence: solid.** This is an *anti-inflammatory* dose, not an antibiotic dose. It is FDA-approved "for the treatment of only inflammatory lesions (papules and pustules) of rosacea," and its label states plainly that "this formulation of doxycycline has not been evaluated in the treatment or prevention of infections," because the 40 mg once-daily dose is designed to sit below the level that acts on bacteria.[^oracea] In two phase 3 placebo-controlled trials (n=269 active, 268 placebo, 16 weeks, baseline about 20 lesions), it reduced inflammatory lesions by 11.8 and 9.5 versus 5.9 and 4.3 for placebo (P<.001 for both), and the Cochrane review rated the doxycycline-40-mg-versus-placebo evidence high-quality.[^doxy][^cochrane] The label also notes "no meaningful effect was demonstrated for generalized erythema (redness) of rosacea," which again separates lesion treatment from redness treatment.[^oracea]

**5. Topical minocycline 1.5% foam (Zilxi / FMX103). Effect: modest to notable · Evidence: solid.** A newer topical antibiotic foam. In two phase 3 vehicle-controlled trials in moderate-to-severe papulopustular rosacea, it reduced inflammatory lesions more than vehicle (17.57 vs 15.65, P=.0031; and 18.54 vs 14.88, P<.0001) and reached higher treatment success (52.1% vs 43.0%, P=.0273; and 49.1% vs 39.0%, P=.0077); the authors concluded "FMX103 1.5% was efficacious for moderate to severe papulopustular rosacea."[^mino] The vehicle arms improved substantially too, so the drug-versus-vehicle gap is real but smaller than the raw improvement suggests.

**6. Oral isotretinoin (off-label, for severe or refractory disease). Effect: notable · Evidence: solid, but a specialist decision.** Low-dose isotretinoin is used off-label when papulopustular rosacea is severe or has not responded to the topicals and antibiotics above. The Cochrane review found "low-dose isotretinoin appeared to be slightly more effective than doxycycline 50-100 mg (high-quality evidence)."[^cochrane] It is a systemic retinoid with teratogenicity and other risks that require prescriber supervision and monitoring, which is why it is reserved rather than first-line. Other tetracyclines (oral tetracycline and standard-dose doxycycline or minocycline) also have evidence: Cochrane cited "moderate-quality evidence for the effectiveness of tetracycline based on two old studies," and rated oral minocycline 45 mg only "may be effective for papulopustular rosacea (low-quality evidence)."[^cochrane]

## What's Overhyped

Everything here is unsupported for the *papulopustular lesions* unless it also appears in The Evidence.

- **Vasoconstrictor "redness" drugs (brimonidine, oxymetazoline) for the bumps and pustules.** These topical alpha-adrenergic agonists constrict facial blood vessels and are approved for, and tested against, persistent facial *erythema*, not inflammatory lesions. In two phase 3 trials, brimonidine 0.5% gel showed "significantly greater efficacy vs. vehicle for the treatment of facial erythema," and the Cochrane review rated brimonidine "more effective than vehicle in reducing erythema in rosacea (high-quality evidence)."[^brimonidine][^cochrane] Oxymetazoline 1% cream is likewise "approved ... for the topical treatment of persistent facial erythema associated with rosacea in adults."[^oxymet] Their effect is temporary constriction of redness, and they do nothing documented for papules and pustules. The redness subtype they target is covered on [[rosacea-erythematotelangiectatic]].
- **Long-term broad-spectrum antibiotics at full antimicrobial doses.** The doxycycline evidence that supports rosacea treatment is for the sub-antimicrobial 40 mg anti-inflammatory dose, whose own FDA label states it "has not been evaluated in the treatment or prevention of infections."[^oracea] The field moved to that lower dose specifically to get the anti-inflammatory benefit without the antibacterial exposure of long-term full-dose antibiotics; the controlled evidence does not show that a higher antimicrobial dose clears lesions better.[^oracea][^doxy]
- **"Rosacea diet" cures.** None of the 106 randomized trials in the Cochrane review tested a diet as a treatment for rosacea; dietary changes are not among the interventions with controlled-trial evidence for clearing papulopustular lesions.[^cochrane]

## The Evidence

**The ranking anchor: the Cochrane review (van Zuuren and Fedorowicz, 2015).** This abridged, updated Cochrane systematic review pooled 106 RCTs (13,631 participants) and attached GRADE certainty ratings. For the topicals it reported that metronidazole and azelaic acid were both "more effective than placebo (moderate and high-quality evidence, respectively)," that "topical ivermectin was more effective than placebo based on two studies (high-quality evidence)" and "slightly more effective than metronidazole in one study," and that brimonidine was "more effective than vehicle in reducing erythema." For orals it found "high-quality evidence for doxycycline 40 mg compared with placebo," "moderate-quality evidence for the effectiveness of tetracycline," that oral "minocycline 45 mg may be effective for papulopustular rosacea (low-quality evidence)," and that "low-dose isotretinoin appeared to be slightly more effective than doxycycline 50-100 mg (high-quality evidence)."[^cochrane]

> Bottom line: across a large, independent evidence base, topical ivermectin and azelaic acid carry the strongest certainty among topicals, metronidazole sits a notch below, and low-dose oral doxycycline is the best-graded oral option, with isotretinoin reserved for severe disease.

**Ivermectin versus vehicle (Stein and colleagues, 2014).** Two identically designed, randomized, double-blind, 12-week studies of once-daily ivermectin 1% cream in moderate-to-severe PPR found treatment success (IGA clear or almost clear) of 38.4% and 40.1% versus 11.6% and 18.8% for vehicle, and inflammatory-lesion reductions of 76.0% and 75.0% versus 50.0% (all endpoints P<.001 from week 4). The authors concluded that "ivermectin 1% cream was effective and safe in treating inflammatory lesions of papulopustular rosacea."[^ivm-vc]

> Bottom line: against an inert vehicle, ivermectin roughly doubled to tripled the odds of clear or almost-clear skin and cut inflammatory lesions by about three-quarters.

**Ivermectin versus metronidazole (Taieb and colleagues, 2015).** A 962-patient, 16-week, investigator-blinded phase 3 trial compared once-daily ivermectin 1% against twice-daily metronidazole 0.75%. Ivermectin was "significantly superior" on inflammatory-lesion reduction (83.0% vs 73.7%, P<0.001) and on the share of patients clear or almost clear (84.9% vs 75.4%, P<0.001), with the gap visible by week 3.[^ivm-mtz]

> Bottom line: head-to-head, ivermectin beat the old first-line topical metronidazole by roughly 9 to 10 percentage points on both lesion clearance and global success.

**Azelaic acid versus metronidazole (Elewski and colleagues, 2003).** A 251-patient, 15-week, double-blind trial found azelaic acid 15% gel reduced inflammatory lesions by 72.7% versus 55.8% for metronidazole 0.75% gel (P<.001), with more patients rated improved on erythema (56% vs 42%, P=.02), a "significant superiority over using 0.75% metronidazole gel."[^elewski] The broader azelaic-acid picture is synthesized on [[azelaic-acid]], which grades it a solid first-line topical for inflammatory rosacea and cites the systematic review finding it "more effective than vehicle for rosacea, acne and melasma."[^king]

> Bottom line: azelaic acid outperformed metronidazole on both inflammatory lesions and redness in a direct trial, placing it alongside ivermectin at the top of the topical tier.

**Anti-inflammatory-dose doxycycline (Del Rosso and colleagues, 2007).** Two phase 3 placebo-controlled trials of 40 mg modified-release doxycycline once daily (n=269 active, 268 placebo, baseline about 20 lesions) reduced inflammatory lesions by 11.8 and 9.5 versus 5.9 and 4.3 for placebo at 16 weeks (P<.001 for both). The authors noted "doxycycline monotherapy at antimicrobial doses has been shown to be effective" but tested the lower sub-antimicrobial dose, concluding "once-daily anti-inflammatory dose doxycycline appears to be effective and safe."[^doxy]

> Bottom line: a dose too low to work as an antibiotic still roughly doubled the lesion reduction seen with placebo, which is the basis for treating rosacea without a full antibiotic course.

**Minocycline 1.5% foam (Gold and colleagues, 2020).** Two 12-week phase 3 vehicle-controlled trials in moderate-to-severe PPR found greater lesion reduction (17.57 vs 15.65, P=.0031; 18.54 vs 14.88, P<.0001) and higher IGA success (52.1% vs 43.0%, P=.0273; 49.1% vs 39.0%, P=.0077) for the minocycline foam than vehicle.[^mino]

> Bottom line: a topical minocycline foam beats its vehicle on inflammatory lesions, though the vehicle arms improved so much that the added benefit from the drug is modest.

Taken together, the syntheses converge: the Cochrane GRADE ratings (topical ivermectin and azelaic acid highest-certainty; metronidazole moderate; doxycycline 40 mg the best oral), the vehicle-controlled RCTs (each drug beats placebo on lesions), and the head-to-head trials (ivermectin beats metronidazole; azelaic acid beats metronidazole) all point the same way, while sharing much of the same underlying trial evidence.[^cochrane][^ivm-vc][^ivm-mtz][^elewski][^doxy][^mino]
