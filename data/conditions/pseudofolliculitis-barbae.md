Pseudofolliculitis barbae (PFB), or razor bumps, is a "chronic inflammatory condition characterized by follicular and perifollicular papules and pustules primarily affecting the beard and neck area."[^dalia] It is caused by shaved hairs re-entering the skin and triggering an inflammatory response, is more likely in people with curly hair, and predominantly affects people with skin of color, in whom it can leave significant [[hyperpigmentation]] and scarring.[^nussbaum][^dalia] It shows up on the [[mens-skincare-routine|men's shaving routine]] more than anywhere else, though anyone who shaves can get it.

> Razor bumps are a shaving problem before they are a skincare problem. Both treatment reviews agree the most effective approach is multifaceted and starts with changing how you shave (or stopping), because the hair itself is the cause; topical keratolytics are the medical mainstay that helps, not a substitute for the shaving change; and laser hair removal is the one option that can reduce the hair long-term. Skincare actives manage razor bumps, they do not cure a problem the razor keeps recreating.

## How to know you have this

PFB shows up as small inflamed bumps and pus-topped spots ("follicular and perifollicular papules and pustules") clustered in the shaved beard and neck area, appearing after shaving rather than as a spreading rash.[^dalia] It is more likely if your hair is curly, and most common and most visible in skin of color, where it can leave dark marks ([[hyperpigmentation]]) and scarring.[^nussbaum][^dalia] Because several follicular problems can look similar (including true [[folliculitis]]), persistent or scarring bumps are worth a clinician's evaluation rather than self-diagnosis.

## The Evidence

Both reviews describe PFB treatment as a multifaceted approach rather than a single fix.[^nussbaum][^dalia] The foundation is behavioral: as Nussbaum and Friedman put it, "the most effective treatments involve a multifaceted approach including behavioral changes in shaving habits as well as the use of topical therapies," and because the shaved hair itself is the cause, reducing or pausing shaving is the foundational step.[^nussbaum] On top of that, "topical therapies are currently the mainstay treatment," which is where the exfoliating keratolytics in the tier list above fit; the reviews list "preventive measures, antibiotics, corticosteroids, keratolytics, chemical depilatories and/or laser treatments" as the current options.[^dalia] The graded items are the topical keratolytics that map to ingredient pages here; they are comparably evidenced mainstays rather than ranked against each other, and concentrations and the general evidence are on each linked page.

Laser hair removal is the emerging long-term option: as Dalia and colleagues note, it "has become a potential long-term treatment option, and additional studies are warranted to understand its long-term efficacy and permanency."[^dalia] Because it reduces the hair that causes the problem, it is the closest thing to a durable fix, but the reviews are cautious about how permanent it is.

**Medical note.** Persistent, scarring, or heavily pigmenting razor bumps are worth a dermatologist's evaluation, particularly for skin of color, where PFB is both more common and more likely to leave lasting marks.[^dalia]
