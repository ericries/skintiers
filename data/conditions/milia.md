A milium (plural milia) is "a small cyst containing keratin," the tiny pearly-white bumps just under the skin surface that are usually multiple.[^dermnet] They are harmless, most common on the eyelids and cheeks, and in adults arise from a vellus (fine) hair follicle rather than any disease process.[^dermnet] The honest framing: this is a cosmetic nuisance, not a condition skincare products meaningfully treat once it has already formed.

## How to know you have this

Milia are firm, dome-shaped, pearly-white or yellowish bumps of 1 to 2mm, sitting just under the skin with no visible opening or pore, most often on the eyelids, cheeks, and forehead.[^dermnet] They are common at every age. In newborns, milia affect an estimated "40 to 50% of newborn babies" and clear on their own within weeks; in older children and adults they can persist for months or longer.[^dermnet] A less common variant, milia en plaque, presents as a cluster of milia within an inflamed patch, typically on an eyelid, behind the ear, or on the jaw, and is more often seen in middle-aged women.[^dermnet]

## What is happening in the skin

A milium is a small epidermoid cyst filled with keratin, the same structural protein that makes up the outer skin layer, and on biopsy it is seen "coming from a vellus hair follicle."[^dermnet] Because the cyst sits under intact skin with no opening to the surface, it is mechanically different from a comedone (blackhead/whitehead), which does have a follicular opening; that difference is exactly why exfoliating acids and pore-clearing routines that work on acne do not reliably clear milia.[^dermnet] Milia can also follow skin trauma such as burns, blistering rashes, or dermabrasion (as the skin heals, milia form at the injury site), and can rarely follow use of certain topical medications including hydroquinone, 5-fluorouracil cream, and topical corticosteroids.[^dermnet]

## What actually works

DermNet is direct about this: "milia do not need to be treated unless they are a cause for concern for the patient," and many "often clear up by themselves within a few months," especially the neonatal and primary childhood forms.[^dermnet] For milia that persist and bother the patient, the standard, reliable fix is physical removal by a professional, most simply de-roofing with a sterile needle or blade to express the keratin plug, or destruction by diathermy and curettage, cryotherapy, chemical peels, dermabrasion, or laser ablation for more extensive cases.[^dermnet] Topical [[retinoids]] "may be helpful" for widespread lesions by speeding cell turnover, and a gentle keratolytic like [[salicylic-acid]] can support that by encouraging surface exfoliation, but neither opens or dissolves an existing cyst the way extraction does; treat them as slow, preventive adjuncts, not a substitute for a dermatologist's needle. Milia en plaque, the inflamed clustered variant, "may improve with minocycline (a tetracycline antibiotic)," an oral-antibiotic decision for a clinician, not a topical one.[^dermnet]

## What's overhyped

There is no cream, serum, or at-home extraction tool that reliably pops a milium the way it pops a blackhead: because the cyst has no surface opening, pressing on it does nothing but risk scarring and hyperpigmentation. Skip DIY needle extraction; a dermatologist or trained aesthetician doing it under sterile conditions is safer and more effective.

## The Evidence

The evidence base here is thin by design, DermNet's own reference list points to a single review article (Berk & Bayliss, 2008, J Am Acad Dermatol) plus standard dermatology textbooks, and DermNet's treatment section is written in qualifying language ("may be helpful," "have been reported to be effective") rather than citing controlled trials.[^dermnet] That reflects reality: milia are a minor, usually self-resolving cosmetic finding, and no one has run large randomized trials on topical treatments for something dermatologists mostly just extract in-office. Treat any confident marketing claim about a milia-dissolving product with skepticism.

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Most milia are harmless and self-resolving, but a firm bump that is new, growing, changing, or that you are not certain is a milium should be looked at by a board-certified dermatologist rather than self-treated. Removal (de-roofing with a sterile needle, curettage, cryotherapy, laser) is a minor in-office procedure, not an at-home one; DIY extraction risks scarring and infection. Milia en plaque and its treatment with an oral antibiotic (minocycline) require a prescriber and can be associated with other skin diseases, so they warrant clinical evaluation. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.
