Photoaging is the skin change caused by cumulative ultraviolet exposure layered on top of ordinary chronological aging, and its hallmark tissue change is solar elastosis: skin that "appears yellow and thickened as a result of abnormal elastic tissue accumulation, due to chronic sun damage on ageing skin."[^elastosis] It shows up as fine lines, coarse wrinkles, rough leathery texture, mottled brown pigment, sallowness, visible broken vessels (telangiectasia), and loss of firmness (laxity). The honest framing: prevention works far better than any repair product, and no topical reverses the underlying dermal damage.

## How to know you have this

Photoaging concentrates on chronically sun-exposed sites: face, ears, neck, forearms, and the backs of hands.[^elastosis] DermNet describes the affected skin as appearing "dry, thick, and yellow, with bumps, wrinkles, or furrowing," sometimes resembling lemon skin, with comedones or a cobblestoned texture and uneven pigmentation.[^elastosis] It typically does not present until the fourth decade of life, but cumulative UV exposure, not age itself, is the driving risk factor, and lighter Fitzpatrick skin types show the changes more readily.[^elastosis]

Persistent rough, scaly, or crusted spots on sun-exposed skin are a different and more serious problem: actinic keratosis, "a precancerous scaly spot found on sun-damaged skin" and an early form of squamous cell carcinoma.[^ak] Anyone with a rough spot that does not resolve, or any lesion that is new, changing, bleeding, or non-healing, needs a dermatologist for evaluation rather than a skincare routine. This page covers cosmetic photoaging; it is not medical guidance for diagnosing or treating skin cancer or its precursors.

## What is happening in the skin

Ultraviolet radiation breaks down collagen and elastin fibers in the dermis; the skin's abnormal elastic-tissue buildup that follows is an overcompensation for that damage, not a repair of it.[^elastosis] Smoking compounds the effect by encouraging degradative enzymes and disorderly elastin deposition deeper in the dermis than sun damage alone reaches.[^elastosis] Because chronic UV exposure also damages DNA, people with visible photoaging are more likely to also have UV-related skin cancers and actinic keratoses, which is why the same sun exposure that ages skin cosmetically also carries the medical risk.[^elastosis]

## What actually works

Prevention is the highest-value intervention: daily broad-spectrum sunscreen ([[sunscreen-uv-filters]]) measurably slows the appearance of new photoaging, and DermNet's own treatment discussion for solar elastosis leads with sun protection and smoking cessation before any cosmetic option.[^elastosis] For repairing texture and fine lines on skin that is already photoaged, [[retinoids]] (tretinoin) has the deepest trial base. [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]] and [[glycolic-acid]] have real but more modest evidence for surface texture and tone. The mottled brown pigment of photoaging (solar lentigines) is its own evidence question, covered on [[hyperpigmentation]]. DermNet also lists topical retinoids, imiquimod, tacrolimus, and estrogen among treatments explored for solar elastosis specifically, "although these have variable efficacy reported."[^elastosis] This page and [[anti-aging]] cover related ground; [[anti-aging]] is the broader goal hub for aging skin generally, while this page is specifically about UV-driven damage.

Procedures that address deeper elastosis and wrinkling, such as laser resurfacing, chemical peels, dermal fillers, and botulinum toxin, are listed by DermNet as cosmetic options for solar elastosis but sit outside topical skincare and outside this site's current scope.[^elastosis]

## What's overhyped

No serum, cream, or "anti-aging" product reverses solar elastosis or rebuilds lost dermal collagen and elastin back to baseline. Topical retinoids and antioxidants improve surface appearance, fine lines, and tone; they do not undo decades of cumulative UV damage. The realistic goal for already-photoaged skin is measurable improvement in texture and pigment, not restoration to unexposed skin.

## The Evidence

The clearest prevention evidence is a randomized, controlled community trial in Nambour, Australia (903 adults under 55, 4.5 years of follow-up), which found that "skin aging from baseline to the end of the trial was 24% less in the daily sunscreen group than in the discretionary sunscreen group (relative odds, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.59 to 0.98])," concluding that "regular sunscreen use retards skin aging in healthy, middle-aged men and women."[^hughes] That trial also tested oral beta-carotene supplementation and found no overall effect on skin aging.[^hughes]

For repair, tretinoin's evidence base is the deepest among topicals, with replicated vehicle-controlled trials and a 2025 meta-analysis of 8 trials (1,361 patients); the fuller evidence detail for retinoid forms lives on [[retinoids]] rather than being repeated here.
