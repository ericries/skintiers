The brown mark left behind after something inflamed the skin. **Bottom line:** the best systematic review of its treatment, 41 studies and 877 patients, found that almost nothing clears it completely, and that the most-used category clears it in about one case in twenty. Most people get partial fading and should plan for that. The general pigment evidence is at [[hyperpigmentation]]; the red mark people confuse this with is at [[post-inflammatory-erythema]].

> **Brown is not red, and they do not respond to the same things.** PIH is pigmentary. [[post-inflammatory-erythema]] is vascular, a mark from dilated or damaged capillaries, and that page owns the distinction and its source. Getting this wrong wastes months, because a pigment-targeting routine does little for a vascular mark and the reverse is also true.

## How to know you have this

Flat brown or grey-brown marks where an inflamed spot used to be, in the same places the inflammation was. They do not blanch under pressure, which is the practical difference from the red marks at [[post-inflammatory-erythema]]; that page owns the distinction and its sources. A 2026 clinical review puts the mechanism plainly: acne-induced hyperpigmentation "results from increased melanin production and deposition triggered by inflammatory mediators, while acne-induced macular erythema reflects vascular alterations".[^lopez]

**Who gets it.** "Up to 85% of patients with Fitzpatrick phototypes IV-VI develop post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation following inflammatory acne lesions."[^lopez] The same review names the risk factors: "Female individuals, darker skin color, severe acne, facial sites, excessive sunlight exposure, and traumatized lesions".[^lopez] Two of those are things you control, which is why not picking a spot and wearing [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] do more than any corrective product.

**How long.** Both marks "can persist for months or even years", and the review notes they contribute to the psychosocial burden of acne "sometimes to a greater extent than active acne itself".[^lopez] Diagnosis, it says, "relies on visual inspection, acne history, and skin phototype assessment", so there is no test to ask for.[^lopez]

## The Evidence

**One systematic review carries most of what is known.** Kashetsky and colleagues searched Embase, MEDLINE, PubMed and clinicaltrials.gov under PRISMA and included 41 studies representing 877 patients.[^review] Their framing of the condition is that it "is a common consequence of inflammatory dermatoses", that "It is more common in patients with darker skin and has significant morbidity."[^review]

**Complete clearance is rare, whatever you use.** Reported complete response was 18.1% (56 of 309) for laser and energy-based devices, 5.4% (20 of 369) for topicals and 2.4% (4 of 166) for combination therapies.[^review] The authors' own conclusion is that "the current treatment approaches yielded unsatisfactory rates of complete response."[^review]

**Partial fading is the realistic goal, and it reorders the table.** Partial response was 84.9% (141 of 166) for combination modalities, 72.4% (267 of 369) for topicals, 61.2% (189 of 309) for laser and energy-based devices and 33.3% (5 of 15) for peels.[^review] So the category that clears PIH outright least often is the one that improves it most often. Any single ranking of these treatments has to pick one of those two orderings and hide the other, which is why this page does not offer one.

**Treatment can make it worse, and the review quantifies that.** "in 2.6% (n = 8/309) of patients treated with laser and energy-based devices, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation worsened."[^review] Poor to no response was 66.7% (10 of 15) for peels, 22.2% (82 of 369) for topicals, 18.1% (56 of 309) for laser and energy-based devices and 12.7% (21 of 166) for combination modalities.[^review] Adverse events were reported in ten patients, "all while using topical treatments."[^review] That risk is the reason the aftercare for an ablative procedure matters, which is covered at [[post-ablative-laser-aftercare]].

**The limit on all of it, stated by the authors.** "the majority of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation cases included were associated with acne, and therefore, the findings and conclusions drawn may have limited applicability to other types of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation."[^review] So these figures describe post-acne marks better than they describe PIH from eczema, a burn, or a procedure. PIH at other body sites has its own thin literature; the axillary case is at [[vahabi-2025-axillary-hyperpigmentation-systematic-review]].

> **Bottom line.** Expect fading rather than clearance, expect it to be slow, and treat the underlying inflammation first, because a mark that keeps being re-made cannot be out-treated. [[acne]] is the commonest source.
