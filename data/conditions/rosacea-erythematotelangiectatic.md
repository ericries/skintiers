Erythematotelangiectatic rosacea (ETR), often called "type 1" rosacea, is the redness-and-vessels form of the disease: persistent flushing and central-facial redness, visible small blood vessels (telangiectasia), and a face that stings, burns, and reacts easily, but without the acne-like papules and pustules of the inflammatory type. For the redness and the vessels, the realistic non-prescription levers are topical skincare and in-office light devices, and the evidence for them is limited. The two prescription drugs approved specifically for the persistent redness, brimonidine and oxymetazoline, reduce it only temporarily and, in the registration trials, only a minority of patients reached the strict success threshold; the visible vessels themselves respond to lasers rather than to any cream, on weaker evidence than the drugs; and the anti-inflammatory topicals that carry Cochrane certainty (azelaic acid, ivermectin, metronidazole) were graded for the papules and pustules of the other subtype, not for type 1's fixed erythema or telangiectasia.

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians look for, described so a reader can recognize them, not a way to self-diagnose. Persistent facial redness has several causes, and rosacea is diagnosed clinically, from the pattern of features on the central face, which only a doctor can assess.[^oge][^wilkin][^rosco] In everyday terms, type 1 rosacea is redness and visible vessels without the acne-like bumps of the other form, clustered on the central face (the cheeks, nose, chin, and central forehead):

- **Redness that does not fully fade.** A background flush over the central face that stays reddened between flares rather than settling completely. The 2002 National Rosacea Society classification named this the erythematotelangiectatic subtype, "flushing and persistent central facial erythema with or without telangiectasia," and the global ROSacea COnsensus (ROSCO) panel treats persistent centrofacial redness "associated with periodic intensification" as diagnostic of rosacea on its own.[^wilkin][^rosco]
- **Easy flushing, and visible vessels.** Flushing is the transient wave of redness and warmth that comes and goes with triggers; telangiectasia are the fine, permanently dilated surface vessels that stay visible between flares. The ROSCO panel judged these two, taken individually, "not considered to be individually diagnostic," so they sit alongside the persistent redness rather than replace it.[^rosco]
- **Skin that stings, burns, and reacts.** The affected skin is often described as burning or stinging, dry-feeling, and easily irritated by products that other people tolerate; the National Rosacea Society classification and the ROSCO recommendations both list these sensory and skin-sensitivity features as part of the condition.[^wilkin][^rosco]

**How it differs from acne.** The clearest separator is what is missing: rosacea produces no blackheads or whiteheads (comedones), so redness and visible vessels without comedones point away from acne.[^oge] The general acne picture is covered on [[acne]].

**How it differs from the other look-alikes.** The inflammatory papules and pustules of type 2 rosacea ([[rosacea-papulopustular]]) are absent in type 1, which is essentially the redness and vessels on their own.[^wilkin] Other causes of a red central face that a dermatologist distinguishes include seborrhoeic dermatitis, which spreads differently across the scalp, eyebrows, and the creases beside the nose, and lupus, which rarely produces pustules.[^oge] The whole hub condition, including the phymatous (type 3) and ocular (type 4) presentations, is mapped on [[rosacea]].

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Rosacea is a chronic medical condition, and diagnosis matters: persistent facial redness has other causes (including lupus, seborrhoeic dermatitis, and photodamage) that a board-certified dermatologist distinguishes. Several rosacea treatments are prescription medicines that fall outside this page's topical-and-device scope, including oral doxycycline and oral isotretinoin; they are named on the sibling page for the inflammatory subtype and require a prescriber. Eye involvement (ocular rosacea) is common and can threaten sight, and is a reason to seek medical care. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate evaluation.

> The honest picture for type 1: the redness can be calmed but not cured. Vasoconstrictor gels blanch it for a few hours a day (with a real risk that it rebounds worse in some people), lasers and light can fade the visible vessels over several sessions on lower-quality evidence, and daily sun protection addresses the most commonly reported trigger. The anti-inflammatory topicals that carry Cochrane certainty (azelaic acid, ivermectin, metronidazole) were graded for papules and pustules, which type 1 does not have.

## What Is Going On Underneath

Rosacea's cause is still officially open. Steinhoff and colleagues describe it as "a chronic inflammatory skin disease of unknown etiology," while identifying two systems that are consistently implicated: "Innate immune mechanisms and dysregulation of the neurovascular system are involved in rosacea initiation and perpetuation."[^steinhoff] For the type 1 redness specifically, four strands of mechanism are worth separating by how well established each is.

**Neurovascular dysregulation and neurogenic inflammation (well supported).** The flushing-and-redness phenotype behaves like a nerve-driven vascular reaction. Schwab and colleagues found that "rosacea has all the characteristics of neurogenic inflammation, a condition induced by sensory nerves via antidromically released neuromediators," and that gene-expression studies "confirmed upregulation of genes involved in vasoregulation and neurogenic inflammation."[^schwab] In plain terms: sensory nerves in the skin release signalling molecules that dilate vessels and recruit inflammation, which fits the burning, stinging, and flushing patients report.

**TRP ion channels (well supported as an association).** Transient receptor potential (TRP) channels are the sensors on nerves and skin cells that respond to heat, cold, and irritants such as capsaicin, and several of the classic rosacea triggers (heat, spice, temperature swings) act through them. Sulk and colleagues measured these channels in rosacea skin and reported that in erythematotelangiectatic rosacea "dermal immunolabeling of TRPV2 and TRPV3 and gene expression of TRPV1 is significantly increased," and concluded that "TRP ion channels may be targets for the treatment of rosacea."[^sulk] That the trigger-sensing channels are over-expressed in exactly the redness subtype is a strong mechanistic clue, though it is an association in patient tissue, not proof of cause.

**The cathelicidin / serine-protease (innate immune) axis (well supported in the papulopustular direction).** Yamasaki and colleagues showed that people with rosacea "express abnormally high levels of cathelicidin in their facial skin" and that "the proteolytically processed forms of cathelicidin peptides found in rosacea are different from those present in normal individuals," driven by increased activity of a stratum corneum tryptic enzyme (the serine protease also known as kallikrein-5, which cleaves the cathelicidin precursor into the peptide LL-37).[^yamasaki] Injecting those rosacea-type peptides, or adding the enzyme, "increases inflammation in mouse skin."[^yamasaki] This axis is the strongest molecular story in rosacea, but it is most directly tied to inflammation (the papules and pustules of type 2); its weight in the fixed erythema of type 1, versus the neurovascular mechanism, is less settled.

**Ultraviolet damage to the dermal vessels and matrix (established as a driver, mechanism partly inferred).** Chronic sun exposure is repeatedly implicated in the vascular and connective-tissue changes of rosacea, and sun exposure is the single most commonly reported trigger (see below). UV degrades dermal collagen and elastin and is thought to weaken the perivascular support that normally keeps small vessels constricted, which fits both the telangiectasia and the photodistribution of the redness; the general UV-and-matrix mechanism is covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], which is the site's best-evidenced anti-photoaging intervention.

The honest summary: the neurovascular and TRP-channel findings map cleanly onto the type 1 redness and flushing, the cathelicidin axis maps more onto inflammation, and no single one has been shown to be the first cause. They are documented associations in patient skin, not a proven causal chain.

## Triggers

The best-known trigger data come from a National Rosacea Society survey of 1,066 rosacea patients, and they are patient-reported (what people noticed provoked their own flares), not controlled provocation experiments. In that survey the most commonly reported triggers were sun exposure (81%), emotional stress (79%), hot weather (75%), wind (57%), heavy exercise (56%), alcohol (52%), hot baths (51%), cold weather (46%), spicy foods (45%), humidity (44%), indoor heat (41%), skin-care products (41%), and heated beverages (36%).[^nrs] These are self-reported associations and vary from person to person; the NRS notes that "what may cause a reaction in one patient may not in another."[^nrs] The heat- and temperature-related items on that list are consistent with the over-expressed thermosensory TRP channels described above, which gives the patient reports a plausible mechanism rather than proof of causation.[^sulk]

## What Actually Works

Graded against controlled evidence, and separated by which problem it addresses: the temporary redness, the fixed vessels, or the triggers. Both approved drugs cleared their trials, but the absolute success rates are modest.

**1. Brimonidine 0.33% topical gel (for the persistent redness, temporarily). Effect: modest and temporary. Evidence: solid.** Brimonidine is an alpha-adrenergic agonist that constricts small facial vessels; it is FDA-approved (as Mirvaso) "for the topical treatment of persistent (nontransient) erythema of rosacea in adults."[^mirvaso] In the two phase 3 vehicle-controlled trials, the primary endpoint was a strict composite: at least a 2-grade improvement on both the Clinician's Erythema Assessment and the Patient's Self-Assessment. On day 29 at 3 hours after application, composite success was 31% vs 11% for vehicle (n=129 vs 131) in study 1, and 25% vs 9% (n=148 vs 145) in study 2, with the drug significantly ahead of vehicle across the 12-hour day.[^mirvaso][^fowler2013] The 2019 Cochrane review graded this "high-certainty evidence for topical brimonidine" for reducing what it called temporarily persistent erythema.[^cochrane2019] Two honest limits define this drug. First, the effect is temporary vasoconstriction lasting hours, and it does nothing for telangiectasia. Second, the label carries a rebound warning: some trial subjects reported "a rebound phenomenon, where erythema was reported to return worse compared to the severity at baseline," and postmarketing reports describe "increased frequency of flushing and/or increased depth of erythema."[^mirvaso] Rebound redness and burning after brimonidine has been documented as a distinct clinical problem.[^routt]

**2. Oxymetazoline 1% cream (for the persistent redness, temporarily). Effect: modest and temporary. Evidence: solid.** Oxymetazoline is a related alpha-adrenergic vasoconstrictor, FDA-approved (as Rhofade) for "the topical treatment of persistent facial erythema associated with rosacea in adults."[^rhofade] Its two phase 3 trials used the same strict composite endpoint (at least a 2-grade improvement on both clinician and patient scales). On day 29 the composite success rates were lower than brimonidine's: in trial 1, 12% to 18% for oxymetazoline versus 6% to 8% for vehicle across hours 3 to 12 (n=222 vs 218); in trial 2, 12% to 16% versus 5% to 9% (n=224 vs 221), significantly ahead of vehicle.[^rhofade][^baumann2018] Cochrane graded this "moderate certainty for topical oxymetazoline."[^cochrane2019] As with brimonidine, the effect is a temporary daily blanching of the background redness, not a change to the vessels, though rebound has been reported less prominently for oxymetazoline.

**3. Lasers and light devices (for the telangiectasia and background redness). Effect: notable for visible vessels. Evidence: mixed (lower quality).** The fixed vessels do not respond to creams; they are targeted by devices that heat and collapse them, chiefly pulsed-dye laser (PDL), the potassium-titanyl-phosphate (KTP) laser, and intense pulsed light (IPL). Cochrane's assessment is honest about the evidence tier: the 2019 update found, for erythema and mainly telangiectasia, "low-to-moderate-certainty evidence for laser and intense pulsed light therapy,"[^cochrane2019] and the 2015 review reported that pulsed-dye laser "appeared to be as effective as intense pulsed light therapy (both low quality evidence)."[^cochrane2015] So the mechanism is direct and the clinical experience is favourable, but the rigorous randomized evidence is thinner than for the vasoconstrictor drugs. These are in-office procedures performed by a clinician, not skincare, and are included here as context.

**4. Photoprotection and barrier-supportive skincare (adjuncts).** Sun exposure is the most commonly reported trigger in the NRS survey (81% of 1,066 patients),[^nrs] and daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is the site's best-evidenced anti-photoaging intervention, with randomized-trial support against clinical endpoints; how to read a label for real UVA coverage is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]. Beyond photoprotection, barrier-supportive ingredients are commonly included in rosacea skincare as adjuncts to reduce the skin sensitivity and dryness of the condition rather than to treat the redness itself: niacinamide, a well-studied barrier-supporting form of vitamin B3, is covered on [[niacinamide]], and centella asiatica, a botanical studied for soothing and barrier support, on [[centella-asiatica]]. These support comfort; they are not established treatments for the fixed erythema or the vessels, which is the job of the graded interventions above.

## What Does Not Work Well for This Subtype, and What Is Overhyped

The topicals with the strongest rosacea evidence, azelaic acid, ivermectin, and metronidazole, carry their Cochrane certainty for the papules and pustules of the inflammatory subtype, not for the fixed redness and vessels of type 1.

- **Azelaic acid, ivermectin, and metronidazole target the papulopustular subtype, not type 1's fixed erythema or telangiectasia.** The 2019 Cochrane review placed these under papules and pustules, finding "high-certainty evidence for topical azelaic acid and topical ivermectin" and moderate-certainty evidence for topical metronidazole, all for reducing inflammatory lesions.[^cochrane2019] [[azelaic-acid]] is a genuinely well-evidenced topical, but its rosacea grade is for papulopustular disease, so it is the sibling page's tool ([[rosacea-papulopustular]]), not an eraser of type 1 redness or visible vessels. Where inflammation coexists it can help; against pure fixed erythema and telangiectasia it is off-label extrapolation, not graded evidence.
- **Oral doxycycline and isotretinoin** likewise carry their Cochrane certainty for papules and pustules, not for the type 1 redness.[^cochrane2019] They belong to the inflammatory-subtype discussion.
- **Over-the-counter "redness relief" creams and "rosacea cures."** No over-the-counter cream is FDA-approved for the persistent erythema of rosacea; the two approved for it are the prescription vasoconstrictors above. Products advertising a rosacea "cure" run against the chronic, relapsing nature of the condition as described by the classifications.[^wilkin][^rosco]

## The Evidence

**The evidence framework: Cochrane 2019 (phenotype-based).** The updated Cochrane systematic review pooled 152 randomized trials (20,944 participants) and graded interventions by what they treat.[^cochrane2019] Its erythema conclusion is the backbone of this page: "For reducing temporarily persistent erythema there was high-certainty evidence for topical brimonidine and moderate certainty for topical oxymetazoline; for erythema and mainly telangiectasia there was low-to-moderate-certainty evidence for laser and intense pulsed light therapy."[^cochrane2019] The word "temporarily" is doing real work: even the high-certainty finding is for a temporary reduction.

> Bottom line: the redness can be reduced temporarily with good certainty (brimonidine) or moderate certainty (oxymetazoline); the vessels respond to lasers and light on weaker evidence; and the anti-inflammatory topicals sit under a different phenotype entirely.

**Brimonidine registration trials (Fowler 2012, Fowler 2013, FDA label).** The two phase 3 double-blind vehicle-controlled trials found once-daily brimonidine "provides significantly greater efficacy than vehicle gel for the treatment of moderate to severe erythema of rosacea," with separation from vehicle "as early as 30 minutes after the first application on day 1 (all P<.001)."[^fowler2012][^fowler2013] The FDA label quantifies it: day-29 composite success (a 2-grade improvement on both clinician and patient scales) of 31% vs 11% and 25% vs 9% at 3 hours, drug versus vehicle.[^mirvaso]

> Bottom line: brimonidine reliably beats a placebo gel at blanching the background redness for several hours, but only a minority (roughly a quarter to a third) hit the strict two-scale success mark, the effect is temporary, and some users get rebound redness worse than baseline.

**Oxymetazoline registration trials (Baumann 2018, FDA label).** In the phase 3 trials, "the proportion of patients achieving the primary efficacy outcome was significantly greater with oxymetazoline versus vehicle (P=0.001)," and the authors concluded it "was effective, safe, and well tolerated in the treatment of moderate to severe persistent facial erythema of rosacea."[^baumann2018] The label's day-29 composite success rates were 12% to 18% (drug) versus 5% to 9% (vehicle) across hours 3 to 12.[^rhofade]

> Bottom line: oxymetazoline works on the same temporary-blanching principle as brimonidine and clears the same strict endpoint, but at lower absolute success rates in its trials.

**Laser and light for the vessels (Cochrane 2015 and 2019).** The device evidence is real but graded lower. Cochrane found pulsed-dye laser "appeared to be as effective as intense pulsed light therapy (both low quality evidence),"[^cochrane2015] and the 2019 update kept laser and IPL for erythema and telangiectasia at "low-to-moderate-certainty evidence."[^cochrane2019]

> Bottom line: for the visible vessels specifically, lasers and IPL are the targeted option and clinicians use them widely, but the randomized evidence behind them is weaker than for the vasoconstrictor drugs, and they are procedures, not skincare.

Across these, the syntheses converge: the redness is treatable but only temporarily and incompletely by topicals, the vessels need devices, and the strongest anti-inflammatory topicals in rosacea address a different phenotype.[^cochrane2019][^mirvaso][^rhofade]
