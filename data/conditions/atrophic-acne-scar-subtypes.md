Dermatologists sort true (atrophic) acne scars into three types: icepick, rolling and boxcar.[^boen] Every treatment ranking on this site is built on trials that mostly ignore which of the three they were treating. **Bottom line:** your subtype decides what is even mechanically sensible, the reviews all say selection should start there, and the comparative evidence cannot tell you which treatment wins for your type. The hub for all post-acne marks is [[acne-scars]].

## How to know you have this

**This page will not let you self-classify, deliberately.** The three subtypes are distinguished by clinical examination, and no source consulted here publishes criteria a reader could apply to their own face. Rather than invent descriptions, this page explains why the distinction matters and what to ask about.

- **If you have true atrophic scarring at all**, it is permanent depression from lost collagen, it does not fade, and topicals do little for it. That sorting (scar versus flat mark) is on [[acne-scars]], and it is the question to settle first.
- **The one subtype with a mechanically specific treatment is rolling.** Rolling scars are tethered to deeper tissue, which is what subcision exists to release; the procedure and its evidence are on [[subcision]]. If a clinician proposes subcision, tethering is the thing they are treating.
- **What to ask a clinician:** which of the three types you have, in what proportion, because mixed patterns are usual and the answer determines whether a needling, resurfacing, releasing or filling approach is on the table at all.
- **See a clinician rather than self-treating.** These are procedural decisions, and scar type is only one input: a 2026 network meta-analysis of 56 randomized trials concludes that "Treatment selection should consider scar type, Fitzpatrick skin phototype, downtime, pain, adverse-effect risk, and patient preference."[^ou] Phototype is on that list because pigmentary risk differs by skin tone.

## Why the evidence cannot answer per subtype

**The classification exists and the trials ignore it.** The classification system this page describes comes from a review built explicitly around it, which searched for studies using the icepick, rolling and boxcar scheme. Its own conclusion is the problem: "There were few high-quality evidence-based studies evaluating the management of acne scarring. Many disparate acne severity scores were used in these studies, and the acne scar type was frequently undefined, making comparison between them difficult."[^boen] That was 2019.

**It is still true in 2026.** A review restricted to trials with verifiable extractable data found that "No included trial reported treatment effects by scar morphology, so phenotype-based comparative efficacy could not be tested."[^liu] So the gap is not that nobody has looked; it is that the primary literature does not record the variable the reviews tell clinicians to use.

**What this means for the rankings on this site.** [[acne-scar-procedures-by-evidence]] and [[acne-scar-treatments-by-downtime-and-pain]] rank procedures across mixed atrophic scarring. They are the best available answer, and they are not subtype-specific. If you have predominantly icepick scars, no published ranking on this site or anywhere else consulted here is telling you what works best for icepick scars specifically. That is worth knowing before paying for a course of treatment.

**One substantive corroboration.** The 2026 network meta-analysis of 56 randomized trials and 1,488 patients found that "Combination therapies generally outperformed monotherapies for moderate-to-severe atrophic acne scars", with fractional CO2 laser plus subcision or plus platelet-rich plasma showing "the most consistent benefits".[^ou] That agrees with the combination finding already on [[acne-scar-procedures-by-evidence]], and it is a third analysis naming a different top combination from the other two, which is itself a reason for caution about any single ranking.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Treating active [[acne]] early remains the only thing that prevents new atrophic scars from forming.
