Folliculitis is "an inflamed hair follicle due to any cause," producing a tender red spot that often has a surface pustule; it can be superficial or deep and can affect anywhere there are hairs, including the chest, back, buttocks, arms, and legs.[^dermnet] It is not one disease. It is a shared pattern of follicle inflammation that different causes, most often bacterial or fungal infection, produce, and the right treatment depends entirely on which one is present.[^dermnet][^statpearls]

## How to know you have this

Folliculitis shows up as small pustules with peri-follicular inflammation in hair-bearing areas: the chest, back, face, scalp, and limbs.[^statpearls] The two common infectious causes look and behave differently. Bacterial folliculitis, usually from Staphylococcus aureus, tends to appear as scattered tender pustules and, if it reaches the deep part of the follicle, can form a painful boil.[^dermnet] Malassezia (Pityrosporum) folliculitis is "an itchy acne-like condition usually affecting the upper trunk of a young adult," with small, uniform (monomorphic) itchy papules and pustules rather than the mixed blackheads, whiteheads, and varied-size lesions typical of acne.[^dermnet] It is the same yeast that causes [[fungal-acne]] and is often confused with acne because it looks similar, which matters because acne treatments do not touch it. History clues that point toward the fungal type include recent oral antibiotic use, increased sweating, occlusive moisturizers, and warm humid conditions, all of which favor Malassezia overgrowth; hot tub or pool exposure instead points toward Pseudomonas ("spa pool") folliculitis.[^statpearls][^dermnet]

## What is happening in the skin

Most folliculitis is infection of the hair follicle itself, most often bacterial, though fungal and viral infections also cause it, alongside noninfectious triggers like ingrown hairs, occlusion, and certain drugs.[^statpearls] In Malassezia folliculitis specifically, the yeast that normally lives on skin proliferates inside the follicle, and its presence there (rather than on the surface) is why oral antifungals are often favored over topical ones: topical agents may not reach deep enough into the follicle to clear it.[^statpearls] A KOH skin scraping can confirm Malassezia under a microscope if the diagnosis is unclear, and swabs can identify the responsible bacteria; most cases, however, are diagnosed clinically without testing.[^statpearls][^dermnet]

## What actually works

For bacterial folliculitis, mild cases often clear on their own within days with good hygiene; more extensive disease responds to antiseptic washes, [[benzoyl-peroxide]], or a topical/oral antibiotic prescribed by a clinician.[^dermnet][^statpearls] Recurrent bacterial folliculitis is often managed with dilute bleach ([[sodium-hypochlorite]]) washes to reduce Staph colonization on the skin. For Malassezia (fungal) folliculitis, the direction reverses: topical antifungals such as [[ketoconazole]], [[ciclopirox]], or [[selenium-sulfide]] are first-line, oral antifungals (itraconazole or fluconazole) are used when topical treatment fails to clear it, and the routine should also stop occlusive moisturizers and any antibiotics that may be feeding the yeast overgrowth.[^dermnet][^statpearls] [[salicylic-acid]] can help as an adjunct exfoliant but does not treat either underlying cause.

## What's overhyped

Antibiotics are the wrong move for Malassezia folliculitis. They do not touch a yeast, and prolonged antibiotic use is itself a listed trigger that can provoke or worsen it, along with occlusive moisturizers and heavy sweating.[^dermnet][^statpearls] Because the fungal form mimics acne so closely, treating it with standard acne antibiotics or benzoyl peroxide-only routines without confirming the cause can leave it unresolved or make it worse. Most simple bacterial folliculitis is also self-limiting: "the only therapy needed is time" in the majority of cases, so escalating straight to oral antibiotics is often unnecessary.[^statpearls]

## The Evidence

DermNet's clinical reference confirms the split etiology: Staphylococcus aureus for common bacterial folliculitis, versus Pityrosporum ovale (Malassezia) for the itchy, acne-like fungal form, with the fungal type's recommended management explicitly including "stopping any antibiotics."[^dermnet] StatPearls corroborates the differential treatment pathways: topical mupirocin or clindamycin (escalating to oral cephalexin or dicloxacillin) for staphylococcal folliculitis, versus oral itraconazole or fluconazole (with topical antifungals as an alternative) for Pityrosporum folliculitis, plus the KOH-preparation diagnostic method for confirming Malassezia.[^statpearls] Both sources agree the underlying diagnosis, not a single universal product, determines what will actually work.

This page is educational and not a substitute for medical diagnosis. Persistent, spreading, painful, or recurrent folliculitis, or folliculitis with fever, should be evaluated by a clinician, since deep or extensive infection (boils, cellulitis) and non-infectious causes (eosinophilic folliculitis, drug-induced folliculitis) need diagnosis-specific treatment that a skincare routine cannot provide.
