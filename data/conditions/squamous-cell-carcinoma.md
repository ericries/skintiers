Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a cancer that starts in the squamous cells, the thin, flat cells that form the top layer of the epidermis, and with [[basal-cell-carcinoma]] it is one of the two most common types of skin cancer, together called nonmelanoma skin cancer.[^nci] The American Academy of Dermatology describes it as a cancer that "often looks like a scaly patch, dome-shaped growth, or sore" that "tends to grow slowly," and notes that "when found early, squamous cell carcinoma is highly treatable."[^aad] This is a medical disease diagnosed and treated by physicians, typically with surgery such as simple excision or Mohs micrographic surgery and other physician-directed options, and it is entirely outside the scope of skincare products.[^nci]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Only a dermatologist can diagnose squamous cell carcinoma, including with a biopsy where needed. Nothing here should be used to delay evaluation of a new or changing spot.

## How to know you have this

The AAD says squamous cell carcinoma usually shows up as a growth on or change to the skin, which may look like a "rough, scaly patch," a "firm, dome-shaped growth," a "spot that looks like a sore surrounded by raised skin," a "sore that doesn't heal and may bleed or heals and comes back," a "wart-like growth," a "horn-like growth," or a "change to a scar."[^aad] It can also be tender, painful, or itchy, and may scab and bleed.[^aad] Color varies across skin tones: the AAD says the growth or spot may be "red or pink," "brown or black," the "same color as your skin," or "one color with flecks of other colors."[^aad] It develops most often on sun-exposed skin such as the face, scalp, neck, arm, hand, and lower leg, though it can appear anywhere.[^aad] The AAD advises anyone concerned about a spot or growth to see a board-certified dermatologist.[^aad]

## Actinic keratosis and spread

Actinic keratosis, a rough, scaly precancerous growth from sun damage, "is a skin condition that sometimes becomes squamous cell carcinoma," which is why these spots are watched.[^nci] Squamous cell carcinoma itself can be serious: the AAD states that left untreated "it may grow deep and spread," and that in people with a weakened immune system it can be aggressive, meaning more likely to return after treatment or to spread.[^aad] It cannot, however, turn into [[melanoma]], because the two cancers arise from different skin cells.[^aad]

## Risk factors

The National Cancer Institute lists the risk factors for basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma as being exposed to natural or artificial sunlight over long periods, a fair complexion (skin that freckles or burns easily, light-colored eyes, red or blond hair), a history of sunburns, a personal or family history of skin cancer or actinic keratosis, certain inherited gene changes or syndromes, skin inflammation that has lasted for long periods, a weakened immune system, being exposed to arsenic, and past treatment with radiation.[^nci] Although a fair complexion is a risk factor, people of all skin colors can get skin cancer.[^nci]

## Prevention and this site's role

Because squamous cell carcinoma is a physician-managed disease, this site's contribution is prevention and prompting early evaluation, not treatment. The key action for any new, changing, or non-healing spot, including a scar or wound that will not heal, is seeing a board-certified dermatologist rather than watching and waiting.[^aad] Because cumulative UV exposure is the main modifiable risk factor the NCI lists,[^nci] sun protection is the lever most within a person's control; sunscreen reduces UV exposure, and its randomized evidence, which is clearest for squamous cell carcinoma, is set out on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].
