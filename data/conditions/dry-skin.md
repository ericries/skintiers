Dry skin, medically xerosis (also xeroderma or asteatosis), "refers to skin that feels dry to touch," occurring "when the skin is lacking moisture in the outer horny cell layer (stratum corneum)," which results in visible cracking of the surface.[^dermnet] It is one of the most treatable complaints in skincare: the fix is not exotic, it is applying enough of the right kind of moisturizer, consistently, while removing whatever is stripping the barrier in the first place.

## How to know you have this

Dry skin looks dull with a rough, scaly quality, and the skin becomes less pliable and can crack; when severe, it may become inflamed and fissured.[^dermnet] Any site can be affected, but the shins are hit more than anywhere else.[^dermnet] "Nearly everyone" over 60 has dry skin, and it is commonly seen alongside atopic dermatitis and in the roughly 20 inherited ichthyosis conditions that begin in early childhood, so a family history or an onset in childhood points toward those rather than ordinary environmental dryness.[^dermnet] If a dry patch becomes persistently itchy, that can mean a form of eczema has developed on top of it, such as asteatotic eczema ("eczema craquelé") in older people or a dry, discoid pattern from over-washing; that itch-plus-dryness picture, especially with no visible rash in an older adult, is sometimes called "winter itch" or chronic pruritus of the elderly.[^dermnet]

## What is happening in the skin

Dry skin comes from "abnormalities in the integrity of the barrier function of the stratum corneum," with an overall reduction in the lipids that normally hold the barrier together (ceramides, cholesterol, free fatty acids) and a reduced water-holding capacity in the outer cell layer.[^dermnet] Onset in adulthood tracks with recognizable triggers: postmenopausal status, hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, malnutrition, some medications (oral retinoids, diuretics, EGFR inhibitors), and environmental exposure such as low humidity, excessive air conditioning, direct heat, excessive bathing, and contact with soap, detergents, and solvents.[^dermnet] That last group is the part most people can actually change.

## What actually works

Moisturizers and emollients are, per DermNet, "the mainstay of treatment of dry skin," used liberally and often enough to reduce itch, improve barrier function, block irritant entry, and reduce water loss.[^dermnet] The full ranked ingredient list is at [[moisturizing]] and the barrier mechanism at [[skin-barrier-repair]]. Within that toolbox, two ingredient classes do the demonstrable work: occlusives that physically slow water loss, led by [[petrolatum]] and also [[mineral-oil]], and humectants that bind water in the skin, including [[glycerin]], [[hyaluronic-acid]], and [[urea]].[^dermnet] DermNet specifically flags formulations containing humectants such as urea as preferable for dry skin, and notes emollients "generally work best if applied to damp skin," ideally with a pH below 7.[^dermnet] For rough, flaky, or scaly dry skin, an alpha hydroxy acid such as [[lactic-acid]] (or its salt, [[ammonium-lactate]], used in prescription-strength lotions) both humectant-binds water and lifts the built-up scale. When dry skin has flared into itchy dermatitis, DermNet notes a topical steroid in an emollient base, or a topical calcineurin inhibitor if steroids are unsuitable, is used short-term on top of moisturizing rather than instead of it.[^dermnet]

## What's overhyped

There is no product that fixes dry skin as a one-time treatment; the DermNet outlook is candid that "a tendency to dry skin may persist life-long, or it may improve once contributing factors are controlled."[^dermnet] Removing the aggravator matters as much as the product: shorter, lukewarm (not hot) showers instead of hot soaks, less frequent bathing, a synthetic detergent cleanser instead of true soap, and a humidifier in winter are all listed by DermNet as prevention alongside moisturizing, and skipping them while relying on a fancier moisturizer is a common way people stay stuck.[^dermnet] Expensive humectant serums are not inherently better than a plain, thick emollient applied liberally and reapplied after every wash; DermNet's own guidance is that the thicker product is often needed, especially on the hands, and that moisturizer choice should weigh tolerance, personal preference, cost, and availability over marketing claims.[^dermnet]

## The Evidence

DermNet's clinical reference summary, the anchor source for this page, sets out the mechanism, the causes, and the treatment approach described above.[^dermnet] For the moisturizing intervention itself, a Cochrane systematic review of emollients and moisturizers for eczema (an abridged version with GRADE assessments) found that using them reduced flares: "fewer flares were seen (risk ratio 0.40, 95% CI 0.23-0.70)," and patients on moisturizer "used less topical corticosteroids over 6-8 weeks (mean difference -9.30 g, 95% CI -15.3 to -3.27)."[^cochrane] StatPearls characterizes petrolatum, the strongest occlusive, as reducing water loss through the epidermis "by nearly 99%," describing it as "a classic example of an occlusive moisturizer and the most effective."[^statpearls] Per-ingredient detail lives on the individual ingredient pages, starting with [[petrolatum]] and [[urea]].
