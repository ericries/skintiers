Melasma is an acquired, symmetric darkening of the skin, described in a clinical and epidemiological review as "irregular brown macules symmetrically distributed on sun-exposed areas," most often across the central face.[^handel] It is strongly hormone-influenced: in about 40 to 50% of women the disease is triggered by pregnancy or by oral contraceptive use, which is where the older names "mask of pregnancy" and chloasma come from, though the same review notes those terms "do not fully characterize the disease."[^handel] Reported female-to-male ratios run 9 or 10 to 1, and the condition concentrates in people with medium-to-deep skin tones (Fitzpatrick III to V).[^handel] Both ultraviolet and visible light drive it, and it is chronic: it darkens in summer, fades in winter, and, per the same review, "long-term therapies are necessary, since recurrence rates are high."[^handel] That sets it apart from ordinary post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and other dark spots, which trace to a single earlier injury at that exact site rather than forming melasma's own symmetric, hormone-linked pattern; [[hyperpigmentation]] covers those other forms and how clinicians tell all three apart.

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians use to recognize melasma. They are for recognition, not self-diagnosis: only a clinician can make the diagnosis, often with a Wood's lamp or dermoscopy.[^handel]

- **Symmetric, blotchy patches, not single spots.** The patches are flat and tan to grey-brown, roughly mirror-image on both sides of the face, and cluster in two patterns: a central pattern across the forehead, nose, cheeks, upper lip, and chin, and a peripheral pattern along the cheekbones and jawline.[^handel]
- **Worsens with sun and heat.** Historical and modern descriptions agree that melasma "worsening was reported to happen after sun exposure, fire heat, cold and skin inflammations," and the same review cites bakers and other workers exposed to oven heat reporting difficulty treating melasma and worsening tied to their working conditions.[^handel]
- **Persistent and prone to come back.** Melasma is "a chronic acquired hypermelanosis of the skin," and because recurrence rates are high, the review states that long-term therapy is what it takes to keep it controlled, rather than a single course clearing it for good.[^handel]
- **Tell it apart from a single dark mark.** Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and sun-driven spots trace to a specific earlier injury or track wherever light lands, so each mark's location is explained by something that happened at that exact spot; melasma has no single preceding lesion and instead forms its own symmetric pattern.[^handel][^davis]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Several of the treatments with the best evidence are prescription drugs or in-office procedures, including the fixed triple-combination cream with hydroquinone, oral tranexamic acid, chemical peels, and lasers, and they require a prescriber or a procedure.[^liu][^delrosario] A new, changing, asymmetric, or single dark spot is a reason to see a board-certified dermatologist rather than assume melasma. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## Sun and visible light protection come first

Every melasma trial is run on a base of daily sunscreen, and the quality of that sun protection changes the outcome. In a double-blind randomized trial, 68 melasma patients (61 completed) all used 4% hydroquinone, and the group whose SPF 50+ sunscreen also contained iron oxide, which blocks visible light, showed 15% greater improvement on the Melasma Area and Severity Index, 28% greater colorimetric improvement, and 4% greater improvement on measured melanin than the group using a UV-only sunscreen, over 8 weeks.[^castanedo] The authors concluded "UV-VL sunscreen enhances the depigmenting efficacy of hydroquinone," evidence the same trial read as "a role for VL in melasma pathogenesis," meaning visible light drives the pigment alongside UV, which is why a tinted, iron-oxide sunscreen outperforms an untinted one in darker skin.[^castanedo] Broad-spectrum, visible-light-blocking sun protection is foundational: it is present in every trial below and it is the one step shown to change outcomes on its own, on top of whichever active is used. How UV filters cover the UVB and UVA spectrum is covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

## Topical actives with evidence

Graded against controlled trials and framed relative to hydroquinone, the long-used reference agent. [[hydroquinone]] is the reference depigmenting drug: it beats placebo, but a network meta-analysis of 59 randomized trials ranked hydroquinone monotherapy 13th of 14 melasma therapies for efficacy, while its FDA-approved fixed triple-combination form, paired with a retinoid and a corticosteroid, was "the most favorable selection among the topical drugs for melasma" in the same ranking, at the cost of more irritation and a label restricting it to short-term use.[^liu][^chan] [[tranexamic-acid]] is available both orally and topically; a placebo-controlled trial found oral tranexamic acid cut the modified Melasma Area and Severity Index by 49% versus 18% for placebo at 3 months, but three months after stopping, the reduction had fallen back toward 26% versus 19%, showing the effect depends on continued treatment.[^delrosario] [[azelaic-acid]] is a well-tolerated dicarboxylic acid, available over the counter and by prescription, that beat hydroquinone 2% (73% versus 19% good-to-excellent results in one RCT) and, across a 6-trial meta-analysis of 673 patients, produced a greater reduction in the severity index than hydroquinone overall, while trials against the stronger hydroquinone 4% trend toward equivalence rather than superiority.[^albzea] [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]] trailed hydroquinone 4% on patient-rated melasma improvement in the one head-to-head trial (62.5% versus 93% good-to-excellent, P<0.05) but tied it on colorimetric measurement, with far fewer side effects (6.2% versus 68.7%).[^espinal] [[niacinamide]] does not inhibit the tyrosinase enzyme the way hydroquinone does; it works by blocking pigment transfer to skin cells, and in one small independent trial 4% niacinamide was comparable to hydroquinone 4% (44% versus 55% good-to-excellent) with fewer side effects.[^navarrete][^hakozaki]

Two further options carry real but comparatively modest evidence. [[kojic-acid]] is a tyrosinase-inhibiting compound usually combined with hydroquinone or glycolic acid rather than used alone, and a pooled comparison across melasma treatments ranked its effect size below hydroquinone, azelaic acid, tranexamic acid, and cysteamine, while giving it one of the lowest irritation rates among the agents compared.[^chang] [[cysteamine]] is a 5% cream marketed as a non-hydroquinone option; it beats placebo, and in the same pooled comparison it did not differ significantly from hydroquinone 4%, though no single trial has shown it clearly beating hydroquinone, and its sulfur odor and irritation make it harder to tolerate.[^chang]

## Honest limits: relapse, and what is out of scope

None of the topicals above cures melasma. It is chronic and relapsing, it darkens with sun and heat, and even the best-performing options fade without ongoing sun protection: the oral tranexamic acid trial's 49% reduction had fallen back toward placebo within three months of stopping.[^handel][^delrosario] Aggressive procedures carry a specific risk that runs the other way: the same clinical review states plainly that melasma "can be triggered or aggravated by cosmetic procedures that induce skin inflammation, such as peelings and therapies with light/laser."[^handel] Separately, the review notes that patients with melasma and higher (darker) skin phenotypes have a higher general frequency of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, the discoloration that inflammation can leave behind.[^handel] Chemical peels and laser or light-based treatments for melasma are prescriber-managed decisions that weigh a person's skin type, history, and the procedure's own risk of making the pigment worse, not something covered here.

## The Evidence

The ranking anchor for topical melasma treatments is a network meta-analysis of 59 randomized trials across 14 therapies, scored on the Melasma Area and Severity Index; it found the fixed hydroquinone triple-combination cream the most favorable topical option and hydroquinone monotherapy near the bottom of the ranking despite beating placebo.[^liu] A double-blind RCT isolated the contribution of visible-light protection, showing an iron-oxide sunscreen improved outcomes over a UV-only sunscreen in patients otherwise using identical hydroquinone treatment.[^castanedo] A placebo-controlled RCT established oral tranexamic acid's real but treatment-dependent benefit.[^delrosario] Head-to-head and pooled trials against hydroquinone establish azelaic acid, vitamin C, and niacinamide's comparative standing, and a separate pooled comparison ranks kojic acid and cysteamine against the same set of agents.[^albzea][^espinal][^navarrete][^hakozaki][^chang] The epidemiological and clinical picture, including hormonal triggers, the female predominance, heat and light as aggravating factors, and the risk that inflammatory procedures pose, comes from a dedicated clinical and epidemiological review.[^handel] Across these sources, the practical convergence is consistent: light exposure, both UV and visible, drives the pigment, sun protection is the one step that both treats and helps prevent relapse, and the depigmenting actives produce real but modest, comparator-dependent gains that fade without it.[^handel][^liu][^castanedo][^delrosario]
