Hives that keep coming back for more than six weeks with no identifiable trigger. **The one thing to take away:** no topical product treats this. The treatment ladder is oral antihistamines first and injected biologics second, and both FDA-approved biologics are licensed only for people who are still symptomatic despite antihistamines.[^xolair][^dupixent]

## How to know you have this

The international guideline defines urticaria as "a condition characterized by the development of wheals (hives), angioedema or both", and a wheal by three features together: "a sharply circumscribed superficial central swelling of variable size and shape, almost invariably surrounded by reflex erythema", "an itching or sometimes burning sensation", and "a fleeting nature, with the skin returning to its normal appearance, usually within 30 min to 24 h".[^guideline]

**That last feature is the useful one.** An individual hive comes and goes within a day. A red itchy patch that stays put for days is something else, and the distinction matters because most of what the site covers, from [[eczema]] to [[psoriasis]], does stay put.

Angioedema is the deeper version: "a sudden, pronounced erythematous or skin colored deep swelling in the lower dermis and subcutis or mucous membranes", with "tingling, burning, tightness, and sometimes pain rather than itch" and "a resolution slower than that of wheals (can take up to 72 h)".[^guideline]

**Then the clock and the trigger.** "Acute urticaria is defined as the occurrence of wheals, angioedema or both for 6 weeks or less. Chronic urticaria (CU) is defined as the occurrence of wheals, angioedema, or both for more than 6 weeks."[^guideline] Chronic urticaria splits in two by whether a trigger can be identified: "When the clinical course has no definite physical eliciting factor involved and shows a spontaneous course, it is described as chronic spontaneous urticaria".[^guideline] Where a definite trigger exists, cold or pressure or sunlight, it is chronic inducible urticaria instead, which is a different diagnosis with different management.[^guideline]

This is a diagnosis to get from a clinician, not from a page. The guideline describes a diagnostic workup including blood tests, and notes that even the tests used to predict who responds to omalizumab have "limited" diagnostic performance.[^guideline]

## The Evidence

**No tier list here, deliberately.** The ladder's first rung, second-generation H1 antihistamines, has no page on this site, and neither does omalizumab. Grading a ladder while two of its three rungs are missing would mislead, so the ladder is stated in prose instead.

**What the labels actually license.** Both approved biologics are second-line by their own wording. Omalizumab is indicated for CSU "in adults and adolescents 12 years of age and older who remain symptomatic despite H1 antihistamine treatment".[^xolair] Dupilumab is indicated "for the treatment of adult and pediatric patients aged 2 years and older with chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) who remain symptomatic despite H1 antihistamine treatment".[^dupixent] Dupilumab reaches younger children and omalizumab does not, which is the clearest practical difference between them on the labels.

**Both labels carry the same limitation, in the same words: "Not indicated for other forms of urticaria."**[^xolair][^dupixent] That excludes the inducible urticarias, and it is the reason the spontaneous-versus-inducible distinction above is not academic.

**Why this is a mast cell disease and not a barrier disease.** The guideline calls urticaria "a frequent, mast cell-driven disease".[^guideline] Nothing applied to the skin surface reaches that mechanism, which is why moisturisers, barrier creams and the rest of this site's usual territory have no role beyond comfort.
