Athlete's foot (tinea pedis) is a fungal infection of the feet that normally occurs in the outermost layer of the skin, and the skin between the toes is a frequent site, where it can cause pain and itchiness.[^cochrane] It is caused by dermatophyte fungi, the same group of organisms treated by [[terbinafine]], and it is managed with topical antifungals rather than skincare cosmetics.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice. Infection that does not clear with an over-the-counter antifungal, spreads, involves the toenails, or occurs in someone with diabetes or a weakened immune system is worth a doctor's evaluation.

## How to know you have this

Athlete's foot typically shows up in the skin between the toes, a frequent site of infection that "can cause pain and itchiness."[^cochrane] Because it lives in the outermost layer of the skin, it commonly looks like scaling, flaking, or cracking, and it can spread to the sole and sides of the foot. A related dermatophyte infection of the toenails (onychomycosis) can affect the entire nail plate and is harder to treat with topical products alone.[^cochrane] A persistent or uncertain rash on the feet is worth confirming with a clinician, since other conditions can look similar.

## Where on the foot it is changes what to buy

**Three common over-the-counter antifungals treat that distinction three different ways, and the labels say so.** [[butenafine]] limits its claim outright: "cures most athlete's foot between the toes. Effectiveness on the bottom or sides of foot is unknown."[^butenafine-label] [[terbinafine]] claims "cures most athlete's foot (tinea pedis)" without that limit, then splits the course by location in its directions, "between the toes only" twice daily "for 1 week" against "on the bottom or sides of the foot" twice daily "for 2 weeks".[^terbinafine-label] [[clotrimazole]] claims athlete's foot generally and directs four weeks of twice-daily use regardless, while telling users to "pay special attention to spaces between the toes".[^clotrimazole-label]

**What that does and does not tell you.** Practically, a cream claiming only the toe webs is the wrong purchase for scaling across the sole, and the same drug may need double the course there. What it is not is evidence that one agent beats another on the sole: the pooled Cochrane review these grades rest on covers "fungal infections of the skin and nails of the foot" as a whole and, checked by string search of its abstract, never reports results by distribution at all.[^cochrane] These label differences record what each sponsor studied and chose to claim.

## The Evidence

The evidence base is a Cochrane review of 67 randomized trials of topical treatments for fungal infections of the foot.[^cochrane] Its finding is practical: the antifungal drug classes all cure far more infections than placebo, and topical allylamines (terbinafine) cure slightly more than azoles, so an over-the-counter [[terbinafine]] cream is the best-evidenced first choice, with the azole [[ketoconazole]] and the hydroxypyridone [[ciclopirox]] as effective alternatives. The graded options are in the tier list above; the placebo-controlled pooled risk ratios of treatment failure were 0.33 for allylamines, 0.30 for azoles, and 0.27 for ciclopiroxolamine, and a meta-analysis of 11 head-to-head trials gave a risk ratio of 0.63 in favour of allylamines over azoles.[^cochrane] Ciclopirox's point estimate is numerically the lowest, but it rests on fewer trials, reflected in its much wider confidence interval (0.11 to 0.66).[^cochrane]
