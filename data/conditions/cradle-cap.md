## How to know you have this

Cradle cap is what infantile seborrhoeic dermatitis is called when it appears on the scalp. Cochrane describes the condition as "a chronic, inflammatory, scaling skin condition, which causes redness and a greasy scaling rash in infants and young children", and notes it "can last from weeks to months, but rarely years".[^cochrane] Most affected children in the trials were under seven months old.[^cochrane]

The single most important thing to know comes from the same source: it is "benign and self-limiting", and it is parents rather than babies who tend to be distressed by it.[^cochrane] A baby with cradle cap is not usually itchy or unwell. If the rash is weeping, spreading rapidly, or the child seems unwell, that is a reason to see a doctor rather than to try another product.

## What the evidence says

> **Everything below rests on very low-certainty evidence, and the condition gets better anyway.** The 2019 Cochrane review found six randomised trials covering 310 children, and concluded there is "uncertainty regarding the effectiveness and safety of studied treatments due to the very low-certainty evidence for all comparisons and outcomes".[^cochrane] Its most useful sentence for a parent choosing a product is this one: "In most studies, the prognosis for the condition was favourable regardless of intervention".[^cochrane]

That single finding is why the ranking above is so flat. When a condition resolves on its own in weeks, any study without a control group will show a treatment working, because the babies would have improved regardless. The review is explicit that future trials "should compare against placebo, no treatment, or standard care".[^cochrane]

Two details are worth knowing. The trials were short, "between 10 and 42 days' duration", and only one followed children until the rash resolved or they reached eight months.[^cochrane] And two of the six trials tested **oral biotin** against placebo, a B vitamin given by mouth for a scalp rash, which is a fair measure of how unsettled this area is.[^cochrane]

The one trial that does compare two active treatments head to head is worth reading properly. In 72 infants, each child had a licochalcone-containing moisturiser on one side of the rash and 1% hydrocortisone on the other. The moisturiser cleared faster at day 3 to 4, 42% against 32%, but "by the end of the first week, this difference was no longer significant", and at two weeks the cure rates were 90% and 92%.[^wananukul] Two very different products, nearly identical two-week results, in a condition that resolves by itself.

## What not to read into a product claim

The study that put this page in the queue is a good example of the trap. A gel cream combining piroctone olamine, biosaccharide gum-2, [[stearyl-glycyrrhetinate]] and [[zinc-pca]] was tested in ten infants, open-label, with no control group, and reported significant reductions in redness and scaling at 15 and 30 days.[^micali] In a self-limiting condition over 30 days, that result is what you would expect from doing nothing. The authors are careful to call the condition "a benign and self-limiting variant of seborrheic dermatitis" in their own first sentence.[^micali]

The adult condition is a different matter with a different evidence base; see [[seborrheic-dermatitis]] and [[dandruff]], and note Cochrane's own remark that its infant findings were "Unlike the reviews investigating the effects of treatments in adults".[^cochrane]
