Keratinocyte carcinoma is the umbrella term for the two commonest skin cancers, basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. The National Cancer Institute's treatment summary states that "There are three main types of skin cancer: Basal cell carcinoma (BCC). Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Melanoma", and that "BCC and SCC are the most common forms of skin cancer and together are referred to as nonmelanoma skin cancers".[^pdq] The third type is on [[melanoma]], and whether to be screened at all is on [[skin-cancer-screening]].

> **This is a clinical diagnosis and a clinical treatment, and the only part of it skincare touches is prevention.** Nothing on this page is a product recommendation. A lesion that is new, changing, bleeding, or simply not healing is a reason to see a clinician, not to try an active on it.

## How to know you have this

**You do not, not reliably, and that is the point.** NCI's summary is written for clinicians and offers no consumer checklist, because the step that settles it is a clinical examination and usually a biopsy. What the summary does describe are patterns rather than tests. One BCC subtype, the morpheaform type, "typically appears as a scar-like, firm plaque", and NCI notes it is "difficult to treat adequately with traditional treatments" precisely "Because of indistinct clinical tumor margins".[^pdq] A cancer that can look like a scar, with edges a surgeon cannot see clearly, is not something a mirror resolves.

The precursor lesion is easier to picture. NCI describes actinic keratoses as "typically red, scaly patches" that "usually arise on areas of chronically sun-exposed skin and are likely to be found on the face and dorsal aspects of the hand", while stressing that they "are potential precursors of SCC, but the rate of progression is extremely low, and most do not become SCCs".[^pdq] That sits on [[actinic-keratosis]].

**The practical rule is behavioural, not visual.** A lesion that changes, bleeds, or does not heal is worth a clinician's eye regardless of what it looks like.

## How Common, and Why Nobody Knows Exactly

**The honest answer starts with a gap in the data.** NCI states that "The total number and incidence rate of nonmelanoma skin cancers cannot be estimated precisely because reporting to cancer registries is not required".[^pdq] What exists instead is an extrapolation: "based on extrapolation of Medicare fee-for-service data to the U.S. population, it has been estimated that the total number of people treated for nonmelanoma skin cancers in 2012 was about 3.3 million".[^pdq] For scale, NCI notes that figure "exceeds all other annual new cases of cancer estimated by the American Cancer Society, which total about 2 million".[^pdq] So more people are treated for these two cancers than for every other cancer combined, and the number is an estimate from billing data rather than a count.

**A terminology note worth carrying.** Two NCI summaries use two different umbrella terms for the same pair. The treatment summary quoted here says "nonmelanoma skin cancers"; the screening summary cited on [[skin-cancer-screening]] says keratinocyte carcinoma. They refer to the same thing, and the newer term avoids defining a disease by what it is not.

## The Evidence: BCC and SCC Behave Differently

**Basal cell carcinoma is the slow one.** NCI describes BCCs as "slow growing and rarely metastasize", while adding that "BCC tends to be locally destructive and can result in serious deforming damage if left untreated or if local recurrences cannot be completely excised".[^pdq] Rarely spreading is not the same as harmless, and the damage described is local and disfiguring. NCI names, among high-risk areas for recurrence after initial treatment, "the central face (e.g., periorbital region, eyelids, nasolabial fold, or nose-cheek angle), postauricular region, pinna, ear canal, forehead, and scalp".[^pdq]

**Squamous cell carcinoma is the one that can spread.** NCI states that SCCs "are composed of keratinizing cells", that "These tumors are more aggressive than BCCs and have a range of growth, invasive, and metastatic potential", and that prognosis "is associated with the degree of differentiation".[^pdq] That difference is why the two are grouped for epidemiology but assessed separately in the clinic.

## Treatment Is Not a Skincare Decision

NCI lists the treatment options for localized BCC as surgical excision with margin evaluation, Mohs micrographic surgery, radiation therapy, curettage and electrodesiccation, cryosurgery, photodynamic therapy, topical fluorouracil, imiquimod topical therapy, and carbon dioxide laser, with hedgehog pathway inhibitors for metastatic or locally advanced disease.[^pdq] Two of those are topical creams, which is precisely why this page exists: topical fluorouracil and imiquimod are prescription treatments for a cancer, chosen and monitored by a clinician, and they are not interchangeable with anything sold as skincare.

**No tier list on this page.** Every option above is a clinical procedure or a prescription drug, none has a graded page on this site, and ranking cancer treatments is not something this site is in a position to do. The precursor lesion that does sit closer to skincare is on [[actinic-keratosis]], which NCI covers in the same summary as "the related noninvasive lesion".[^pdq]

## What Skincare Actually Contributes

Prevention, and that is not a small thing. The graded evidence for daily photoprotection is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], and the practical case for it belongs there rather than being restated here.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Diagnosis of either cancer requires a clinician and usually a biopsy, and nothing here should be used to decide that a lesion is or is not one.
