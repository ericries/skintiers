Vitiligo is "an acquired, chronic, depigmenting disorder of the skin, in which pigment-producing cells (melanocytes) that determine the colour of skin, hair, and eyes are progressively lost," leaving milky-white patches (leukoderma).[^dermnet] It is "currently widely accepted that vitiligo is the result of autoimmune destruction of melanocytes," not a cosmetic pigmentation problem.[^dermnet] This is a medical condition: diagnosis and treatment belong with a dermatologist, and nothing on this page is a substitute for that care.

## How to know you have this

Vitiligo shows up as well-defined, chalk- or milky-white macules and patches with convex borders, usually asymptomatic, that can coalesce into larger areas.[^dermnet] It favors sun-exposed sites and areas of repeated friction or injury (the Koebner phenomenon), and also body folds and the nipples.[^dermnet] It affects an estimated 0.5-2% of the population, with no reliable way to predict how much pigment will be lost or how fast; extension typically happens in cycles of active loss followed by stable periods, sometimes with partial spontaneous repigmentation from the hair follicles in between.[^dermnet] Loss of hair color (poliosis) in affected areas occurs in 10-60% of patients and can predict a poorer response to treatment because it signals fewer melanocyte reserves in the hair follicle.[^dermnet]

## What is happening in the skin

Genetics account for roughly 80% of vitiligo risk, environment for the rest.[^dermnet] The accepted model is autoimmune: oxidative stress makes melanocytes more fragile, CD8+ T cells release interferon-gamma that drives chemokine signaling and melanocyte apoptosis, and resident memory T cells in the skin then maintain an "autoimmune memory" that causes relapses at the same sites.[^dermnet] Vitiligo also clusters with other autoimmune disease, most strongly thyroid disease, which affects up to 15% of adults with vitiligo.[^dermnet] More than 20-30% of people with vitiligo have a first- or second-degree relative with it.[^dermnet]

## What actually works

There is no cure, and DermNet is blunt that "treatment is often unsatisfactory": the realistic goals are stopping progression and achieving partial repigmentation, and response is best on the face and trunk and poorest on the hands, feet, and areas with white hair.[^dermnet] Narrowband UVB phototherapy is the traditional mainstay for widespread disease; a DermNet-cited meta-analysis of 35 studies found a "marked or clinically useful response" in 36% of patients after 12 months of narrowband UVB and 62% after 12 months of PUVA, with the face and neck responding best.[^dermnet] Phototherapy is a light-based procedure delivered in a clinic or with a prescribed home device, not a topical product, so it sits outside this site's tier list but is a real first-line option to discuss with a dermatologist.

Among topicals, [[opzelura-ruxolitinib-cream]] (ruxolitinib cream) is the first and only FDA-approved treatment for repigmenting nonsegmental vitiligo, a JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor that blocks the interferon-gamma signaling driving the disease.[^dermnet][^dailymed] [[tacrolimus-topical|Topical tacrolimus]] and pimecrolimus are preferred for the eyelids, face, neck, and skin folds because they do not cause the skin thinning that long-term steroid use can.[^dermnet] [[topical-corticosteroids|Topical corticosteroids]] can be used on the trunk and limbs for up to three months.[^dermnet] Topical vitamin D derivatives are considered second-line and effective mainly in combination with other treatments.[^dermnet] Sun protection matters for a different reason here than in most skin conditions: depigmented patches cannot tan, so they burn easily on UV exposure, and sunburn can trigger the disease to spread, while a tan on the surrounding normal skin makes vitiligo patches look more visible by contrast.[^dermnet]

## What's overhyped

No topical, supplement, or "natural" remedy repigments vitiligo reliably; DermNet flags pseudocatalase and topical prostaglandin inhibitors as still unproven, with "further studies... needed to confirm efficacy."[^dermnet] For stable, longstanding patches that have not responded to medical therapy, surgical options (melanocyte transplantation, punch grafting) or depigmentation of the remaining normal skin exist, but these are procedures for a dermatologist to evaluate, not something a skincare routine achieves.[^dermnet] Camouflage makeup and self-tanners (dihydroxyacetone) can even out appearance cosmetically but do not treat the underlying disease.[^dermnet]

## The Evidence

Ruxolitinib cream's approval rests on two identically designed phase 3 trials (TRuE-V1 and TRuE-V2, 674 patients with nonsegmental vitiligo, mean 7.4% affected body surface area) that randomized patients 2:1 to ruxolitinib cream or vehicle twice daily for 24 weeks. At least 75% facial repigmentation (F-VASI75) was reached by 29.9% of ruxolitinib patients in both trials, versus 7.5% on vehicle in TRuE-V1 and 12.9% on vehicle in TRuE-V2; a deeper response (F-VASI90) was reached by roughly 15% of ruxolitinib patients versus 2% on vehicle.[^dailymed] Response on the body was less dramatic than on the face, and DermNet notes "sustained safety after 52 weeks" was shown across the two trials.[^dermnet] Full trial detail and the drug's boxed warning live on [[opzelura-ruxolitinib-cream]].
