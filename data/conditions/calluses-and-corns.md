Calluses and corns are thickened skin, and the over-the-counter pharmacy for them is narrow: essentially one active, [[salicylic-acid]], sold at two strengths. The useful thing to know before buying any of it is how long the one randomized trial in corns says this actually takes.

**The label says 14 days. The trial says months.** That gap is the single most useful thing on this page, and it is set out under The Evidence below.

## How to know you have this

The trial's most useful finding is not about treatment at all, it is about location. Corns on the top of the toe or between the toes resolved far faster than corns on the sole: median 5.9 months for dorsal and interdigital corns against 14.9 months for plantar corns, a difference that reached significance where the treatment comparison did not.[^rct] Where the lesion sits tells you more about how long it will take than which remedy you pick.

The packaging will not tell you what you have. The same 40% plaster is labelled "Corn Remover Wart remover" in a single purpose line, so the box does not distinguish a corn from a plantar wart.[^label] That matters because the two look similar on a sole and are not the same problem. This site has no warts page yet, so it is not the place to work out which you have.

One thing on the label does change what you should do. It says plainly: "Do not use if you are a diabetic if you have poor blood circulation on irritated skin or any area that is infected or reddened".[^label] Foot hyperkeratosis in someone with diabetes or impaired circulation is a clinical matter, not a self-treatment one.

## The Evidence

One randomized trial read for this page tests the thing the shelf actually sells. A 40% salicylic acid corn plaster directs users to "repeat procedure every 48 hours as needed for up to 14 days (until corn is removed)".[^label] In a trial of 324 corns in 201 participants, median time to resolution was 10.0 months with salicylic acid plasters against 13.4 months in the scalpel-debridement comparator group.[^rct] Whatever these products do, they do not do it in a fortnight.

The trial is also more equivocal than that comparison suggests. Its abstract reports that the rate of resolution was substantively higher with the plasters, and in the same breath that treatment was not significantly related to time to corn recurrence across the full 12 months, with a hazard ratio of 1.189 and a confidence interval from 0.780 to 1.813.[^rct] Only the abstract was read, so those two statements are reported as they stand rather than reconciled here. Treatment was not significantly related to the quality-of-life or foot-pain-and-disability measures either.[^rct]

## What the OTC shelf actually contains

Searching the federal drug listing for products whose brand name contains corn or callus returns 120 records, and the pattern is uniform: salicylic acid at 17% in liquids and solutions, or 40% in plasters, patches and pads, submitted as over-the-counter monograph drugs rather than approved under their own applications.[^ndc] Regulatory status generally is on [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

Two things in that listing are worth knowing before you shop. Several products pair the 17% salicylic acid with bee venom, declared as Apis mellifera venom at 1 g per 100 mL; the salicylic acid is the part with an indication, and nothing read for this page supports the venom.[^ndc] And at least one liquid sold as a wart, corn and callus remover declares no monograph keratolytic at all, listing Angelica dahurica root and asiatic acid as its only actives while still being submitted in the monograph category.[^ndc] Marketing category in that listing is supplied by the labeller, not adjudicated by the agency, which is the gap such a product sits in.
