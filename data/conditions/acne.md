Acne vulgaris is the most common skin condition treated by dermatologists, driven by a mix of follicular hyperkeratinization, sebum, *Cutibacterium acnes*, and inflammation. Graded skeptically against controlled evidence for **topical skincare**, the best-studied over-the-counter and prescription topicals produce **modest** lesion reductions, the gap between the "best" and "second-best" topical is often smaller than the marketing implies, and the single largest evidence base for these treatments is rated moderate to very low certainty.[^1][^2]

## How to know you have this

These are the features clinicians look for, described so a reader can recognize the pattern rather than self-diagnose. The American Academy of Dermatology's 2024 guideline characterizes acne by two kinds of lesion appearing together, non-inflammatory comedones and inflammatory papules, pustules, and nodules, on the face or trunk.[^8] StatPearls describes the same picture, "comedones (blackheads), and inflammatory papules, pustules, and nodules," appearing "primarily on the face."[^9] Only a clinician can make the diagnosis.

- **Blackheads and whiteheads are the sign that points to acne.** The lesion that sets acne apart is the comedo, and it comes in two forms. StatPearls describes open comedones (blackheads) as a plugged follicle that "appears as papules with a central, dilated follicular orifice containing gray, brown, or black keratotic material," and closed comedones (whiteheads) as forming "when keratin and sebum block the pilosebaceous orifice beneath the skin surface."[^9]
- **Red bumps and pus spots.** Inflammatory lesions sit alongside the comedones. StatPearls describes the progression from "a small papule with erythema" to pustules and, when "many pustules coalesce," to the "nodules and cysts called nodulocystic acne."[^9]
- **Where it shows up.** Acne "occurs on the affected individuals' face, neck, chest, upper back, and upper arm areas, where large, hormonally responsive sebaceous glands are abundant," per StatPearls.[^9] The Lancet review likewise locates the affected follicles "on the face, neck, chest, and back."[^10]
- **Who tends to get it.** Acne "is commonly observed in adolescents and young adults," with StatPearls putting prevalence among adolescents at "35% to over 90%."[^9]

**Telling it apart from look-alikes.** The feature that separates acne from its common mimics is the comedo. StatPearls notes that "rosacea can be differentiated from acne by the presence of comedones and the absence of telangiectasia": acne carries blackheads and whiteheads and lacks visible dilated vessels, while rosacea shows the reverse, sitting on central-face flushing and visible vessels without comedones.[^9] That centrofacial redness pattern is covered on [[rosacea]]. The itchy, eczematous rash of eczema is defined by different features again, in the skin folds rather than by comedones; [[atopic-dermatitis]] covers that pattern. Because features overlap, separating acne from its look-alikes still calls for clinical examination.[^8]

**Medical disclaimer.** This is educational content, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat any individual. Acne is frequently a *medical* condition: moderate-to-severe, nodulocystic, or scarring acne - and acne that is not improving on topicals - warrants evaluation by a board-certified dermatologist, because untreated inflammatory acne can leave permanent scars. Several of the most effective acne treatments are **oral prescription drugs that fall outside this page's topical-skincare scope**: oral antibiotics, oral isotretinoin, and hormonal therapy (e.g., combined oral contraceptives, spironolactone). They are named here so readers know they exist; they require a prescriber, carry their own risks and monitoring, and are not graded on this page. Nothing here should be used to delay appropriate medical care.

## What Actually Works

Ranked by effect size against controlled evidence. The anchor is a 179-RCT network meta-analysis (Mavranezouli 2022), which pooled ~35,000 observations across 49 treatment classes and reported each treatment's percentage lesion reduction versus placebo, with 95% credible intervals (CrI).[^1] The lesion-reduction figures and ranks cited below come from its mild-to-moderate acne efficacy network, which the authors report comprised 90 RCTs and 41 treatment classes, so "rank 15" means 15th of those 41.[^1] Read the intervals rather than the point estimates alone: most of the monotherapy intervals overlap heavily, so the fine ordering among them (ranks 2 to 5 below) is soft.

**1. Retinoid + benzoyl peroxide combination - Effect: notable (relative to other topicals) · Evidence: solid.** The top-ranked *topical* option. In the network meta-analysis, a topical retinoid combined with benzoyl peroxide reduced lesions **26.16%** vs placebo (95% CrI 16.75-35.36, rank 15), clearly ahead of either component alone, and the authors identified a combined retinoid with benzoyl peroxide as the most effective topical pharmacological option for mild-to-moderate acne.[^1] The independent Cochrane review agrees directionally, finding combination regimens outperform benzoyl-peroxide monotherapy.[^2] The two ingredients act by different mechanisms - comedolytic ([[tretinoin]]/[[adapalene]]) plus oxidative antibacterial ([[benzoyl-peroxide]]) - which is why they are combined rather than substituted. This is the strongest topical starting point supported by the evidence.

**2. Topical retinoid - [[tretinoin]] or [[adapalene]] - Effect: modest · Evidence: solid.** As monotherapy, topical retinoids reduced lesions **18.27%** vs placebo (95% CrI 10.28-26.14, rank ~23).[^1] [[adapalene]] gel 0.1% and [[tretinoin]] cream 0.05% are of *equivalent* efficacy head-to-head (n=409, 10-week RCT), but adapalene is "significantly better tolerated ... in terms of erythema, dryness, desquamation and stinging/burning," which makes it the gentler default.[^3] Retinoids are the backbone of the #1 combination.

**3. [[benzoyl-peroxide]] - Effect: modest · Evidence: mixed.** Benzoyl peroxide monotherapy reduced lesions **15.60%** vs placebo in the network meta-analysis (95% CrI 6.02-25.11, rank 25).[^1] The Cochrane review found it may be more effective than placebo (risk ratio **1.27**, 95% CI 1.12-1.45; 3 RCTs, 2,234 participants) but rated that evidence **low-certainty**, and found "little to no difference" versus [[adapalene]] (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.90-1.10; 5 RCTs, 1,472) or topical clindamycin (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.68-1.34; 1 RCT, 240).[^2] Its distinctive value is that *C. acnes* does not develop resistance to it - useful as a partner to a topical antibiotic - not raw potency.

**4. [[salicylic-acid]] - Effect: modest · Evidence: moderate (leave-on) / uncertain (peels).** In a 500-patient, 16-week evaluator-blind RCT (improvement assessed at 12 weeks), a 2% leave-on salicylic-acid hydrogel matched [[adapalene]] gel (improvement rate 51.01% vs 43.10%; p=0.0831, no significant difference) with comparably low adverse-event rates.[^4] Caveat worth flagging: the network meta-analysis captured salicylic acid only as in-office **chemical peels** (39.70% vs placebo, 95% CrI 12.54-66.78, rank 9) - a *physical* treatment with a very wide interval and high uncertainty, not the OTC leave-on most shoppers mean.[^1] So the leave-on evidence is decent but thinner than the retinoid/BPO base.

**5. [[azelaic-acid]] - Effect: modest · Evidence: solid (but weakest signal here).** A systematic review pooling 16 acne RCTs found azelaic acid "more effective than vehicle for improving global assessments and reducing acne severity."[^5] Be honest about the network meta-analysis, though: there azelaic acid had the lowest point estimate of the mainstream topicals and its interval **crossed zero** (9.54% vs placebo, 95% CrI -1.83-20.59, rank 31).[^1] A legitimate, well-tolerated option - see [[azelaic-acid]] for its full grade - but positioned last among the evidence-backed topicals for acne.

## What's Overhyped

Quarantined and discounted. Treat everything here as unsupported unless it appears in The Evidence, and note what does *not* appear: none of the DIY remedies below is among the 49 treatment classes the network meta-analysis found worth pooling - there are no controlled acne trials establishing them.[^1]

- **"Natural" / DIY spot fixes - toothpaste, lemon, baking soda.** No controlled evidence that any of these treats acne, and each carries a documented downside. Lemon juice is a specific hazard: citrus contains furocoumarins (psoralens) that cause **phytophotodermatitis** - a phototoxic burn-like reaction with blistering and hyperpigmentation - when juice on the skin meets sunlight.[^7] Baking soda and toothpaste are alkaline/irritant and disrupt the skin barrier.
- **Most "acne" cleansers.** Every topical graded above earned its evidence as a **leave-on** product with sustained skin contact; the RCTs behind them tested leave-on formulations.[^1][^2][^3][^4] A cleanser is rinsed off in seconds, giving active ingredients minimal contact time. Cleansing removes surface oil and is fine hygiene, but a rinse-off "acne wash" does not deliver the same treatment exposure as the leave-on product its label borrows its actives from.
- **Pore strips.** They mechanically pull sebaceous filaments and surface debris off the nose, a cosmetic and temporary effect. Like the DIY remedies above, pore strips are not among the 49 treatment classes the network meta-analysis found worth pooling, and no controlled acne trial shows a benefit for follicular hyperkeratinization or inflammation; their effect is grooming, not treatment.[^1]
- **"Higher strength clears faster."** In a head-to-head RCT, 5% benzoyl peroxide reduced inflammatory lesions **75.0%** versus **72.7%** for 2.5% - a 2.3-point difference - while both far exceeded placebo (**41.7%**). Adverse-event incidence was 58.8% with 5% BPO and 56.4% with 2.5%, both higher than placebo, but with "no obvious difference between the 2.5% and 5% BPO groups."[^6] The large jump, in both lesion reduction and adverse-event incidence, is between having an effective active and not, not between the two BPO strengths.

## The Evidence

**The ranking anchor: Mavranezouli 2022 (BJD).** A systematic review and network meta-analysis of **179 RCTs** (~35,000 observations, 49 treatment classes across the whole review; the efficacy networks used 90 RCTs and 41 treatment classes for mild-to-moderate and 56 RCTs for moderate-to-severe acne) estimated percentage lesion change versus placebo with 95% credible intervals.[^1] For mild-to-moderate acne it found a combined retinoid + benzoyl peroxide most effective among topical options (26.16%, 95% CrI 16.75-35.36), with topical retinoid monotherapy at 18.27% (95% CrI 10.28-26.14), benzoyl peroxide at 15.60% (95% CrI 6.02-25.11), salicylic-acid chemical peels at 39.70% (95% CrI 12.54-66.78), and azelaic acid at 9.54% (95% CrI -1.83-20.59). **Certainty limits are central, not a footnote:** the authors rated the quality of included RCTs "moderate to very low" with "high" overall uncertainty, especially for physical treatments - which is exactly why the salicylic-acid-peel point estimate, despite ranking 9th, should be read with its very wide interval in mind, and why the fine ordering among the monotherapies is not statistically robust.[^1]

**Benzoyl peroxide: large evidence base, modest certainty (Cochrane 2020).** The Cochrane review found benzoyl peroxide "may be more effective than placebo" (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.12-1.45; 3 RCTs, 2,234 participants) but with "little to no difference" versus adapalene (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.90-1.10; 5 RCTs, 1,472) or clindamycin (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.68-1.34; 1 RCT, 240) - all **low-certainty**. Treatment discontinuation for adverse effects roughly doubled versus placebo (RR 2.13, 95% CI 1.55-2.93; 24 RCTs, 13,744), driven by "erythema, pruritus, and skin burning."[^2] The surprising takeaway for so ubiquitous a drug: the effect is modest and no better than its main rivals.

**Retinoid head-to-head (Cunliffe 2002).** A 10-week, multicentre, investigator-masked RCT (n=409) found adapalene gel 0.1% "demonstrated equivalent efficacy in reduction of acne lesion counts and global improvement" versus tretinoin cream 0.05%, and was "significantly better tolerated."[^3]

**Salicylic acid leave-on (Ye 2024).** A multicenter, randomized, evaluator-blind RCT (n=500; 249 vs 251) found a 2% supramolecular salicylic-acid hydrogel and adapalene gel "equally effective in the treatment of mild to moderate acne vulgaris" (12-week improvement rates 51.01% vs 43.10%; p=0.0831), with no difference in adverse-event rates.[^4]

**Azelaic acid (King 2023).** A systematic review pooling 16 acne RCTs found azelaic acid "more effective than vehicle for improving global assessments and reducing acne severity."[^5] Its full, indication-by-indication grade is on the [[azelaic-acid]] profile.

**Strength does not equal speed (Kawashima 2017).** A 12-week, placebo-controlled RCT reported median inflammatory-lesion reductions of 72.7% (2.5% BPO) and 75.0% (5% BPO) versus 41.7% (placebo) - the two strengths near-identical to each other (a 2.3-point gap), both far above placebo.[^6]

Across these, three methodologically independent syntheses converge on the headline - the network meta-analysis (ranking), the Cochrane review (combinations beat monotherapy; monotherapies are interchangeable), and the constituent head-to-head RCTs (adapalene about equal to tretinoin; salicylic acid about equal to adapalene) - while noting they draw on much of the same underlying trial evidence.[^1][^2][^3][^4]
