Phytophotodermatitis is the burn-like rash you get when certain plant juices land on skin that then sees sunlight. It is "a non-immunologic skin reaction triggered by certain plants followed by exposure to ultraviolet radiation emitted by sunlight".[^life] The nickname margarita burn comes from its most common modern cause: squeezing limes outdoors.

> **The one thing to understand: this is not an allergy.** It is a phototoxic chemical reaction, so it does not require a sensitised immune system and can happen to anyone given enough plant juice and enough sun. That also means avoiding it is purely mechanical, which is wash it off and cover up, rather than anything a product can do. The rash itself fades; the **brown marks it leaves behind are the part that lasts months**, and those are treated as [[hyperpigmentation]], not as an ongoing burn.

## How to know you have this

The shape is the strongest clue. Because the reaction only happens where plant juice actually touched skin and then met sunlight, the rash reproduces the splash, the drip, the streak or the handprint, with sharp irregular borders rather than the diffuse, symmetrical spread of eczema. Redness and burning come first, sometimes with fluid blisters and swelling, and brown marks follow as the inflammation settles.[^life] A dermatologist confirms it from that history and appearance; the 2024 review notes "patch testing, Wood's lamp examination, or skin biopsy" among the approaches used in diagnosis, though patch testing is for excluding an allergic cause rather than proving this one.[^life]

## What Causes It

The culprits are furanocoumarins (psoralens), plant compounds that become reactive under UV light. The mechanism is "the interaction between plant-derived photosensitizing compounds (e.g., furanocoumarins and psoralens) and ultraviolet light leading to skin damage (e.g., erythema, fluid blisters, edema, and hyperpigmentation)".[^life]

A 2024 review names the plant families worth recognising as "Apiaceae, Rutaceae, and Moraceae", and the specific offenders as "Heracleum mantegazzianum, Ruta graveolens, Ficus carica, and Pastinaca sativa", that is giant hogweed, rue, fig and wild parsnip, adding that "limes containing furocoumarin have been linked to lime-induced photodermatitis".[^life] In practice that covers bartending and cooking with citrus, gardening and strimming near hogweed or parsnip, and handling figs or rue.

## The Misdiagnosis That Matters

This condition has a serious diagnostic twin, and it runs in **both** directions.

A 2024 systematic review gathered "21 case reports or series encompassing 29 patients" of children investigated for both dermatitis and non-accidental trauma. Among the 26 initially investigated as non-accidental trauma, "26.9% involving Child Protective Services", the final diagnoses were "irritant contact dermatitis (53.8%), phytophotodermatitis (30.8%), allergic contact dermatitis (7.7%), perianal infectious dermatitis (3.8%), and atopic dermatitis (3.8%)".[^king] So phytophotodermatitis was the second most common skin condition behind an initial abuse investigation, which is unsurprising once you picture a handprint-shaped mark on a child who was handed a lime wedge.

The review is equally clear about the opposite error: "Three patients initially diagnosed with nontraumatic dermatitis were later found to be victims of physical (2/3; 66.7%) or sexual abuse (1/3; 33.3%)".[^king] Knowing this rash exists is a reason for a clinician to take a careful history, including "contact with furocoumarin-containing plants/fruit" and "parallel family/peer cutaneous presentations", and not a reason for anyone to talk themselves or a clinician out of a safeguarding concern.[^king] The authors note their own limits: potential underreporting, and "the inclusion of low-quality study designs and evidence".[^king]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Blistering, widespread, or facial involvement, and any rash on a child whose cause is unclear, needs a doctor rather than a skincare routine.

## The Evidence and What To Do About It

**Prevention is the only part with a reliable answer**, and it is not a product. Wash plant or fruit juice off skin promptly, before sun exposure, and cover up when handling the plants above; the review lists "adequate clothing (e.g., long-sleeved garments and gloves)" as the preventive measure.[^life] Sunscreen is sensible general practice (see [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]) but it is not established here as protection against a juice already on the skin.

**The acute rash** is a burn-like phototoxic injury. The 2024 review discusses "topical and systemic therapies" without specifying a graded regimen in the record cited here, so no treatment ranking is published on this page rather than inventing one; inflammation is commonly managed with [[topical-corticosteroids]] under medical direction.[^life]

**The aftermath is the part skincare actually addresses.** Hyperpigmentation is listed among the reaction's direct effects, and in practice it outlasts the rash by months.[^life] Treating it is the same problem as any post-inflammatory pigment, covered on [[hyperpigmentation]], where the graded options including [[azelaic-acid]], [[niacinamide]], [[tranexamic-acid]] and [[hydroquinone]] are set out. Sun protection on the healing marks matters there for the same reason it does in [[melasma]].
