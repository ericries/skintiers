Body acne, most often on the back and chest, is ordinary [[acne]] appearing on the trunk rather than a separate disease. StatPearls notes that acne occurs "on the affected individuals' face, neck, chest, upper back, and upper arm areas, where large, hormonally responsive sebaceous glands are abundant," so the trunk is affected for the same reason the face is.[^statpearls] The mechanism is identical: the same four processes, "Increased sebum production (seborrhea), Follicular hyperkeratinization, *C acnes*... Inflammation," drive lesions wherever they form, and the full evidence and grading live on [[acne]] and are not repeated here.[^statpearls]

## How to know you have this

These features help tell truncal acne apart from its common mimics; they are for recognition, not self-diagnosis, which a clinician confirms.

- **The same lesions as facial acne, on the trunk.** Comedones (blackheads and whiteheads), red papules and pustules, and sometimes deeper nodules on the back, chest, and shoulders, the sites StatPearls names for acne.[^statpearls]
- **Not itchy and uniform.** An itchy eruption of small, monomorphic bumps, especially after antibiotics or in hot, sweaty conditions, points instead to [[fungal-acne]], which does not respond to acne treatments.
- **Not rough, non-inflamed bumps on the upper arms or thighs.** Those are usually [[keratosis-pilaris]], a keratin plugging problem rather than acne.

## What is different about treating the body

The disease is the same; the practicalities are not. The back and chest are a large surface area, so wash-off benzoyl peroxide (a lathered short-contact wash) is a common, practical way to treat the whole region, accepting that rinsing reduces contact time versus a leave-on. Occlusion, sweat, and friction from clothing and gear aggravate truncal acne, and it is worth distinguishing from [[fungal-acne]], an itchy, uniform eruption caused by yeast that does not respond to acne treatments.

The topical actives are the same first-line options as for the face. StatPearls puts topical retinoids ("tretinoin, tazarotene, adapalene, and trifarotene") in initial management for most patients, with benzoyl peroxide or a topical antibiotic added for papulopustular acne, and topical salicylic acid "as an alternative to topical retinoids for individuals who are unable to tolerate or obtain them."[^statpearls] More severe, widespread, or scarring truncal acne is where oral therapy comes in: StatPearls lists oral antibiotics (primarily tetracyclines), hormonal therapy, and, for "severe recalcitrant nodular acne associated with scarring," oral isotretinoin, all clinician-managed.[^statpearls] The graded topical options that already have pages here are ranked above.

Because truncal acne is the same disease as facial acne, the underlying evidence and per-active grading are not duplicated here: they live on [[acne]] and on the linked ingredient pages ([[adapalene]], [[benzoyl-peroxide]], [[salicylic-acid]]).
