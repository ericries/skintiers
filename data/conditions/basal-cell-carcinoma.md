Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is a cancer that begins in the basal cells at the base of the epidermis, and with [[squamous-cell-carcinoma]] it is one of the two most common types of skin cancer, together called nonmelanoma skin cancer.[^nci] The American Academy of Dermatology describes it as a skin cancer that "tends to grow slowly and rarely spreads," adding that "it can grow deep, so you want to treat this skin cancer early."[^aad] This is a medical disease diagnosed and treated by physicians, most commonly with surgery, and it is entirely outside the scope of skincare products; skincare's only role is prevention through sun protection.[^nci][^aad]

> Basal cell carcinoma is a physician-managed skin cancer, not a cosmetic concern. It is the most common skin cancer, usually slow-growing and rarely spreading, but it can grow deep and destroy surrounding tissue if left untreated, so early evaluation matters. Skincare does not treat it; the one thing skincare contributes is prevention, daily broad-spectrum sun protection, covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and [[sun-damage-photoaging]]. Any new, changing, or non-healing spot should be seen by a dermatologist.

## How to know you have this

The AAD says basal cell carcinoma often shows up as "a slow-growing bump, sore-like growth, or rough-feeling area" on the skin.[^aad] On lighter skin tones a common sign is "a firm, raised, round growth that's shiny and pink or red," and a classic presentation is "a pearly, translucent bump with visible blood vessels."[^aad] Because it develops most often on sun-exposed skin, the National Cancer Institute notes nonmelanoma skin cancers "occur most often in areas of the skin exposed to the sun, such as the nose, ears, lower lip, or top of the hands."[^nci] Any new growth, a sore that does not heal, or a spot that is changing warrants evaluation by a board-certified dermatologist rather than self-diagnosis.

## The Evidence

Treatment of basal cell carcinoma is physician-directed and typically surgical: the AAD states that surgical excision "is the most common treatment for basal cell carcinoma," and that "many early basal cell carcinomas are cured with surgical excision," with Mohs micrographic surgery and other options used depending on the tumor.[^aad] None of this is skincare; there is no over-the-counter product that treats a skin cancer.

Where skincare matters is prevention. The National Cancer Institute lists the risk factors for basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma as "being exposed to natural sunlight or artificial sunlight (such as from tanning beds) over long periods of time," a fair complexion, "having a history of sunburns," a personal or family history of skin cancer or actinic keratosis, certain inherited gene changes, long-lasting skin inflammation, a weakened immune system, arsenic exposure, and past radiation treatment.[^nci] Daily broad-spectrum sun protection is the evidence-based way to lower that UV risk, set out on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and [[sun-damage-photoaging]]. The NCI also notes that "although having a fair complexion is a risk factor for skin cancer, people of all skin colors can get skin cancer."[^nci]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a diagnosis. Basal cell carcinoma is diagnosed and treated by physicians; a new, changing, bleeding, or non-healing spot should be evaluated by a dermatologist. It is distinct from [[melanoma]], a less common but more dangerous skin cancer.
