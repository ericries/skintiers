An ingrown hair is a hair that curls back or grows sideways into the skin instead of out of the follicle, producing a small inflamed bump. It happens most after shaving, waxing, or plucking, and anywhere body hair is removed. When ingrown hairs cluster in the shaved beard and neck they are the specific condition [[pseudofolliculitis-barbae]] (razor bumps), which is where the clinical evidence has been studied; this page covers the general phenomenon.

> An ingrown hair is a hair-removal problem before it is a skincare problem. The most reliable fix is to change or pause how you remove the hair, because the hair itself is the cause; topical exfoliating keratolytics help the skin shed around the follicle but do not cure a problem the razor or wax keeps recreating; and for a permanent reduction, laser hair removal targets the hair itself. Individual bumps are better left alone than dug at with tweezers or a needle.

## How to know you have this

An ingrown hair shows up as a small, sometimes itchy or tender raised bump, often with a visible hair looped under the surface or a pus-topped spot, in an area that was recently shaved, waxed, or plucked. Curly or coarse hair and close hair removal make it more likely, the same risk factors identified for razor bumps, which "can occur to anyone who shaves, and is more likely in those with curly hair."[^nussbaum] Clustered, chronic bumps in the beard and neck are [[pseudofolliculitis-barbae]] specifically; widespread pus-filled bumps that spread beyond hair-removal areas are more likely true [[folliculitis]] (a follicle infection), so persistent, spreading, or scarring bumps are worth a clinician's evaluation rather than self-treatment.

## The Evidence

The studied form of ingrown hairs is pseudofolliculitis barbae, and its treatment reviews are the best evidence base; the numbers and citations are set out on [[pseudofolliculitis-barbae]]. In that review, "effective treatment for PFB requires a multifaceted approach," with "topical therapies... currently the mainstay treatment" and "laser hair removal... a potential long-term treatment option."[^dalia] The same logic applies to ingrown hairs elsewhere on the body:

- **Change the hair removal.** Because the removed hair is the cause, the foundational step is shaving less closely, shaving less often, or pausing, and reconsidering waxing and plucking, which can drive the hair back into the skin. This is covered for the face on [[pseudofolliculitis-barbae]] and the [[mens-skincare-routine|shaving routine]].
- **Exfoliating keratolytics.** Topical [[retinoids]], [[glycolic-acid]], and [[salicylic-acid]] help the skin shed around the follicle opening so hairs are less likely to be trapped; reviews describe these keratolytics as the topical mainstay.[^dalia] An over-the-counter option built on this idea is [[tend-skin-the-skin-care-solution]].
- **Laser hair removal.** By reducing the hair itself, laser hair removal is the one approach that can lastingly reduce ingrown hairs, which the review calls "a potential long-term treatment option," though it notes "additional studies are warranted to understand its long-term efficacy and permanency."[^dalia]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a diagnosis. Painful, spreading, pus-filled, or scarring bumps, or ones that keep recurring, should be evaluated by a clinician, since they can be [[folliculitis]] or need prescription treatment.
