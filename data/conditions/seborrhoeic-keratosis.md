Seborrhoeic keratoses are benign growths of the outer skin layer, and they are so ordinary in older skin that one community survey of 2,038 Shanghai residents over 60 reported them as "the most common benign skin tumor with a prevalence of 100%" in that population, meaning every valid case examined had at least one.[^shanghai] They do not need treating. The interesting question for this site is what happens when someone decides to treat one anyway, and the answer is unusually well documented, because exactly one prescription drug has ever been approved in the United States for this purpose and its own trial numbers are on the record.

That drug was ESKATA, a 40% (w/w) hydrogen peroxide topical solution approved on 14 December 2017 under NDA 209305 to Aclaris Therapeutics.[^fda] Its label is blunt about the restriction: it "is indicated for the treatment of seborrheic keratoses that are raised", it is "to be administered by a healthcare provider", and a session means "Apply 4 times, approximately 1 minute apart, to the targeted lesion(s)".[^label] The FDA's own product record now lists its marketing status as Discontinued.[^fda] There is no at-home version of it, and a query of openFDA's drug listing directory for brand names containing "keratosis" returned no matching records at all, so no marketed product found there is named for this condition.[^ndc]

## How to know you have this

Honestly, by having a clinician look, and sometimes not even then. The hard case is seborrhoeic-keratosis-like melanoma, and a review of it in *Melanoma Research* states the problem directly: SK-like melanoma and seborrhoeic keratosis "are often clinically indistinguishable even for experienced dermatologists", with many lesions "misdiagnosed and not excised with histopathologic confirmation" precisely because they look benign.[^sklike] The same review describes the lesion that should worry you as a black, dark-brown or light, slightly elevated papule, nodule or plaque with rapid growth.[^sklike] Rapid growth in a lesion you had assumed was a harmless age spot is the signal to get it looked at, not to treat it yourself.

Two pieces of folklore are worth retiring. The first is the sign of Leser-Trélat, the belief that a sudden crop of new seborrhoeic keratoses marks a hidden internal cancer. The largest test of it followed 1,752 consecutive cases against the Swedish Cancer Registry and found a relative risk of 1.2 (95% CI 1.0 to 1.3), attributed mainly to cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma rather than internal malignancy; among 62 patients with a non-skin cancer diagnosed within a year of their keratosis diagnosis, 6 possibly showed the sign, against 5 of their 62 matched cancer-free controls. The authors concluded the study "gives no evidence to support the opinion that eruptive seborrheic keratoses are related to internal cancer risk."[^lesertrelat]

The second is that the small dark papules common on Black and Afro-Caribbean faces are a separate disease requiring separate products. A review of dermatosis papulosa nigra in the *Journal of Drugs in Dermatology* notes that its histology "is not significantly different from that of seborrheic keratosis" and that it "does not pose any health dangers," with removal sought for cosmetic reasons.[^dpn] Its warning about elective removal is the useful part: take care "so as to not merely exchange one defect for another such as a scar or discoloration."[^dpn]

## The Evidence

The ESKATA label reports two double-blind, vehicle-controlled trials in 937 subjects, each with four clinically typical raised keratoses on the face, trunk or extremities, treated at baseline and again at day 22 if needed, assessed at day 106. Clearance of all four target lesions was reached by 4% of treated subjects in Study 1 and 8% in Study 2, against 0% on vehicle in both. Clearance of at least three of four lesions reached 13% and 23%, against 0%.[^label]

Set those figures beside the same label's adverse-reaction table, drawn from the same 937 subjects. In the treated arm the proportion with any grade of each local reaction was 99% erythema, 97% stinging, 91% edema, 90% scaling, 81% crusting, 58% pruritus, 39% hyperpigmentation, 24% vesicles, 19% hypopigmentation, 15% erosion, 9% ulceration, 4% atrophy and 3% scarring.[^label] So the drug beat its vehicle, which had a 0% clearance rate and much lower reaction rates, and it did so by clearing every target lesion in fewer than one treated patient in ten while causing a visible local reaction in nearly all of them. The label also carries a warning that eye exposure can cause corneal injury and permanent eye injury "including blindness."[^label]

One limit on those pigmentary numbers deserves stating plainly: 98% of the 937 subjects were Caucasian and the mean age was 68.7 years.[^label] A 39% hyperpigmentation and 19% hypopigmentation rate measured in an almost entirely white trial population does not tell you what the same treatment does to darker skin, where dyspigmentation after a procedure is the outcome people most want to avoid. The trials do not answer that question, and nothing read for this page does.

There is no evidence tier list on this page because there is nothing on this site to rank. The one topical with trial data behind it for this use is a prescription in-office solution that is no longer marketed, and no graded ingredient page here has seborrhoeic-keratosis-specific evidence read for it. Grading [salicylic acid](/ingredients/salicylic-acid/) or [fluorouracil](/ingredients/fluorouracil-topical/) for this condition on the strength of their other uses would be inventing a ranking, so neither is graded here.
