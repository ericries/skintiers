A prescription acne gel whose own trials tested it against both of its ingredients separately, which is rarer than it sounds. **Bottom line:** the combination works, it beats each component alone, and the margin over them is far smaller than the margin over the vehicle. What clindamycin and benzoyl peroxide each do is on [[clindamycin-topical]] and [[benzoyl-peroxide]].

> **Two numbers worth holding together.** In the first trial, 55.0 percent of inflammatory lesions cleared on Acanya against 34.5 percent on the vehicle gel, a wide gap.[^label] Against benzoyl peroxide alone, the figure was 49.3 percent, a gap of under six points.[^label] Most of what the product does, its own components already do.

## Summary

Approved under NDA050819, marketed by Bausch Health US, LLC, with the current label effective 2020-09-30.[^openfda] Marketing category NDA, not an unapproved marketed drug, confirmed in the NDC directory.[^openfda] Indicated "for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris in patients 12 years or older".[^label]

**The strength is written two ways on the same label.** It is sold as a 1.2%/2.5% gel, and the label explains that each gram "contains 10 mg (1%) clindamycin as phosphate, and 25 mg (2.5%) benzoyl peroxide".[^label] The 1.2% refers to clindamycin phosphate, the 1% to clindamycin itself; both numbers describe the same tube.

**It expires faster than you would expect.** The pharmacist is instructed to store it refrigerated before dispensing, and to "Dispense ACANYA Gel with a 10-week expiration date".[^label] A ten-week clock is unusual for a topical and is worth knowing before a prescription is filled for a long course.

**No price.** None appears on the label and no retailer was fetched.

## The Rubric

**Treating moderate to severe acne vulgaris at 12 years or older (health)**
Graded notable on solid evidence. Two 12-week multi-centre randomized blinded trials, "identical in design", compared the gel against clindamycin alone, benzoyl peroxide alone and vehicle alone.[^label] The gel won on every co-primary measure in both. Solid is the grade because of that design, not because the label asserts it.

**Beating clindamycin alone or benzoyl peroxide alone (health)**
Graded modest on solid evidence. The margin exists and it replicates, which is what makes it credible, and it is small. The practical question for a reader is whether a combination prescription is worth it over a single agent, and the honest answer from these trials is yes but not by much.

## The Evidence

**The design is the good part.** Most acne products that reach this site were tested against vehicle alone, which answers only whether they beat doing nothing in a cream. These two trials included both monotherapy arms, so the combination had to beat its own ingredients to look good.[^label]

**Trial 1, 399 on Acanya against 408, 406 and 201.** Clear or almost clear on the Evaluator's Global Severity scale: 29 percent, against 21 percent for clindamycin, 19 percent for benzoyl peroxide and 14 percent for vehicle.[^label] Inflammatory lesion reduction: 55.0 percent against 47.1, 49.3 and 34.5.[^label] Non-inflammatory: 45.3 percent against 38.0, 40.2 and 28.6.[^label]

**Trial 2, 398 against 404, 403 and 194.** Clear or almost clear: 28 percent against 23, 23 and 11.[^label] Inflammatory: 54.2 percent against 45.3, 45.7 and 23.3.[^label] Non-inflammatory: 41.2 percent against 34.3, 34.5 and 19.2.[^label]

**What the label does not publish.** No p-values, no confidence intervals and no statistical test of any kind accompanies either table, so the tables show direction and magnitude but not whether each gap reached significance.[^label] The vehicle arms also differ sharply between the two trials, 34.5 against 23.3 percent inflammatory reduction, which is a reminder that a vehicle arm is not a fixed quantity.

**The safety point that matters most is not irritation.** The label's first warning is colitis: "Orally and parenterally administered clindamycin has been associated with severe colitis, which may result in death", and it records that "Diarrhea, bloody diarrhea, and colitis (including pseudomembranous colitis) have been reported with the use of topical and systemic clindamycin", instructing that the gel "should be discontinued if significant diarrhea occurs".[^label] Reported application-site reactions were rare by comparison, each at 0.1 percent.[^label]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven unless it also appears in The Evidence above.

- **That a combination is simply better than its parts.** Supported in direction, overstated in size. Both trials show single-digit margins over each monotherapy.[^label]
- **"Clear or Almost Clear" as a success rate.** At best 29 percent of patients reached it in twelve weeks, meaning roughly seven in ten did not.[^label] The phrase describes the minority outcome.
- **Antibiotic resistance is the unstated trade.** Benzoyl peroxide is in the formulation partly because it reduces resistance to the antibiotic beside it, a point belonging to [[clindamycin-topical]] rather than restated here.
