Winlevi is the brand name for clascoterone 1% cream, a prescription-only topical androgen receptor inhibitor made by Sun Pharmaceutical Industries and applied twice daily to treat acne vulgaris in patients 12 years of age and older.[^winlevi][^dailymed] The FDA first approved it in August 2020, the first approval anywhere for a topical androgen antagonist acne drug.[^dhillon]

> A twice-daily, 1% prescription cream that blocks androgen receptors directly in the skin rather than acting as a retinoid, antimicrobial, or antibiotic. Its two foundational phase 3 trials found a real but modest improvement over an inert vehicle, in the same range as other FDA-approved topical acne actives, with no trial yet placing it above or below the established retinoids. The full trial data, mechanism, and guideline placement are set out on [[clascoterone]].[^dailymed]

## Summary

Winlevi is Sun Pharma's brand of clascoterone cream 1%, the topical form of clascoterone (cortexolone-17alpha propionate), dispensed by prescription and applied twice daily as a thin layer to acne-affected skin.[^winlevi][^dailymed] The drug's mechanism, trial base, and limits are covered in depth on [[clascoterone]]; in short, it is a locally acting androgen receptor inhibitor with a modest, statistically significant effect over vehicle and a newer, thinner evidence base than the decades-old topical retinoids and antibiotics.

The product-specific evidence behind this exact 1% cream comes from two identical, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled phase 3 trials (1,440 patients), summarized under [The Evidence](#the-evidence) below.[^dailymed] The FDA label restricts the approved indication to patients 12 and older, even though the underlying trials enrolled patients as young as 9.[^hebert]

Winlevi is Rx-only in the United States; no over-the-counter purchase path exists.[^winlevi] See [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy) for purchase details.

## The Evidence

Clascoterone's mechanism, full trial record, and guideline placement are set out in depth on [[clascoterone]]; in brief, it has replicated, vehicle-controlled evidence for facial acne but no head-to-head trial against the established topical retinoids, and the 2024 AAD guideline gives it only a conditional recommendation, the same tier as azelaic acid.

The trials behind this label. Two identical, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled phase 3 trials (CB-03-01/25 and CB-03-01/26) enrolled 1,440 patients aged 9 to 58 with moderate or severe facial acne, applying clascoterone cream 1% or vehicle twice daily for 12 weeks.[^hebert] Restricted to the FDA-approved 12-and-older population, the label reports Investigator's Global Assessment (IGA) treatment success (clear or almost clear, with at least a 2-grade improvement) of 18.8% vs 8.7% (95% CI for the difference, 10.1% [4.1-16.0%]) in one trial and 20.9% vs 6.6% (95% CI, 14.3% [8.9-19.7%]) in the other, both favoring clascoterone.[^dailymed]

Systemic exposure. The label reports measurable systemic absorption and a small, reversible signal: in a pharmacokinetic substudy, transient HPA-axis suppression was seen in 1 of 20 adult subjects (5%) and 2 of 22 adolescent subjects (9%) after 14 days of twice-daily use, with all subjects back to normal HPA-axis function 4 weeks after stopping treatment.[^dailymed]

> **Bottom line.** This exact cream carries a real, replicated, modest effect over vehicle for facial acne in patients 12 and older. It has no comparative trial against [[tretinoin]], [[adapalene]], or [[benzoyl-peroxide]], and no trial specifically testing it for hormonal or PCOS-associated acne. Full trial numbers and guideline context are on [[clascoterone]].

## What's In It

The active and inactive ingredients as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed]

- **Active: [[clascoterone]] 1%** (10 mg per gram of cream), an androgen receptor inhibitor. See [[clascoterone]] for the graded mechanism and trial record.

Inactive ingredients: cetyl alcohol (emollient), citric acid monohydrate (pH), edetate disodium (chelator), mineral oil (emollient), mono- and di-glycerides (emulsifiers), polysorbate 80 (emulsifier), propylene glycol (solvent/humectant), purified water, and [[tocopherol|vitamin E]] (antioxidant), as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed]

The label directs a thin layer applied twice daily (morning and evening) to the affected area, for topical use only.[^dailymed] Supplied as a white to almost white cream in 60 g aluminum tubes.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it also appears in The Evidence above.

- **"A hormone-free way to treat hormonal acne."** Misleading framing. Clascoterone works by blocking androgen receptors, the same drug-class mechanism as oral anti-androgens; it is topical and locally acting rather than systemic, not "hormone-free."[^dailymed]
- **"No systemic effects."** Not fully supported. The FDA label reports measurable systemic absorption and a reversible HPA-axis suppression signal in a small subset of subjects in a pharmacokinetic substudy.[^dailymed]
- **Proven better than tretinoin or adapalene for acne.** Not supported. No cited trial compares clascoterone directly against an established topical retinoid, and the AAD guideline places it in a conditional rather than strong recommendation tier (full detail on [[clascoterone]]).
