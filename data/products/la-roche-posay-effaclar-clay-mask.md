La Roche-Posay's Effaclar Clay Mask (sold in some markets as the Effaclar Sebo-Controlling Mask) is a rinse-off clay mask for oily and blemish-prone skin, built on kaolin and a second smectite clay, and priced at $27.00 for 100 ml.[^escentual]

> The mask's job is oil absorption, and clays do genuinely soak up surface sebum and mattify shine, a transient cosmetic effect set out on [[bentonite]]. But the only human data on this specific mask is the brand's own uncontrolled, single-arm study, and no controlled trial shows a clay mask clears acne, so the "blemish" framing runs ahead of the evidence.[^escentual]

## Summary

La Roche-Posay positions the Effaclar Clay Mask as "the first dedicated clay mask" from the brand, "designed to gently remove excess sebum from the skin's surface without over-drying or causing inflammation" for oily and blemish-prone skin, and describes it as "formulated with Argilla and Kaolin clays, which remove sebum from deep within the skin."[^escentual] The active idea is straightforward and real: kaolin and smectite clays are absorbent, and a clay mask pulls surface oil off the skin and leaves it looking less shiny for a while. That mattifying effect is a transient cosmetic one, and the general evidence for clays as oil absorbers, along with its limits, is set out on [[bentonite]] rather than repeated here.

The one human study of this Effaclar mask was run by the manufacturer as an open-label, single-arm test with no placebo or vehicle-mask comparison, so its reported drops in measured skin oiliness cannot be separated from washing the face or from expectation; that study and its caveats are covered on [[bentonite]]. No controlled trial shows this mask, or clay masks generally, treats [[acne]] as opposed to temporarily reducing surface oil.

The formula is a simple clay base rather than an actives serum: after water, the two clays lead the list, with panthenol and glycerin as soothing humectants, titanium dioxide and mica-free white pigment for the opaque look, and a light emulsifier and preservative system ([full list below](#whats-in-it)). There is no salicylic acid, niacinamide, or other studied acne active in it, so it should be read as an oil-absorbing cosmetic mask, not a leave-on treatment.

It is listed at $27.00 for 100 ml at the retailer Escentual ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-09-18).[^escentual]

## The Evidence

No independent controlled trial tests this mask. Its clay actives, kaolin and a magnesium aluminium silicate smectite, absorb surface oil, a cosmetic effect whose evidence and limits are laid out on [[bentonite]]; the single human study of this exact product was manufacturer-run, uncontrolled, and single-arm, which cannot attribute the measured oil reduction to the clay rather than to washing or expectation (also covered on [[bentonite]]). For acne specifically, the closest controlled evidence for any clay mask is an add-on trial of a different, bentonite-based mask, [[salimi-2025-bentonite-alcea-facial-mask-acne-rct]], whose advantage did not survive statistical adjustment; it does not test this product. Treat the Effaclar Clay Mask as an oil-absorbing cosmetic, not an acne treatment.
