La Roche-Posay Pure Vitamin C12 Serum is a leave-on morning serum built around a high, 12% concentration of L-ascorbic acid (pure vitamin C), combined with salicylic acid and a soothing ingredient the brand calls neurosensine.[^lrp]

> A concentrated antioxidant serum. Vitamin C is a well-evidenced antioxidant, and this product carries a genuinely high 12% dose of its reference form, L-ascorbic acid, second on the declared ingredient list right after water. What the concentration does not come with is a published trial of the finished formula: the antioxidant and collagen case rests on the category evidence set out on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]], and the brand's own wrinkle and radiance percentages come from an unpublished internal test (see [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims)). Pure L-ascorbic acid also oxidizes on contact with air; the manufacturer's own FAQ acknowledges the serum can change color over time and does not describe the bottle as airless or light-blocking. The formula carries added fragrance.[^lrp]

## Summary

Pure Vitamin C12 Serum is a morning antioxidant serum from La Roche-Posay's dermatological skincare line. The active carrying the case for it is [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|L-ascorbic acid]], the pure and best-studied form of vitamin C, delivered here at 12%, within the concentration range linked to antioxidant and collagen effects on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]], and listed second on the declared ingredient list, immediately after water.[^lrp] Two other actives round out the formula: [[salicylic-acid|salicylic acid]], at an undisclosed low concentration that the manufacturer positions for pore appearance rather than acne treatment, and neurosensine, the brand's name for acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester, a synthetic dipeptide it describes as helping skin "feel soothed."[^lrp]

What sets this product apart from a plainer vitamin C serum is mainly its dose. What it lacks, compared with a formula like [[skinceuticals-c-e-ferulic|SkinCeuticals C E Ferulic]], is a published, product-specific efficacy trial: the manufacturer's own figures, "-41% reduction in wrinkles," "+70% improvement in radiance," and similar, come from an unpublished, uncontrolled 63-person, 8-week internal test rather than a peer-reviewed study, detailed in [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^lrp] Pure L-ascorbic acid is also the least stable form of vitamin C: it oxidizes and discolors with air exposure, and the manufacturer's own FAQ confirms the serum "can react on contact with the air, causing it to change color," while still calling it "effective"; the brand recommends only room-temperature storage and does not describe the bottle as airless or light-blocking.[^lrp] The formula carries added fragrance, since parfum/fragrance is the last ingredient on the declared list.[^lrp][^target] It is listed at $44.99 for 30 ml on the manufacturer's site and $37.99 for the same size at Target (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-06).[^lrp][^target]

## The Evidence

Topical L-ascorbic acid is a well-studied antioxidant with characterized roles in collagen synthesis and defense against UV-driven oxidative damage; the form's evidence across its uses is mapped on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]. What is specific to this product is its concentration and the marketing claims layered on top of the base active.

The declared ingredient list places ascorbic acid second, immediately after water, consistent with the manufacturer's stated 12% concentration, near the top of the range within which topical vitamin C's antioxidant and collagen effects have been studied.[^lrp] No published, product-specific trial of this formula was found. The manufacturer's own performance figures, "-41% reduction in wrinkles," "+70% improvement in radiance," "+83% improvement in smoothness," and "92% saw clinically improved fine lines," come from an unpublished internal test, described only as "average clinical scoring on 63 subjects after applying the product for 2 months, twice a day," with no stated control or vehicle arm and no peer review.[^lrp]

> Bottom line. The formula's headline number, 12% pure vitamin C, sits within the concentration range that has real, if modest, controlled evidence for antioxidant and collagen effects (see [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]). The brand's own wrinkle and radiance percentages come from a small, uncontrolled, unpublished internal test rather than an independent trial.[^lrp]

The salicylic acid and neurosensine additions are undosed on the label. Salicylic acid's general evidence, strongest for wart removal and more mixed and second-tier for acne, is mapped on [[salicylic-acid]]; the manufacturer positions it here for "pore" appearance in a sensitive-skin formula rather than as an acne treatment, and publishes neither a concentration nor a product-specific test of it.[^lrp]

## What's In It

Actives the manufacturer names as key ingredients:[^lrp]

- [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|Ascorbic acid]] (L-ascorbic acid), 12% by the manufacturer's own figure, listed second on the ingredient declaration, right after water
- [[salicylic-acid|Salicylic acid]], concentration not disclosed; positioned by the manufacturer as a pore-refining exfoliant rather than an acne treatment
- Acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester, branded "neurosensine," a synthetic soothing dipeptide
- [[tocopherol|Tocopherol]] (vitamin E), an antioxidant

Base, texture, and preservative system: water, cyclohexasiloxane, glycerin, alcohol denat., potassium hydroxide, polymethylsilsesquioxane, polysilicone-11, dimethicone, propylene glycol, pentaerythrityl tetraethylhexanoate, C13-14 isoparaffin, sodium hyaluronate ([[hyaluronic-acid]]), hydrolyzed hyaluronic acid, adenosine, PEG-20 methyl glucose sesquistearate, poloxamer 338, polyacrylamide, toluene sulfonic acid, ammonium polyacryloyldimethyl taurate, xanthan gum, caprylyl glycol, disodium EDTA, BHT, laureth-7, and parfum/fragrance.[^lrp]

The formula contains added fragrance: parfum/fragrance is the last ingredient on the declared list.[^lrp][^target] It carries no stated airless or light-blocking packaging feature; the manufacturer's FAQ instead tells buyers that color change from air exposure is normal and the serum "is still effective."[^lrp] A retailer listing of the same product records a similar ingredient declaration and independently confirms the salicylic acid, the neurosensine (as acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester), and the added fragrance.[^target]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing terms not established by the graded evidence above:

- **"-41% reduction in wrinkles," "+70% improvement in radiance," "+83% improvement in smoothness," "92% saw clinically improved fine lines."** These come from the manufacturer's own unpublished test, described as "average clinical scoring on 63 subjects after applying the product for 2 months, twice a day."[^lrp] No control arm, comparator, or peer review is described.
- **A dermatologist endorsement.** The product page quotes a dermatologist, Dr. Anna Karp, saying vitamin C "helps boost skin's radiance, reduce the appearance of wrinkles, and even skin tone," but the page itself labels her a "Sponsored Dermatologist," a paid endorsement rather than an independent review.[^lrp]
