DermaHarmony 2% Pyrithione Zinc Bar Soap, sold under the brand name "Zinc Therapy SOAP," is an FDA OTC-monograph drug, a true saponified bar (not a synthetic detergent bar) whose sole active ingredient, pyrithione zinc, is listed at 2%, the top of the concentration range the FDA recognizes for wash-off dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis products.[^cfr][^brand] The label's approved uses are limited to controlling and reducing the symptoms of dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis; the brand separately markets it, off-label and without a cited trial, for body acne and other Malassezia-linked conditions.[^dailymed][^brand]

> A genuine OTC drug soap, not a cosmetic bar with a dandruff claim bolted on: the FDA drug-facts panel lists pyrithione zinc 2% as the sole active, at the ceiling of the monograph range, in a real saponified-oil base.[^dailymed] Pyrithione zinc's antidandruff and antiseborrheic effect is real but graded moderate against ketoconazole and selenium sulfide on this site, on thinner recent head-to-head evidence, [detailed on the ingredient page](#the-evidence).[^dailymed] The brand's fungal-acne and body-acne framing is a plausible mechanism extrapolation, not a tested indication; [see Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^brand]

## Summary

DermaHarmony 2% Pyrithione Zinc Bar Soap is a 4 oz (113 g) bar registered with the FDA as an over-the-counter drug (NDC 71819-001-01), carrying a full Drug Facts panel.[^dailymed] The active ingredient is pyrithione zinc 2%, and the labeled purpose is "Dandruff, Seborrheic dermatitis," with uses stated as "Controls and reduces the symptoms of dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis."[^dailymed] Directions call for using it in place of regular soap on affected areas, working up a lather and massaging it in, at least twice a week or as directed by a doctor.[^dailymed]

The soap base itself is a genuine saponified-oil bar (sodium palmate, sodium cocoate, sodium palm kernelate from palm and coconut oils), not a synthetic surfactant bar, with glycerin, titanium dioxide, oat kernel flour, olive oil, and vitamin E rounding out the inactive ingredients.[^dailymed] That base is a health-neutral vehicle; the health claim rests entirely on the pyrithione zinc active, whose antifungal action against the Malassezia yeast driving dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis is graded in full on [[zinc-pyrithione]].[^dailymed]

The brand also tags and markets this bar for body acne, jock itch, tinea versicolor, and keratosis pilaris, conditions not named on the FDA label.[^brand] Those are cosmetic/unproven extrapolations from pyrithione zinc's antifungal mechanism against Malassezia, the same yeast implicated in [[fungal-acne|fungal acne (Malassezia folliculitis)]], but no trial of pyrithione zinc specifically in fungal acne was found; the evidence base for that condition centers on azole antifungals and selenium sulfide instead, [set out under Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^brand]

## The Evidence

The FDA OTC monograph recognizes pyrithione zinc as an anti-dandruff active at 0.3 to 2 percent "when formulated to be applied and then washed off after brief exposure," which is exactly this bar's format and, at 2%, the top of that recognized range.[^cfr] The full cross-product evidence base for pyrithione zinc, including how it is graded relative to ketoconazole and selenium sulfide, lives on [[zinc-pyrithione]]; this product page does not duplicate that grading. No published trial of this specific bar was found; the health claim here rests on category-level monograph recognition of the active at a matching, and maximal, disclosed concentration, the same relationship COSRX's 0.1% retinol cream has to the category-level retinol evidence.

> **Bottom line.** This bar's dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis claim rests on FDA monograph recognition of pyrithione zinc at 2%, its full disclosed concentration, not on a trial of the bar itself; the ingredient-level evidence and its ranking against other antifungals are on [[zinc-pyrithione]].

## What's In It

The label states the product is for external use only, to avoid contact with eyes, and to ask a doctor before use if the condition covers a large area of the body.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Standard of care in the US for seborrheic dermatitis maintenance."**[^brand] A brand characterization. Pyrithione zinc is a real, FDA-monograph-recognized antifungal for this use, but the ingredient page on this site grades it a notch below ketoconazole and selenium sulfide, whose recent comparative trial evidence is deeper; see [[zinc-pyrithione]] for that ranking.
- **Body acne, jock itch, tinea versicolor listed as product tags and discussed in the brand's own product copy as targets of pyrithione zinc's antifungal action against Malassezia.**[^brand] These are off-label uses not named on the FDA drug-facts panel.[^dailymed] The mechanism, antifungal activity against Malassezia, is the same one implicated in [[fungal-acne]], but no trial of pyrithione zinc in fungal acne specifically was found; treat this as a plausible-but-untested extrapolation rather than an established use.
- **"Using the lowest strength medication that effectively treats a condition is generally considered the best practice in medicine," recommending patients step down to the brand's 1% version for long-term maintenance.**[^brand] A general medical principle stated by the brand in its own marketing copy for a different (lower-concentration) product in its line; not a claim this site independently verified against a trial comparing 1% and 2% pyrithione zinc maintenance outcomes.
