Neutrogena T/Gel Extra Strength Therapeutic Shampoo was the stronger member of the T/Gel line, built on "Neutar" solubilized coal tar extract at a strength equivalent to coal tar 1%, twice the concentration of the standard [[neutrogena-tgel-therapeutic-shampoo-coal-tar|T/Gel Therapeutic]] (coal tar 0.5%), for dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and psoriasis of the scalp.[^brand][^dailymed] Neutrogena has discontinued it in the US: the product's own page on neutrogena.com now carries the title "DISCONTINUED T/Gel Therapeutic Shampoo, Extra Strength" and its meta description states "T/Gel Therapeutic Shampoo-Extra Strength has been discontinued."[^brand]

> A doubled-strength coal tar shampoo, coal tar 1% against the standard formula's 0.5%, sharing the same FDA-monograph indications and the same modest, dated evidence base covered on [[coal-tar]]. It is no longer sold: Neutrogena's own site marks it discontinued.[^brand] The same active strength lives on in a comparable coal tar 1% "extra strength" shampoo still sold and FDA-labeled under a store brand.[^dailymed]

## Summary

This was the higher-strength therapeutic option in Neutrogena's coal tar shampoo range, positioned above the standard T/Gel Therapeutic. Its active was Neutrogena's "Neutar" solubilized coal tar extract at a strength equivalent to coal tar 1%, double the 0.5% coal tar strength of the standard formula. That strength is corroborated by an FDA-labeled store-brand shampoo that names Neutrogena's Extra Strength formula as its comparator active and lists "Solubilized Coal Tar Extract 4.0% (Coal Tar, 1.0%)."[^dailymed] Coal tar's evidence lives on [[coal-tar]] and in the ranking on [[anti-dandruff-seborrheic-dermatitis-treatments-by-evidence]]: it is a recognized FDA over-the-counter active for these scalp conditions, but the evidence base is modest and largely dated relative to newer antifungal shampoos, and it stains and increases photosensitivity.

The product is discontinued in the US. Neutrogena's own current product page for it displays "DISCONTINUED T/Gel Therapeutic Shampoo, Extra Strength" as its title and states the product "has been discontinued," directing shoppers to other Neutrogena hair products instead.[^brand] The identical active strength remains available from other manufacturers: a Walmart-brand coal tar shampoo on DailyMed lists "Solubilized Coal Tar Extract 4.0% (Coal Tar, 1.0%)" as its active and states on its carton it should be compared "to Neutrogena T/Gel Therapeutic Shampoo Extra Strength Formula active ingredient," confirming the 1% coal tar strength this page describes.[^dailymed]

## The Evidence

No independent clinical trial of this specific Neutrogena shampoo formula was located. The defensible facts are its active, coal tar equivalent to 1% (twice the 0.5% strength of the standard T/Gel Therapeutic), and the FDA-monograph standing of coal tar for dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and scalp psoriasis, set out in full on [[coal-tar]].[^dailymed] Coal tar's modern high-quality trial base is thin relative to azole antifungals and selenium sulfide, which is why it sits below them in the ranking on [[anti-dandruff-seborrheic-dermatitis-treatments-by-evidence]] and on [[seborrheic-dermatitis]] and [[psoriasis]]. A higher percentage of the same active does not by itself establish a proportionally larger clinical effect; no product-specific dose-response trial comparing this Extra Strength formula against the standard-strength version was located.

The practical trade-offs are coal tar's, not this formula's: it can stain skin, hair, and fabric, has a tar odor, and the comparable store-brand label warns to "use caution in exposing skin to sunlight after applying this product" because "it may increase your tendency to sunburn for up to 24 hours after application."[^dailymed] The cancer worry that surrounds anything coal-derived has been tested directly and is addressed on [[coal-tar]].

> **Bottom line.** A doubled-strength coal tar 1% medicated shampoo, sharing coal tar's genuine but modest and dated FDA-monograph evidence for dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and scalp psoriasis, and now discontinued by the maker.[^brand][^dailymed] For anyone seeking this strength of coal tar, comparable 1% "extra strength" store-brand shampoos remain sold and FDA-labeled; for most people the better-evidenced first-line choices are the antifungals ranked on [[anti-dandruff-seborrheic-dermatitis-treatments-by-evidence]].

## What's In It

The active was Neutar solubilized coal tar extract, equivalent to coal tar 1%; expressed on the FDA label of the equivalent store-brand formula as "Solubilized Coal Tar Extract 4.0% (Coal Tar, 1.0%)."[^dailymed] Neutrogena's own product-specific inactive ingredient list for this discontinued formula was not available on its current site. A store-brand shampoo compared to this product's active ingredient and matching its 1% coal tar strength lists on its FDA label: water, sodium laureth sulfate, cocamidopropyl betaine, cocamide MEA, laureth-4, polysorbate 20, bis-hydroxyethyl dihydroxypropyl stearammonium chloride, PEG-120 methyl glucose dioleate, disodium EDTA, citric acid, phenoxyethanol, fragrance, methylchloroisothiazolinone, methylisothiazolinone, red 40, blue 1, yellow 5, and yellow 6; this is the comparator's own formula, not a confirmed match to Neutrogena's exact inactive list, and is included here only to show the general base such a coal tar shampoo uses.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Controls dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and psoriasis of the scalp."** This is the FDA-monograph use for coal tar and is accurate for a coal tar 1% product; the equivalent store-brand label states the active "controls the symptom of: dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, psoriasis."[^dailymed] The effect is genuine but modest, and the coal tar evidence base is dated relative to newer antifungals, as set out on [[coal-tar]].
- **"Extra strength."** Literally true in concentration: coal tar 1% is double the standard T/Gel Therapeutic's 0.5%.[^dailymed] But a higher percentage of the same active does not by itself prove a proportionally larger clinical effect, and no product-specific dose-response trial comparing the two strengths was located.
