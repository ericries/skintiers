A four-filter organic sunscreen from [[krave-beauty|KraveBeauty]], sold on a beetroot extract that does none of the filtering. **Bottom line:** the UV protection is real and conventional, and the genuinely unusual thing about this product is how much the brand publishes about its own testing. Listed at $20 for 50 mL and showing as unavailable on the brand's own store when checked. The filter evidence is at [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

> **Most sunscreen brands say "clinically proven" and stop. This one prints the receipts.** KraveBeauty states that "For Beet The Sun we carried out a total of 6 tests: 2 FDA Broad Spectrum Tests, 2 FDA SPF Static Tests, 1 ISO 24444 (2019 version) and 1 ISO 24442", adds that "It's common to only perform these tests once, but we knew it was important for you to be extra clear", and reports that "we found the exact rating to be 42 & 43 which fell under our projected 40 SPF rating".[^brand] Those ISO numbers are the real standards for in-vivo SPF and in-vivo UVA, and labelling 40 on a measured 42 to 43 errs in the consumer's favour.

## Summary

Four organic UV filters, no mineral. FDA's drug registry records the actives as **avobenzone 30 mg/mL, homosalate 100 mg/mL, octisalate 50 mg/mL and octocrylene 30 mg/mL**, under two entries, one listing Krave Beauty, LLC and one listing the manufacturer Englewood Lab, Inc., both as OTC monograph drug products.[^ndc] The brand's own page gives the same set as percentages: "Homosalate 10%", "Octisalate 5%", "Avobenzone 3%" and "Octocrylene 3%".[^brand] Two independent sources, one formula, no discrepancy.

KraveBeauty lists it at **$20.00 for 50 mL**, with a two-pack at $34.00. Every variant showed `available: false` on the brand's own product feed read on 2026-11-13, so it is listed but not purchasable there at that moment; this page does not infer discontinuation from that.[^brand]

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: notable, for the only thing a sunscreen has to do.** Broad-spectrum SPF 40 from a standard, well-understood filter set. [[avobenzone]] is the UVA filter, with [[octocrylene]] stabilising it and [[homosalate]] and [[octisalate]] covering UVB. The evidence for using sunscreen at all is the strongest on this site and lives at [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]; nothing here is specific to this bottle.
- **Evidence quality: solid for the category, and unusually transparent for the product.** The brand reports six tests where one of each would have satisfied the requirement, names ISO 24444 and ISO 24442 correctly, and publishes the measured numbers rather than only the rounded label.[^brand] It also explains that the UVA testing is what produces the "+++" in the PA value.[^brand]
- **Record finding: the UVA claim is quantified, and it is the brand's own figure.** "Through these tests, we found that Beet The Sun can protect against 88 to 94% of UVA rays".[^brand] That is a specific number from the company's own commissioned testing, reported here as theirs and not independently verified. It is still more than almost any competitor discloses.
- **Record finding: the hero ingredient is not doing the work.** "Beta Vulgaris (Beet) Root Extract" sits eighth in the inactive list, behind water, propanediol, pentylene glycol, a thickener, bisabolol, a photostabiliser and a preservative.[^brand] The brand's claim for it is antioxidant support "against oxidative stress", not UV filtering, and the page gives no evidence for that claim.[^brand]
- **The PA rating is a Japanese system, not a US one.** The label reads "BROAD SPECTRUM SPF 40 PA+++".[^brand] Broad Spectrum is the US monograph term; PA+++ comes from the ISO 24442 test the brand describes. Both appear on the same tube, which is legitimate and worth understanding rather than treating as two separate guarantees.

## The Evidence

None specific to this product beyond its own regulatory testing, which is what every legally sold US sunscreen must do. The difference is that this brand published the count, the standards and the measured values instead of the phrase *clinically proven* alone.

Whether to prefer organic filters or mineral ones is argued at [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and is not re-run here; [[cotz-sensitive-spf-40]] is the mineral-only counterpart this site covers, from a brand whose entire range avoids organic filters.

> **Bottom line.** A conventional, competent chemical sunscreen whose marketing hero is irrelevant to its function and whose testing disclosure is better than the category norm. Buy it for the SPF, not the beetroot, and check stock before planning on it.

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted from the brand's own product page and **not** endorsed here.

- "Supercharged with Beetroot Extract — a powerful antioxidant that gives your skin additional protection against oxidative stress".[^brand] Antioxidant support is a plausible but unevidenced addition here; the page read gives no study for it, and the measurable protection comes from the four filters.
- "Clinically Proven: Provides SPF 40 Broad Spectrum protection. Based on clinical tests conducted at two independent facilities."[^brand] True and also mandatory. Every SPF number on a US shelf rests on this test; what distinguishes this brand is publishing how many times it ran it.
- "made to look and feel like your skin" and "without a white cast".[^brand] Cosmetic claims about finish, not protection, and organic filters do not leave the white cast that high-zinc formulas can.
