The Ordinary Ascorbyl Glucoside Solution 12% is a water-based leave-on serum built around ascorbyl glucoside, a shelf-stable vitamin C derivative, at a manufacturer-declared 12%, sold in a single 30 ml bottle and marketed for antioxidant support and skin-tone evenness.[^1]

> A cheap, derivative-only vitamin C serum. Ascorbyl glucoside has no antioxidant activity of its own: skin enzymes must first cleave off its glucose molecule and release free ascorbic acid before it can act, and researchers describe it as an "ascorbic acid pro-drug."[^2] That conversion is confirmed only in ex vivo skin explants, not living human trials of the plain ingredient; the fuller case, including how thin the human pigmentation and antiaging evidence is, and how it depends on concentrated complexes rather than the 12% solution here, is set out on [[ascorbyl-glucoside]].[^2] It is listed at $14.80 for 30 ml, cheaper than most single-form, higher-dose L-ascorbic acid serums such as [[la-roche-posay-pure-vitamin-c-12-serum|La Roche-Posay Pure Vitamin C12]] ($44.99/30 ml) or [[skinceuticals-c-e-ferulic|SkinCeuticals C E Ferulic]] ($185/30 ml).[^1]

## Summary

Ascorbyl Glucoside Solution 12% is The Ordinary's plain vitamin C derivative serum: water, then [[ascorbyl-glucoside]] at a manufacturer-declared 12%, then a short list of solvents, pH adjusters, a chelator, and preservative boosters, with no other named actives.[^1] The general case for [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|L-ascorbic acid]], the reference form of vitamin C with the strongest antioxidant and collagen evidence, does not transfer directly here: ascorbyl glucoside is inert until skin converts it, and that conversion has been confirmed only in ex vivo human skin explants and an in vitro model, not a living-human trial of the plain molecule.[^2]

The human trial evidence that does exist for ascorbyl glucoside used it differently from how this product presents it: a 24-week trial that improved solar lentigo pigmentation used a 28% ascorbyl glucoside-arginine complex, far more concentrated than this 12% solution and a different formulation entirely; a trial that reached significance for wrinkles and brown spots combined ascorbyl glucoside with an unrelated NF-kB inhibitor, and the authors credited that other ingredient, not ascorbyl glucoside; and in one in vitro comparison it was the weakest tyrosinase inhibitor of six depigmenting agents tested. The full picture, with study sizes and limits, is on [[ascorbyl-glucoside]].

The manufacturer markets this serum as "less irritation potential" than direct vitamin C and as supporting "the visual effects of damage caused by environmental stressors"; those claims are weighed against the evidence under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^1] It is listed at $14.80 for the single 30 ml size on The Ordinary's own site, and an independent retailer, Cult Beauty, lists the same 30 ml size at EUR 17.00 with an identical declared ingredient list (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-16).[^1][^3]

## The Evidence

Ascorbyl glucoside is a glucose-conjugated derivative of vitamin C with no antioxidant activity of its own; skin must enzymatically cleave it to release free ascorbic acid first, and it is described in the literature as an "ascorbic acid pro-drug."[^2] No published trial tests The Ordinary's 12% ascorbyl glucoside solution; its grades above rest on the general ingredient case for ascorbyl glucoside, none of it generated on this exact formula.[^1]

The strongest mechanistic data are ex vivo: in human skin explants and a reconstructed epidermis model, ascorbyl glucoside "was completely metabolized to AA by the skin before entering the receptor compartment," and at a lower tested concentration than a comparator ascorbic acid formulation, the antioxidant protection was similar.[^2] That confirms the conversion mechanism but stops at explanted skin, not a living human trial. The clearest human pigmentation data come from a 24-week, half-face trial that used a 28% ascorbyl glucoside-arginine complex, more than double this product's 12% and a different formulation (an amino-acid complex, not the plain ingredient), and reduced solar lentigo pigmentation versus placebo.[^2] A separate trial that reached significance for wrinkles and brown spots combined ascorbyl glucoside with the NF-kB inhibitor 4-hexyl-1,3-phenylenediol; the authors attributed the effect to NF-kB inhibition, not to ascorbyl glucoside, and because both actives were applied together the trial cannot isolate ascorbyl glucoside's own contribution.[^2] In a direct in vitro comparison against five other depigmenting agents, ascorbyl glucoside was the weakest tyrosinase inhibitor tested, clearly behind bakuchiol and 4-butylresorcinol.[^2]

> **Bottom line.** No trial isolates this product or its 12% concentration. The mechanism (conversion to ascorbic acid) is confirmed only ex vivo; the human trials that exist for ascorbyl glucoside used a far more concentrated complex or combined it with an unrelated active credited for the effect, and in a direct comparison it was the weakest of six tyrosinase inhibitors tested.[^2]

The full ingredient list (INCI) as declared by the manufacturer, confirmed against an independent retailer listing.[^1][^3] The active with a functional role is linked to its own page; the rest is the solvent, pH, and preservative system.

Actives:

Base, texture, and preservative system: water, propanediol (solvent), aminomethyl propanol and triethanolamine (pH adjusters), isoceteth-20 (emulsifier/solubilizer), xanthan gum (thickener), dimethyl isosorbide (solvent), ethoxydiglycol (solvent), trisodium ethylenediamine disuccinate (chelator), and 1,2-hexanediol and caprylyl glycol (preservative boosters).[^1][^3]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by the product-specific evidence above:

- **"Unlike direct forms of vitamin C, this easy-to-use serum contains a water-soluble vitamin C derivative that converts into vitamin C once applied, giving it less irritation potential."** The conversion itself is supported by ex vivo skin research, but that same research measured the antioxidant payoff only in explanted skin, not a living-human irritation or efficacy comparison against direct L-ascorbic acid.[^1][^2]
- **"Helps support the appearance of skin tone evenness and skin smoothness"** and **"protects skin from the visual effects of damage caused by environmental stressors, helping to maintain a more radiant complexion."** Positioning claims. The human pigmentation evidence for ascorbyl glucoside used a 28% arginine complex, not this product's plain 12% solution, and no cited trial measures smoothness or radiance for the ingredient at any concentration (on [[ascorbyl-glucoside]]).[^1]
- **The 12% concentration itself, as a selling point.** No published trial tests ascorbyl glucoside at 12% alone; the positive human pigmentation result used a 28% complex, and no data compares outcomes across concentrations of the plain ingredient.[^2]
