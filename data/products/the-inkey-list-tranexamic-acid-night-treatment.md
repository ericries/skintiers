The INKEY List Tranexamic Acid Night Treatment is a budget dark-spot serum built around a manufacturer-declared 2% [[tranexamic-acid]] paired with 2% ascorbyl glucoside (a vitamin C derivative) and an acai berry extract, sold in a single 30 mL bottle.[^1] The brand's own site now lists this same formula under the name "Tranexamic Acid Serum," describing it as the successor to the "Tranexamic Acid Night Treatment" this page covers; the ingredient list, price, and size match across both names.[^1]

> Tranexamic acid is a synthetic antifibrinolytic drug with real but modest melasma evidence, strongest at an oral dose; its topical evidence is thinner and the closest topical study combined it with kojic acid and niacinamide rather than testing it alone (see [[tranexamic-acid]]). This serum's labeled 2% sits below that study's 3%, and no trial has tested this exact two-active Inkey formula.[^1][^2]

## Summary

This INKEY List serum pairs a manufacturer-declared 2% [[tranexamic-acid]] with 2% ascorbyl glucoside, a vitamin C derivative, and an acai berry (Euterpe oleracea) extract, marketed for "hyperpigmentation & fade dark spots."[^1] Tranexamic acid is a synthetic lysine derivative used off-label in dermatology for melasma; its case, strongest by the oral route and thinner and more mixed topically, is set out in full on [[tranexamic-acid]].

No published trial isolates this product or its specific active combination. The closest topical study tested a 3% tranexamic acid, kojic acid, and niacinamide serum, a different formula at a higher tranexamic acid concentration than the 2% labeled here.[^2] Ascorbyl glucoside, the second named active, has no vehicle-controlled human trial of the plain molecule at typical serum strength; skin must first convert it to ascorbic acid before it can act, a step confirmed in ex vivo tissue but not tied to a living-skin efficacy trial for this ingredient alone ([see The Evidence](#the-evidence)).

The brand's site now sells this same INCI list, price, and size under the name "Tranexamic Acid Serum," describing it as the current name for what was sold as "Tranexamic Acid Night Treatment."[^1] It lists for $19 for 30 mL on The INKEY List's own site (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-16).[^1]

## The Evidence

Whether tranexamic acid works, and for what, is covered on [[tranexamic-acid]]. Topical tranexamic acid carries a strong recommendation from one systematic review of melasma treatments, but its quantitative evidence is thinner than the oral route's, and the leading topical clinical study tested a three-active 3% tranexamic acid, kojic acid, and niacinamide serum, not tranexamic acid alone.[^2] That study is the closest published analog to this product; it used a higher tranexamic acid concentration (3% vs. this product's labeled 2%) and two additional actives this Inkey formula does not carry (kojic acid and niacinamide), so it cannot isolate what 2% tranexamic acid alone, or paired with ascorbyl glucoside, would do.[^1][^2]

Ascorbyl glucoside, the serum's other named active at a labeled 2%,[^1] is a shelf-stable vitamin C derivative that skin must enzymatically convert to ascorbic acid before it has any antioxidant activity. Human trials of the plain molecule alone, at typical serum strength, have not been published; existing human data test it combined with other actives or as a concentrated complex (see [[ascorbyl-glucoside]]).

No trial has tested this Inkey formula, at these concentrations, in combination.

> **Bottom line.** No trial isolates this product. Its tranexamic acid grade rests on category evidence that is itself thinner topically than orally, drawn from a different, higher-concentration three-active formula; its second named active, ascorbyl glucoside, has no standalone human efficacy trial at serum strength.[^1][^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by the product-specific evidence above:

- **"Powerful dark spot correcting serum that transforms uneven skin tone, reduces hyperpigmentation, fades discoloration, and reveals your brightest, most radiant complexion yet."** No trial of this product exists; the underlying case for topical tranexamic acid at any concentration is thinner and more mixed than the oral route's, and the closest topical study used a different, three-active, higher-concentration formula.[^1][^2]
- **"2% Acai Berry Extract: Promotes even skin tone."** An antioxidant-marketed botanical; not an outcome measured for this formula.[^1]
- **"2% Vitamin C derivative: Brightens skin tone."** Ascorbyl glucoside must first be converted by skin to ascorbic acid before it has any antioxidant activity, and no vehicle-controlled human trial of the plain molecule alone at serum strength has been published (see [[ascorbyl-glucoside]]).[^1]
