Retin-A is the original branded formulation of tretinoin cream, a prescription-only topical retinoid manufactured by Bausch Health under its Ortho Dermatologics label; the 0.1% cream is the strongest of the three concentrations sold under this name.[^dailymed] Its FDA-approved indication is narrow, topical treatment of acne vulgaris, and the label states plainly that "the safety and efficacy of the long-term use of this product in the treatment of other disorders have not been established."[^dailymed] Tretinoin's separate, well-evidenced use against photoaging is real but off-label for this product and is graded in full on [[tretinoin]].

> A first-line acne topical whose FDA label covers acne only, not the anti-aging use it is often reached for. As monotherapy for acne its effect is modest, roughly in the middle of the pack of acne treatments and improved by adding [[benzoyl-peroxide]] or matched in efficacy by better-tolerated [[adapalene]].[^mavranezouli][^cunliffe] For photoaging, a double-blind RCT tested this exact 0.1% cream strength directly and found it significantly improved photoaging versus an inert vehicle, though no better than the lower 0.025% strength and with significantly more irritation.[^griffiths] Retin-A itself is the brand; generic tretinoin cream is widely available and, per GoodRx, typically costs less than the brand list price.[^goodrx]

## Summary

Retin-A (tretinoin) Cream, 0.1% contains tretinoin, "all-trans-retinoic acid," at 0.1% by weight in "a hydrophilic cream vehicle of stearic acid, isopropyl myristate, polyoxyl 40 stearate, stearyl alcohol, xanthan gum, sorbic acid, butylated hydroxytoluene, and purified water."[^dailymed] The label's only approved use is health-focused and narrow: "RETIN-A is indicated for topical application in the treatment of acne vulgaris."[^dailymed] Tretinoin is also the best-evidenced topical ingredient for photoaging, a cosmetic use this cream is not labeled for; that broader evidence base, and the honest limits the manufacturer's own labeling places on it (a different-strength Renova label states tretinoin does not reverse photoaging or restore younger skin), live on [[tretinoin]] and [[retinoids]], not re-derived here.

## The Evidence

**Acne (the label indication).** This product's approval rests on tretinoin's decades-old acne evidence base, summarized and graded on [[tretinoin]]: a network meta-analysis of 179 acne RCTs found topical retinoid monotherapy produced a mean 18.27% improvement over placebo, ranking 22nd among acne treatment classes, while adding benzoyl peroxide raised that to 26.16%.[^mavranezouli] A separate 409-patient head-to-head RCT found adapalene gel 0.1% had "equivalent efficacy in reduction of acne lesion counts and global improvement" against tretinoin cream 0.05%, but was "significantly better tolerated than tretinoin cream 0.05% in terms of erythema, dryness, desquamation and stinging/burning."[^cunliffe] Neither trial is specific to the 0.1% strength or the Retin-A brand; they establish the acne case for tretinoin as a class.

**Photoaging (off-label for this product).** One trial is directly relevant to this exact formulation strength: a double-blind, vehicle-controlled 48-week RCT randomized 99 photoaged patients to 0.1% tretinoin cream (n=32), 0.025% tretinoin cream (n=35), or vehicle (n=32).[^griffiths] "Both 0.1% and 0.025% tretinoin produced statistically significant overall improvement in photoaging of the face compared with vehicle; there were no clinically or statistically significant differences in efficacy between the two concentrations of tretinoin."[^griffiths] Epidermal thickening was similar between strengths (30% vs 28% versus an 11% decrease with vehicle), but "irritant side effects (erythema and scaling) were statistically significantly greater with 0.1% tretinoin than with 0.025% tretinoin."[^griffiths] In plain terms, this specific 0.1% strength earns no extra photoaging benefit over the milder 0.025% cream in the one trial that compared them head to head, only more irritation. The wider photoaging evidence base (a foundational 1988 JAMA trial and a 2025 meta-analysis of 8 RCTs) is set out on [[tretinoin]] and is not specific to this strength.

## What's In It

- **Active: tretinoin 0.1%**, "all-trans-retinoic acid," as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed] See [[tretinoin]] for the graded mechanism and full evidence base.
- **Vehicle:** "stearic acid, isopropyl myristate, polyoxyl 40 stearate, stearyl alcohol, xanthan gum, sorbic acid, butylated hydroxytoluene, and purified water," as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed]

The label states use should be discontinued "if hypersensitivity to any of the ingredients is noted," and warns the cream may cause severe local erythema and peeling, heightened sun sensitivity, and should be kept away from the eyes, mouth, and mucous membranes.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Anti-aging" or "wrinkle" use.** Not on this product's own label. Retin-A's FDA approval covers acne vulgaris only; the label explicitly states long-term use for other disorders is unestablished.[^dailymed] The photoaging evidence for tretinoin as an ingredient is real and covered on [[tretinoin]], but it is an off-label use of this specific drug, not a claim the manufacturer makes on this label.
- **"Higher strength works better."** Not supported for photoaging by the one trial that tested this exact 0.1% strength head-to-head against 0.025%: efficacy was statistically indistinguishable between the two, while irritation was significantly greater at 0.1%.[^griffiths]
