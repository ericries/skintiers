The Lux Glo is reVive's $79 cordless face device, the mid-priced member of the Lux Collection that the brand currently leads with. The brand calls it "a lightweight, cordless red and blue light therapy device you can take anywhere" with three 3-minute settings: Anti-Aging, Acne, and Acne plus Anti-Aging.[^brand] The category evidence is on [[home-led-light-therapy]] and the maker on [[revive-light-therapy]].

> **The modality is the best-graded one for home acne devices. The percentages next to it are not evidence.** Blue plus red is Level B with a Grade I recommendation in the 2026 GRADE review, and this device emits both.[^home] But the two numbers the brand puts on the page, 97.4% and 100%, come from a study described in a single sentence: "Consumer study of participants ages" 39 to 66.[^brand] No sample size, no control group, no measurement beyond what participants said they saw. And the 100% figure, which covers pores and texture, was measured in people aged 39 to 66, which is not the population that the acne claim is about.

## Summary

A glide-over-the-face LED device emitting "Red, infrared, amber, and blue light" with a stated "Primary wavelength range" of 415 to 880 nm.[^brand] Blue at the bottom of that range plus red is the combination with the best grade for home acne devices, so the physics here is reasonable.[^home] The brand's directions are three minutes per area on clean, makeup-free skin.[^brand] What is missing is any device-level trial, and no LED Technologies 510(k) record carries the Lux name.[^openfda]

## The Rubric

**Mild to moderate acne, on the Acne setting**
- **Effect size: modest, and carried by the modality.** Blue plus red LED for acne is Level B, Grade I, the best grade in the GRADE review's acne section.[^home] Nothing measures what this particular device achieves.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** The brand's acne-adjacent number is a self-reported percentage from an uncontrolled consumer study with no stated n.[^brand]

**Fine lines and wrinkles, on the Anti-Aging setting**
- **Effect size: minimal.** LED for facial rejuvenation is Level B, behind home fractional nonablative lasers at Level A.[^home] The brand's 97.4% is participants reporting what they saw.[^brand]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Same consumer study, same missing n and missing control.[^brand]

**Knowing what you are actually buying**
- **Effect size: not applicable; a disclosure point.** The page states a wavelength range and a list of colors, not a peak wavelength per color.[^brand] Since the blue-red grade turns on which wavelengths are present, a range from 415 to 880 nm tells you blue and infrared are the endpoints but not how much of the output is the red that carries the acne pairing.
- **Evidence quality: not applicable; brand-stated specification.**

## The Evidence

**What the clearance record says about the Lux name: nothing.** LED Technologies holds 15 FDA 510(k) clearances and none is named Lux Glo, Lux, or Glo.[^openfda] The firm does disclose: all 15 are filed as 510(k) Summaries rather than Statements, the same pattern as [[nuface-trinity-microcurrent-device]] and the opposite of [[foreo-espada-blue-light-pen]].[^openfda] The gap is matching a retail name to a file. The brand does run an "FDA Cleared" browse category, which is true of its range in general.[^brand]

**The consumer study is the weakest kind of support, and the age range makes it weaker.** Two figures appear with an asterisk, and the asterisk resolves to one sentence naming only the age band, 39 to 66, and adding that individual results may vary.[^brand] A consumer-perception study with no control arm cannot separate the device from regression to the mean, seasonal change, or the attention people pay their skin when they start a new routine. The specific problem here is the pairing: a 100% figure covering "pores, skin tone, texture, or overall skin condition" sits under an "anti-acne" label, while the participants were 39 to 66, an age band in which inflammatory acne is much less common than the signs of aging the other number is about.

**Wording to watch across this brand.** The product text says "clinically tested, multi-wavelength LED light therapy", which attaches to the modality rather than to the device, exactly as [[revive-light-therapy-lux-spot]] does.[^brand] Elsewhere on the same page the line-level copy escalates to "clinically proven LED wavelengths with higher optical output energy".[^brand] Proven is a stronger word than tested, and it is still the wavelengths being described, not this device.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Home LED is cleared in this category for mild to moderate acne; nodular, cystic or scarring acne is a reason to see a dermatologist rather than buy a device.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"clinically tested, multi-wavelength LED light therapy"**. Accurate as scoped.[^brand] It claims the modality was tested, not this unit, and that reading is defensible.
- **"clinically proven LED wavelengths with higher optical output energy"**. Weaker than it sounds.[^brand] Higher than what is not stated, and no optical output figure appears on the page.
- **"97.4% saw visible improvement in fine lines, wrinkles, or skin tone"** and **"100% saw visible improvement in pores, skin tone, texture, or overall skin condition"**. Both are self-reported, uncontrolled, and carry no sample size.[^brand] Treat them as customer-satisfaction figures, not efficacy data.
- **"See results in as little as 2 weeks!"**. Brand wording with no cited measurement.[^brand]
- **"Red, infrared, amber, and blue light"** and **"Primary wavelength range"** of 415 to 880 nm. A specification, and the one that matters most here, since blue plus red is what the GRADE review graded.[^brand][^home]
