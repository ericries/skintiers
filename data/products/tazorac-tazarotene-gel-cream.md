Tazorac is the brand name for prescription [[tazarotene]] gel and cream, sold at 0.05% and 0.1% by Almirall, LLC, and FDA-approved for two separate uses: plaque psoriasis and facial acne vulgaris.[^gel][^cream]

> A real, dual-indication prescription retinoid, and the older, higher-strength tazarotene product line (the newer Arazlo lotion is a lower 0.045% strength aimed at better tolerability; see [[arazlo-tazarotene-0-045-lotion]]). Only the 0.1% strength is approved for acne; both 0.05% and 0.1% are approved for psoriasis. In its own registration trials, roughly a fifth to two-fifths of patients reached at least 75% improvement, a real vehicle-beating effect but well short of most patients clearing. Retinoid irritation, burning, stinging, dryness, peeling, is common, and it is strictly contraindicated in pregnancy.[^gel][^cream]

## Summary

Tazorac is generic-name [[tazarotene]], a prescription topical [[retinoids|retinoid]], formulated as a gel and a cream, each sold at 0.05% and 0.1%.[^gel][^cream] It carries two separate FDA indications. The gel label states it is "indicated for the topical treatment of patients with plaque psoriasis of up to 20% body surface area involvement" at both strengths, and that the 0.1% gel "is also indicated for the topical treatment of patients with facial acne vulgaris of mild to moderate severity."[^gel] The cream label indicates both strengths "for the topical treatment of patients with plaque psoriasis," with the 0.1% cream "also indicated for the topical treatment of patients with acne vulgaris."[^cream] Note that only the 0.1% strength is approved for acne in either formulation; the 0.05% strength is psoriasis-only. Neither label establishes safety or efficacy for acne under age 12.[^gel][^cream] Tazorac is the older, higher-strength tazarotene brand; a newer, lower-strength 0.045% lotion, Arazlo, was formulated for better tolerability and carries its own separate acne trials (see [[arazlo-tazarotene-0-045-lotion]]).[^gel]

The product-specific case rests on the gel's own vehicle-controlled registration trials, described in full under [The Evidence](#the-evidence): in acne, 38% and 18% of 0.1%-gel patients reached at least 75% global improvement at 12 weeks in two trials, versus 20% and 10% on vehicle; in psoriasis, 28% and 18% of 0.05%-gel patients and 38% and 25% of 0.1%-gel patients reached that same threshold across two trials, versus 12% and 10% on vehicle.[^gel] That is a real, vehicle-beating effect in both conditions, but most treated patients did not reach that threshold.

Local irritation is the consistent limiting side effect. The gel label lists desquamation, burning/stinging, dry skin, erythema and pruritus as common in acne trials, and pruritus, burning/stinging, erythema, worsening of psoriasis, irritation, and skin pain as common in psoriasis trials, each in 10 to 30% of patients.[^gel] The cream label lists a similar profile: desquamation, dry skin, erythema, and burning sensation in acne trials, and pruritus, erythema, and burning in psoriasis trials.[^cream] Both formulations are strictly contraindicated in pregnancy: "TAZORAC Gel is contraindicated in: Pregnancy. Retinoids may cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant female," with the same wording, substituting "Cream," on the cream label.[^gel][^cream] This is a prescription drug; see a prescriber for use and dosing, and see [[acne]] and [[psoriasis]] for the broader condition context.

## The Evidence

The general case for tazarotene as a receptor-selective retinoid pro-drug, and how it compares across formulations and to [[tretinoin]] and [[adapalene]], is covered on [[tazarotene]] and [[retinoids]]; this section covers what the Tazorac labels report for these products.

For acne, the gel label reports two large vehicle-controlled trials of the 0.1% gel applied once daily in patients with facial acne vulgaris; at 12 weeks, patients reaching at least 75% global improvement were 38% and 18% on the gel, versus 20% and 10% on vehicle.[^gel] For psoriasis, the gel's two registration trials reported patients reaching that same 75%-global-improvement threshold at 12 weeks in 28% and 18% of 0.05%-gel patients and 38% and 25% of 0.1%-gel patients, versus 12% and 10% on vehicle.[^gel]

> **Bottom line.** In both of its own FDA indications, the 0.1% gel beat vehicle by a wide margin on a 75%-improvement threshold, a real effect, but most patients across both conditions, including active-drug patients, did not reach that threshold at 12 weeks.[^gel]

The cream carries its own separate vehicle-controlled trial program for both indications, at the same two strengths and with the same pregnancy contraindication, but this page does not restate cream-specific trial figures beyond what is quoted above; see the cream label for its own numbers.[^cream] How tazarotene's registration evidence compares to the newer Arazlo lotion and to other retinoids is covered on [[tazarotene]], which this page does not re-argue.

## What's In It

The active and inactive ingredients as declared on the FDA-approved prescribing information.[^gel][^cream]

Active:

- [[tazarotene]] 0.05% or 0.1%, a prescription topical [[retinoids|retinoid]], in gel or cream form.[^gel][^cream]

Gel inactive ingredients: ascorbic acid, benzyl alcohol, butylated hydroxyanisole, butylated hydroxytoluene, carbomer homopolymer type B, edetate disodium, hexylene glycol, poloxamer 407, polyethylene glycol 400, polysorbate 40, purified water, and tromethamine.[^gel]

Cream inactive ingredients: benzyl alcohol 1%, carbomer 1342, carbomer homopolymer type B, edetate disodium, medium chain triglycerides, mineral oil, purified water, sodium hydroxide, sodium thiosulfate, and sorbitan monooleate.[^cream]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **A dual-purpose retinoid for acne and psoriasis.** Accurate as a regulatory description: both the gel and cream carry separate FDA indications for facial acne vulgaris (0.1% only) and plaque psoriasis (0.05% and 0.1%).[^gel][^cream] It is not accurate to imply most patients clear either condition; registration-trial responder rates at 12 weeks ranged from about a fifth to two-fifths of treated patients.[^gel]
