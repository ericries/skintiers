SKIN1004 sells this as the Hyalu-Cica Water-Fit Sun Serum UV, a lightweight facial sunscreen labeled SPF50, built on the four-filter avobenzone/homosalate/octisalate/octocrylene combination and marketed for hydration under its "Hyalu" (hyaluronic acid) and "Cica" (centella asiatica) branding, confirmed at that exact name, size, and ingredient list on skin1004.com and sold under a closely matching name at Target.[^1][^2]

> This product's four UV filters split unevenly by function. Avobenzone, homosalate, octisalate, and octocrylene together give reasonably strong UVB (sunburn) protection, the same recognized US-monograph set covered in general on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], but the formula's only UVA-contributing filter is avobenzone, an older, inherently unstable filter that offers weaker UVA protection than the Tinosorb- and Mexoryl-class filters used in some sibling Korean sunscreens, including the four dedicated modern UVA filters in [[beauty-of-joseon-aqua-fresh-sun-serum|Beauty of Joseon's Aqua-fresh]], its closest comparator in this dataset. SPF50 is a Korean (MFDS) label claim, not independently FDA-tested for this SKU, and the hyaluronic acid ("Hyalu") and centella asiatica ("Cica") hydration ingredients sit well down the ingredient list at undisclosed concentrations.[^1]

## Summary

The Hyalu-Cica Water-Fit Sun Serum UV is part of SKIN1004's "cica" family, alongside the [[skin1004-madagascar-centella-air-fit-suncream|Centella Air-Fit Suncream Plus]] and the [[skin1004-madagascar-centella-asiatica-100-ampoule|Centella Ampoule]]. Where the Air-Fit Suncream is a mineral (zinc oxide/titanium dioxide) formula, this one is a chemical sunscreen: the brand's own ingredient list opens with avobenzone 2.7%, homosalate 13.6%, octisalate 4.5%, and octocrylene 9%, the classic older US-monograph filter combination described in general on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^1]

That filter set matters for a US buyer specifically. As the general sunscreen page explains, avobenzone is "the sole chemical approved by the FDA to afford UVA protection" in the US, and homosalate, octisalate, and octocrylene are all part of the same long-approved US menu, unlike the Tinosorb- and Mexoryl-family filters used in some other Korean sunscreens sold into this market ([see The Evidence](#the-evidence)). That makes this product's UV-filter chemistry unusually uncontroversial for a US audience even though it is imported and its SPF50 label is a Korean (MFDS), not FDA, rating. Regulatory familiarity is not the same thing as UVA performance, though: [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]'s own cited source states that Tinosorb- and Mexoryl-class filters "offer better protection from UVA and free radicals than avobenzone," so this formula's single-filter UVA coverage is weaker than the modern multi-filter systems used in some comparator products, even though its regulatory footing is more familiar to US buyers.

Past the filters, the formula's hydration ingredients (hydrolyzed sodium hyaluronate, sodium hyaluronate, and hyaluronic acid itself) and centella asiatica extract sit well down a 35-ingredient list, all at undisclosed concentrations.[^1] General evidence for topical hyaluronic acid's surface-hydration effect is on [[hyaluronic-acid]]; general evidence for centella asiatica is on [[centella-asiatica]]. No trial of this specific product was found.

The brand's own storefront lists the 50 ml size at $19.00 (10% off to $17.10 at last check); Target separately carries a matching 50 ml listing at $25.99 (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy)).[^1][^2]

## The Evidence

No published trial isolates this exact product. Its grades rest on two things: the general, well-supported case for broad-spectrum sunscreen (photoaging and skin-cancer reduction), set out with its caveats on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], and the identity of the specific filters this formula uses, read directly off its own ingredient list.[^1]

That general page, [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], explains that regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use has genuine randomized-trial support for slowing photoaging and reducing skin-cancer burden, though that evidence traces largely to a single long-running Australian cohort and an independent Cochrane review is more cautious. It also explains the country-specific filter gap this product sits inside: the US sunscreen monograph has historically offered a narrower filter menu than Korea, the EU, or Japan, so many US-approved products, including this one, rely on the older set of filters, avobenzone, homosalate, octocrylene, octisalate. Avobenzone is described there as the sole chemical approved by the FDA to afford UVA protection, and inherently unstable, which is why octocrylene is included as a stabilizer. Because this product's four listed filters are all part of that same recognized US set, it is easier to place its UV-filter safety and regulatory footing than a Korean sunscreen built on Tinosorb- or Mexoryl-family filters.

SPF50 is the brand's own printed label claim and follows Korea's MFDS testing system, not the FDA's; no independent lab test of this SKU's actual SPF or broad-spectrum performance was located. PA (UVA) rating was not stated on the fetched product page.[^1]

> **Bottom line.** This product's UV filters are all part of the long-approved, FDA-monograph US filter set, avobenzone, homosalate, octisalate, octocrylene, more US-regulator-familiar than the Korea-only filters used in some sibling Korean sunscreens in this dataset. But the set splits unevenly by function: four recognized filters give reasonably strong UVB coverage, while the lone UVA-contributing filter, avobenzone, is older and inherently unstable, and [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]'s own cited source rates it as offering weaker UVA protection than the Tinosorb- and Mexoryl-class filters standard elsewhere, including the four such filters in [[beauty-of-joseon-aqua-fresh-sun-serum|Beauty of Joseon's Aqua-fresh]]. Its SPF50 label is still an unverified Korean claim, and the hyaluronic acid and centella asiatica ingredients carry only general, product-unspecific evidence.[^1]
