Some By Mi Yuja Niacin 30 Days Blemish Care Serum (sold in current packaging as the Yuja Niacin Anti Blemish Serum) is a lightweight Korean serum built on 10% niacinamide in a base of 83% yuja (citron) extract, with vitamin C derivatives, licorice root extract, and soothing bisabolol, in a 50 ml bottle.[^iherb]

> An inexpensive, widely sold K-beauty niacinamide serum aimed at post-acne dark spots and oily, blemish-prone skin. Topical niacinamide is well tolerated and has modest evidence for evening facial hyperpigmentation, supporting the skin barrier, lowering facial oil, and helping mild blemishes; that health-oriented case sits on [[niacinamide]]. The 83% yuja extract is the hero marketing ingredient rather than a proven active, and the 10% niacinamide is above the 2% to 5% used in the trials. It lists at about $19.70 for the 50 ml bottle.[^iherb][^stylevana][^cc]

## Summary

Some By Mi Yuja Niacin is a thin, fast-absorbing serum aimed at oily and blemish-prone skin and at the dark marks left behind after a spot heals. Its health-oriented uses come from [[niacinamide]], a form of vitamin B3 present at 10%: topical niacinamide is well tolerated and has modest evidence, much of it from small or manufacturer-run studies, for lightening facial hyperpigmentation, for supporting the skin barrier and lowering water loss, for [lowering how much oil the face produces](#the-evidence), and for mild inflammatory acne. That general case, with its primary trials and their limits, is set out on [[niacinamide]]. The "brightening" the brand foregrounds splits into two things: fading actual dark spots, which is the health-relevant pigment use above, and a general radiance or glow on already-even skin, which is a cosmetic effect.

The name and the label lead with yuja, a Korean citron (Citrus junos) whose extract makes up 83% of the formula in place of most of the water.[^iherb] The evidenced active in the bottle is the niacinamide; the yuja extract is the brand's signature ingredient rather than a proven one, and the formula also carries small amounts of two stable vitamin C derivatives, licorice root extract, and adenosine low in the ingredient list ([see What's In It](#whats-in-it)).

Two things set it apart from a barebones niacinamide serum, and they pull in different directions. It is a more cosmetic, sensorial formula than a plain water-based serum, built on a citrus-extract base with fragrance and plant oils. And its 10% niacinamide sits above the 2% to 5% used in the clinical work: the cosmetic chemists at Chemist Confessions write that "niacinamide is actually clinically tested at 2% to 5%," that the range delivers the "sebum-reducing, brightening, barrier-supporting benefits of niacinamide," and that "higher doses will always increase the risk of skin irritation even for the most seemingly vanilla ingredient."[^cc] It is inexpensive and easy to find: Stylevana lists the 50 ml bottle at $19.70 ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28), and iHerb shows it at 4.6 out of 5 across 2,454 customer reviews.[^iherb][^stylevana] A plainer 10% niacinamide serum such as [[the-ordinary-niacinamide-10-zinc-1|The Ordinary Niacinamide 10% + Zinc 1%]] costs less per ml.

The brand's "Clinical Solution," "Non-Comedogenic Tested," and "Dermatologist Tested" language is weighed against the evidence under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Topical niacinamide is a well-tolerated active with modest evidence for lightening facial hyperpigmentation, supporting the skin barrier, and mild inflammatory acne; how well each use holds up, and how much of it comes from small or manufacturer-run trials, is set out on [[niacinamide]]. Its effect on facial oil is narrower and worth naming here because it is the product's oil-control positioning: a 2% niacinamide moisturizer significantly lowered the rate at which facial glands put out oil in a 4-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 100 Japanese subjects, and lowered surface oil in a 6-week split-face trial of 30 subjects, though both measured sebum rather than acne lesions; the two trials are detailed on [[niacinamide-sebum-studies]].

What is specific to this product is narrower. It is a 10% niacinamide serum in an 83% yuja extract base, and no published clinical trial tests this exact formulation, so its grades rest on the category evidence for niacinamide at the concentrations that were actually studied, which were lower than 10%.[^iherb] In the pigmentation trials niacinamide was tested at 4% to 5%, where 4% niacinamide matched hydroquinone 4% in one small independent melasma study and was better tolerated; in the barrier work it lowered water loss at levels around 2%; in acne, 4% nicotinamide matched topical clindamycin in a single randomized trial. That primary evidence, with its numbers, denominators, and limits, lives on [[niacinamide]], and none of it uses a 10% product or the yuja base. On the concentration question, Chemist Confessions state that "niacinamide is actually clinically tested at 2% to 5%" and that "higher doses will always increase the risk of skin irritation even for the most seemingly vanilla ingredient."[^cc]

## What's In It

The ingredient list (INCI) as declared on the retailer listing.[^iherb] The ingredients with a functional role are linked to their own pages; the rest is the base, texture, and preservative system.

Actives and notable ingredients:

- [[niacinamide]] 10%, a form of vitamin B3
- yuja (Citrus junos) fruit extract 83%, the citron extract the formula uses in place of most of the water, and the brand's signature ingredient
- [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|3-O-ethyl ascorbic acid and sodium ascorbyl phosphate]], two stable vitamin C derivatives, low in the list
- alpha-bisabolol, a soothing component of chamomile; licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) root extract; adenosine; and panthenol (provitamin B5)

Base, texture, and preservative system: caprylic/capric triglyceride and hydrogenated poly(C6-14 olefin) (emollients), 1,2-hexanediol and butylene glycol, [[glycerin]], carbomer and xanthan gum (thickeners), arginine (pH adjuster), rapeseed seed oil and other plant extracts, [[tocopherol]] (vitamin E), a trace B-vitamin and beta-carotene complex, hydrogenated lecithin, ethylhexylglycerin, and disodium EDTA.[^iherb]

The formula also contains citron peel oil and the fragrance components limonene and linalool.[^iherb]

## Tolerability

The manufacturer labels the serum "Non-Comedogenic Tested" and "Dermatologist Tested."[^iherb] These are label claims rather than independently reported outcomes. The formula contains citron (yuja) peel oil at a declared 293.1 ppm and the fragrance allergens limonene and linalool, which can be a consideration for fragrance-sensitive or reactive skin.[^iherb] Chemist Confessions note that niacinamide above the studied 2% to 5% range carries a higher chance of irritation without added proven benefit.[^cc]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing terms not established by the graded evidence above:

- **"Yuja Extract 83%" as the hero ingredient.** Yuja (Citrus junos) extract is the base the formula is built on and the brand's signature ingredient; the evidenced active in the bottle is the 10% niacinamide.[^iherb] The niacinamide evidence is set out on [[niacinamide]].
- **"Anti Blemish," "cares for dark spots," and "brighten up dull skin tone."** Niacinamide's evidenced pigment use is specifically fading facial hyperpigmentation and melasma, comparable to hydroquinone 4% in one small trial, and mild inflammatory acne comparable to topical clindamycin in one RCT, both on [[niacinamide]]; generic radiance for already-even skin is beyond what those studies tested.[^iherb]
- **"Alpha-Melight brightening capsules" and "Clinical Solution" / "New Cleanical Solution."** Proprietary and positioning terms; no independent trial reports their clinical effect.[^iherb]
- **The 10% niacinamide concentration.** The 10% strength is above the 2% to 5% at which niacinamide was clinically tested; Chemist Confessions state that range delivers the ingredient's benefits and that "higher doses will always increase the risk of skin irritation."[^cc]
