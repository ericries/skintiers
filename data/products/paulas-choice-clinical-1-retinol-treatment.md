Paula's Choice Clinical 1% Retinol Treatment is a leave-on lotion built around a manufacturer-stated 1% [[retinol]] concentration, paired with [[peptides]] and a vitamin C ester, sold by [[paulas-choice|Paula's Choice]].[^pcsg][^dermstore] It sits alongside the brand's lower-strength [[paulas-choice-skin-perfecting-8-aha-gel|Skin Perfecting 8% AHA Gel Exfoliant]] in the wider Paula's Choice lineup.

> A high-strength retinol treatment: the manufacturer states "High-strength 1% retinol concentration" on its own product page, at the upper end of over-the-counter retinol dosing.[^pcsg] Retinol has vehicle-controlled trial evidence for fine wrinkles and pigment in aging skin (see [[retinol]]), but the manufacturer's own clinical support for this specific formula is a study described only as "an independent clinical study with 33 subjects" over 4 weeks, with no control arm, statistical result, or published journal disclosed, detailed in [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^dermstore] The manufacturer's own directions frame 1% retinol as strong enough to require a gradual introduction ("limit initial use to three times per week; gradually increase frequency... as tolerated") and warn of a possible "delayed sensitized reaction."[^pcsg] At $65.00 for 30 ml at Dermstore, it is priced above most single-form OTC retinol serums.[^dermstore]

## Summary

Clinical 1% Retinol Treatment is a lotion-textured [[retinol]] product from Paula's Choice, sold direct through the brand's own regional storefronts and through Dermstore in the US.[^pcsg][^dermstore] Unlike many OTC retinol serums, the manufacturer states the retinol concentration directly in its own product copy: "High-strength 1% retinol concentration."[^pcsg] Dermstore's listing for the same SKU independently confirms both the product name and a "1% Retinol" callout in its own ingredient summary, and reproduces an identical full ingredient declaration.[^dermstore] One percent is at the high end of over-the-counter retinol dosing; the manufacturer's FAQ describes it as comparable in strength to a prescription "0.02 tretinoin," a brand comparison not backed by a cited trial and treated as a marketing claim below.[^pcsg]

The general case for topical [[retinol]], a vitamin A precursor with vehicle-controlled trial evidence for fine wrinkles and pigment in aging skin, is mapped on its own ingredient page. What is specific to this product is the declared 1% dose (unusually explicit for an OTC retinol label) paired with a "Triple Peptide Blend" and a vitamin C ester (tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate), both listed ahead of several botanical extracts on the full INCI.[^dermstore] [[peptides|Cosmetic peptides]] have modest, mostly preliminary evidence for the appearance of aging, and this formula's peptide contribution is not tested as an isolated variable in any product-specific study; the wider peptide evidence is set out on [[peptides]]. Tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate is one of the lipid-soluble vitamin C esters described on [[vitamin-c]], where conversion to the active L-ascorbic acid form is reported as being in doubt.

The formula's higher retinol dose brings a documented trade-off: the manufacturer's own directions call for a gradual introduction schedule ("limit initial use to three times per week; gradually increase frequency to every other night, then finally each evening as tolerated") and warn that "some people may experience a delayed sensitized reaction to 1% retinol," with instructions to reduce frequency or mix the product into another moisturizer if "redness, flaking, tenderness, or drying of skin" occurs.[^pcsg] The manufacturer's own "clinically proven" wrinkle claim, reproduced on the Dermstore listing, is attributed only to "an independent clinical study with 33 subjects" over 4 weeks, with no control arm, statistical result, or published journal cited, detailed in [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^dermstore] It is listed at $65.00 for 30 ml at Dermstore (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-07).[^dermstore]

## The Evidence

Topical [[retinol]] is a well-studied vitamin A precursor with vehicle-controlled trial evidence for improving fine wrinkles and pigment in aging skin; that evidence is graded in full on its own page. What is specific to this product is that the manufacturer discloses the concentration directly, "High-strength 1% retinol concentration," on its own product page, rather than leaving it undosed as many competing OTC retinol serums do.[^pcsg] On the full INCI, retinol sits 17th of 53 ingredients, immediately after the vitamin C ester tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate and ahead of the peptide and humectant ingredients, consistent with a sub-1%-by-weight active positioned among the formula's other functional actives rather than its base ingredients.[^pcsg][^dermstore]

No published trial tests this exact formula. The manufacturer's own product-specific efficacy claim, carried on the Dermstore listing, states the product is "Clinically proven to visibly reduce wrinkles in 4 weeks" and "Based on an independent clinical study with 33 subjects," with no control or vehicle arm, no statistical result, and no published journal disclosed.[^dermstore] Separate before/after image alt-text on the same listing states that "91% noticed an increase in skins firmness" [sic] and "94% agree skin looks and feels healthier" after 8 weeks, again without a disclosed study design.[^dermstore]

> Bottom line. Retinol has real category-level evidence for fine wrinkles and pigment (see [[retinol]]), and this product's 1% dose is disclosed by the manufacturer at the high end of OTC retinol strength, but the formula's own clinical support is an unnamed, undisclosed-design study of 33 subjects, not an independently verifiable trial.[^dermstore]
