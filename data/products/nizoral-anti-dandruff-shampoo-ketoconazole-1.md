Nizoral Anti-Dandruff Shampoo is an over-the-counter medicated shampoo from Kramer Laboratories built around the antifungal drug ketoconazole at 1% w/w, sold in single bottles from 4 to 19 fl oz plus a 22 fl oz twin-pack of two 11 fl oz bottles, and used intermittently (every 3 to 4 days) rather than as a daily shampoo.[^1][^2]

> An OTC drug, not a cosmetic: the FDA-labeled use is to control "flaking, scaling and itching associated with dandruff," and ketoconazole is one of the better-evidenced dandruff actives, with randomized trials and a Cochrane review behind the drug class.[^2][^4] The main caveat is strength: most of that evidence used 2% ketoconazole, and in the one randomized head-to-head trial the 1% concentration sold here was effective but significantly inferior to 2% at reducing flaking and Malassezia counts.[^3] A reasonable first-line dandruff shampoo, with the stronger 2% version the usual escalation if 1% is not enough.

## Summary

This is the ketoconazole dandruff shampoo most people can buy without a prescription in the US, mid-priced for a drugstore medicated shampoo (about $17 for 7 fl oz as listed at Target).[^5] It is regulated as an OTC drug: the Drug Facts label states the active ingredient as "Ketoconazole 1% w/w," the use as "controls flaking, scaling and itching associated with dandruff," and directions of one lathered application every 3 to 4 days for up to 8 weeks, then as needed.[^2]

[[ketoconazole|Ketoconazole]] is an azole antifungal that works against Malassezia yeasts, the fungi associated with dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis. The class evidence is real: a 2015 Cochrane review of topical antifungals for seborrhoeic dermatitis pooled 12 ketoconazole trials (3,253 participants) and found a 31% lower risk of failed clearance versus placebo, with remission rates similar to topical steroids and fewer side effects.[^4]

The product-specific question is the 1% concentration. The prescription-strength studies and most of the Cochrane evidence used 2%. One open randomized trial (66 patients, severe dandruff or scalp seborrhoeic dermatitis) compared the two strengths directly: both worked, but 2% was significantly superior (p < 0.001) at reducing flakiness and Malassezia density, with a trend to fewer relapses in follow-up.[^3] So this product's grades rest partly on directly relevant 1% data, unusual for this site, but the same trial that supports it also shows it is the weaker of the two strengths ([see The Evidence](#the-evidence)).

## The Evidence

Two health uses, graded above:

**Dandruff (the labeled use).** This is what the product is approved and labeled for, and the claim "clinically proven to control flaking, scaling and itching from dandruff" on the label matches the labeled indication rather than overreaching it.[^1][^2] The 1% strength itself was tested in Pierard-Franchimont and colleagues' open-label, randomized parallel-group trial (unblinded, with a clinical-assessment endpoint, and no placebo or vehicle arm): after 2 and 4 weeks, 1% ketoconazole improved flaking, Malassezia counts, and overall dandruff severity from baseline, while 2% was "significantly superior over KET 1% (p < 0.001) for decreasing both in flakiness and Malassezia density," with only 6 mild adverse events across the whole trial.[^3]

**Scalp seborrheic dermatitis.** The Cochrane review found topical ketoconazole 2% gave a "31% lower risk of failed clearance of rashes compared with placebo (risk ratio (RR) 0.69, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.59 to 0.81, eight studies, low-quality evidence)" at four weeks, matched steroids on remission, and caused 44% fewer side effects than steroids.[^4] That is supportive class evidence, but it is 2% evidence, and seborrheic dermatitis is not on this product's OTC label; the label in fact warns against use "on scalp that is broken or inflamed."[^2] The review carries its own dedicated "Ketoconazole (2%) versus ketoconazole (1%)" comparison section, drawing on the same Pierard-Franchimont trial cited above, where 2% again outperformed 1% (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.80), confirming the 1%-vs-2% gap is not limited to dandruff.[^4]

> **Bottom line.** The active works and the labeled dandruff claim is honest. The known trade-off of this specific product is its 1% strength: directly tested, effective, and significantly weaker than the 2% concentration most of the evidence is built on.[^3][^4]

## What's In It

As an OTC drug the label separates the active from everything else. Active ingredient: "Ketoconazole 1% w/w."[^2]

Inactive ingredients, verbatim from the Drug Facts label: "benzyl alcohol, BHT, blue 1, citric acid, cocamide MEA, fragrance, glycol distearate, hydrochloric acid, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, polyquaternium-7, sodium benzoate, sodium chloride, sodium cocoyl sarcosinate, sodium hydroxide, sodium laureth sulfate, tetrasodium EDTA, water."[^2]

Note the list is alphabetical, as OTC Drug Facts inactive lists are, so unlike a cosmetic INCI list it does not convey concentration order. The base is a conventional detergent shampoo (sodium laureth sulfate and sodium cocoyl sarcosinate surfactants) and it does contain added fragrance and a blue dye.[^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by the product-specific evidence above:

- **"10x more effective at fighting the primary cause of dandruff than the active ingredient in the leading dandruff shampoo."** The brand's own footnote concedes the limits: "Based on laboratory testing of the active ingredient in Nizoral compared to the active ingredient in the leading dandruff shampoo. Not a product to product comparison."[^1] A lab potency figure against an unnamed comparator active is not a clinical outcome.
- **"The only over-the-counter anti-dandruff shampoo that contains Ketoconazole."** A US-market exclusivity claim taken from the brand site; not independently verified here.[^1]
- **"Gets right to the root of dandruff by controlling the fungus that causes it."** Simplified mechanism talk; the product's own label hedges more carefully, saying a fungus found on every human head "is often associated with dandruff."[^2]
- **"Clean, fresh scent."** A cosmetic claim; the formula does contain added fragrance, which fragrance-sensitive users may want to know.[^2]
