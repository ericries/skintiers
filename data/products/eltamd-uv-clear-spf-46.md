EltaMD UV Clear is an oil-free, fragrance-free face sunscreen lotion rated Broad Spectrum SPF 46, built on the UV filters zinc oxide 9.0% and octinoxate 7.5% with niacinamide in the base.[^1][^5]

> A lightweight US mineral (zinc oxide) face sunscreen, and broad-spectrum protection is the best-evidenced way to lower photoaging and sun-related skin-cancer risk. Skin doctors have recommended it for years, particularly for sensitive, acne-prone, and rosacea-prone skin. Its honest limit is the UVA side: a 9% zinc oxide load gives solid sunburn (UVB) protection but more modest UVA coverage than the modern filters standard in the EU and Korea, and it is priced above most US mineral sunscreens. The SPF and UVA filter science is set out on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^6][^7]

## Summary

UV Clear is a daily facial sunscreen from EltaMD, a US brand widely sold through dermatology offices. It is a light mineral lotion made for everyday facial use and is popular with people who have sensitive, acne-prone, or rosacea-prone skin, and it is often worn under makeup. Its core purpose is health rather than cosmetics: broad-spectrum protection against UV is the single best-evidenced way to reduce photoaging and lower the risk of sun-related skin cancer, a case with randomized-trial support that is set out on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

What sets it apart from a plainer sunscreen is its all-mineral filter system and its extras. The only sun filter that covers UVA here is [[zinc-oxide]] at 9%, a photostable mineral filter, so the formula avoids the organic (chemical) filters some sensitive skin reacts to, and it is oil-free, fragrance-free, and carries niacinamide ([the filter breakdown is in The Evidence](#the-evidence)).[^1][^5] It is a longtime dermatologist favorite: Allure titled its review "Why Dermatologists Love the EltaMD UV Clear," and quoted board-certified Miami dermatologist Christopher O'Connell saying "My favorite sunscreen is EltaMD Clear," which he calls "a clear zinc-oxide sunscreen that also contains niacinamide to help give the skin a glow."[^6]

The honest caveat is the UVA side of that protection. Measured against the global field, a 9% zinc oxide load sits below the high-load modern filters (Tinosorb, Mexoryl) standard in the EU, Korea, and Australia, and the US "Broad Spectrum" mark is a single pass/fail test rather than a graded UVA rating ([The Evidence](#the-evidence) sets out the filter science).[^1][^4] Reception is not all praise: Wirecutter tested it and found it "fell short of our expectations," with panel testers finding it "greasy compared with our picks" and one tester with darker skin noticing "a blue sheen" after rubbing it in.[^7]

On how it is worn, EltaMD's own directions say to "Apply liberally to face and neck 15 minutes before sun exposure" and to "Reapply at least every 2 hours."[^5] Allure's Sarah Kinonen describes wearing it "in the morning, after my serums and moisturizer, right before my BB cream and bronzer."[^6] It is on the pricier side for a US sunscreen, listed at about $45 for the 1.7 oz size ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28), well above most drugstore mineral sunscreens.[^5]

## The Evidence

Regular use of a broad-spectrum sunscreen has randomized-trial support for slowing photoaging and for reducing melanoma and squamous-cell tumor burden, among the strongest clinical evidence in skincare; that evidence, and how the SPF (UVB) and UVA axes differ, is set out on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]. What is specific to this product is its filter set and its FDA label rating.

The Drug Facts panel lists the actives as zinc oxide 9.0% and octinoxate 7.5%, at SPF 46.[^1] SPF is a UVB (sunburn) number: the SPF 46 rating certifies UVB strength and does not by itself certify UVA (photoaging) protection, which is a separate axis described on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

In this formula the two axes come from two different filters. Octinoxate (ethylhexyl methoxycinnamate) is a UVB filter; a 2013 review describes ethylhexyl methoxycinnamate as an agent "which filters UVB."[^3] The UVA coverage therefore rests on the zinc oxide. A review of the two mineral filters reports that titanium dioxide is "primarily a UVB absorbing compound, while ZnO is more efficient in UVA absorption," and that zinc oxide "absorbs more UVA-1 radiation," the longest-wavelength UVA.[^2] Zinc oxide is a photostable, broad-spectrum mineral filter, so unlike the avobenzone-based US sunscreens it does not depend on a stabilizer; at 9% it provides genuine but moderate UVA attenuation.

Two limits keep this off the top of the UVA scale. The US "Broad Spectrum" claim is a single pass/fail test, not a graded UVA rating, so this product carries no numeric UVA figure of the kind the EU (UVA-PF) and Asian (PA) systems require.[^1] And measured against the global field, a 9% zinc oxide load sits below the high-load modern-filter products (built on Tinosorb or Mexoryl) sold in the EU, Korea, and Australia: a 2020 Journal of Drugs in Dermatology review argues that US sunscreens as a group let through substantially more UVA than European sunscreens, and that the modern filters available abroad protect against UVA and free radicals better than avobenzone.[^4] The pass/fail mechanism, the critical-wavelength definition, and the global filter landscape are set out on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

> **Bottom line.** The FDA-tested SPF 46 is a high UVB (sunburn) rating.[^1] The UVA (photoaging) protection comes from zinc oxide 9%, a photostable mineral filter more efficient at UVA than titanium dioxide, because octinoxate protects only against UVB.[^2][^3] That UVA coverage is genuine but moderate; the US "Broad Spectrum" mark is a pass/fail test rather than a graded UVA rating, and a 9% zinc oxide load is below the high-load modern-filter (Tinosorb, Mexoryl) sunscreens sold in the EU, Korea, and Australia.[^1][^4]

On reception, this is a genuine dermatologist favorite that beauty reviewers rate more highly than one independent test panel did. Allure ran its review under the headline "Why Dermatologists Love the EltaMD UV Clear," with board-certified Miami dermatologist Christopher O'Connell naming it "My favorite sunscreen," and its own reviewer, Sarah Kinonen, calling it "truly a great sunscreen" that "seeps into skin seamlessly, without creating more sensitivity."[^6] Wirecutter, testing a large field of face sunscreens, placed it among products that "fell short of our expectations": its panel found it "greasy compared with our picks," and one tester with darker skin noticed "a blue sheen" long after rubbing it in.[^7]

## What's In It

The active filters and inactive ingredients as declared on the FDA Drug Facts label.[^1] Ingredients with a functional role are linked to their own pages; the rest are the base, texture, and preservative system.

UV filters (Drug Facts actives):

- [[zinc-oxide]] 9.0%, a mineral (physical) broad-spectrum filter and the source of this formula's UVA coverage
- [[octinoxate]] 7.5% (ethylhexyl methoxycinnamate), a UVB filter

Other functional ingredients:

- [[niacinamide]] (vitamin B3), listed high on the inactive list; EltaMD markets the formula as containing 5% niacinamide[^5]
- [[hyaluronic-acid]] (as sodium hyaluronate), a humectant
- [[tocopherol]] (vitamin E, as tocopheryl acetate), an antioxidant
- lactic acid

Base, texture, and preservative system: purified water, cyclopentasiloxane and triethoxycaprylylsilane (silicone system), octyldodecyl neopentanoate (emollient), hydroxyethyl acrylate/sodium acryloyldimethyl taurate copolymer and polyisobutene (texture and film formers), butylene glycol, PEG-7 trimethylolpropane coconut ether and oleth-3 phosphate (emulsifier and surfactant), phenoxyethanol and iodopropynyl butylcarbamate (preservatives), and phosphoric acid (pH adjuster).[^1]

Not in the formula: no added fragrance, and no organic (chemical) UVA filter such as avobenzone; the UVA coverage is entirely from the mineral zinc oxide.[^1]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing terms not established by the graded evidence above:

- **"5% niacinamide," and skin prone to breakouts and rosacea.** EltaMD markets the niacinamide content (5%), says the formula "helps calm and protect skin prone to discoloration and breakouts," and lists the product as good for "Acne Prone" and "Rosacea" skin.[^5] Niacinamide's own evidence is on [[niacinamide]]; the FDA-rated benefit on this product's label is the Broad Spectrum SPF 46 UV protection.[^1] The acne and rosacea positioning is a manufacturer claim, and any brightening or "glow" benefit is cosmetic, separate from the graded sun-protection benefit.
- **"#1 Dermatologist-recommended professional sunscreen brand."** A manufacturer marketing claim.[^5] The attributed dermatologist and editor opinions above are quoted in The Evidence and the Summary.
- **"Oil-free," "fragrance-free," "non-comedogenic," "hypoallergenic," "paraben-free."** Manufacturer label and marketing claims, not independently tested outcomes.[^5]
