A prescription brush-on nail solution from Bausch, approved as NDA 203567 on 6 June 2014 and labelled for toenail onychomycosis caused by two specific dermatophytes.[^fda] **Bottom line:** the trial numbers are on [[efinaconazole]] and they are real but modest; what this page adds is what the label asks of you and which nails its trials left out.

> **Forty-eight weeks, once a day, and the clock does not start where you think.** The label directs application to affected toenails once daily for 48 weeks using an integrated flow-through brush, covering "the toenail, the toenail folds, toenail bed, hyponychium, and the undersurface of the toenail plate".[^label] Complete cure was assessed at week 52, four weeks after therapy ended.[^label] Nothing about this is a short course.

## Summary

The indication is narrower than "nail fungus". The label covers onychomycosis of the toenails "due to Trichophyton rubrum and Trichophyton mentagrophytes", two named dermatophytes, and says nothing about fingernails or about the other organisms that cause nail disease.[^label] The condition itself is on [[onychomycosis]].

**The trials picked their nails carefully, and that is what a reader should check first.** Enrolment required 20% to 50% clinical involvement of the target toenail in adults aged 18 to 70, and excluded nails "without dermatophytomas or lunula (matrix) involvement".[^label] So the cure rates quoted everywhere for this product were produced in moderately affected nails with no fungal mass and no involvement of the growth plate. If your nail has either, the published numbers do not describe it; that gap is the subject of [[dermatophytoma]].

The label's own endpoint hierarchy is worth noticing too. Mycologic cure, meaning negative culture and negative KOH, ran far ahead of complete cure in both trials, which are set out on [[efinaconazole]].[^label] Killing the fungus and restoring a normal-looking nail are not the same achievement, and the label measures them separately.

Practical notes from the label rather than from marketing: it comes as a clear, colourless to pale yellow solution in 4 mL and 8 mL bottles, is flammable and must be kept from heat or flame, and should be stored upright.[^label] The adverse reactions above 1% are ingrown toenails, application site dermatitis, application site vesicles and application site pain, which is a profile of local irritation rather than systemic risk.[^label] There is no boxed warning, verified absent from the current label.[^label]

**No price is given here.** Bausch does not publish a list price in the label and no primary source for one was found, so none is quoted rather than repeating a figure from a retailer. This is a prescription product whose cost to a patient depends on insurance.

## The Rubric

**Clearing toenail onychomycosis (health)**
Graded modest on gold-standard evidence, inherited from [[efinaconazole]] rather than earned again here. Two 52-week double-blind vehicle-controlled trials is a better evidence base than almost anything else on this site; the result that good evidence honestly reports is that most treated nails do not reach complete cure.

**Nails with a dermatophytoma or matrix involvement**
Graded unknown. The trials excluded them by design, so there is no figure to quote, and absence of a trial is not evidence of failure or of success.

## The Evidence

The trials, their sizes and every cure rate are on [[efinaconazole]] and are not repeated here. What this page records is the boundary around them: 48 weeks of daily treatment, assessment at week 52, adults 18 to 70, 20% to 50% nail involvement, no dermatophytoma, no matrix involvement, and two named dermatophytes in the indication.[^label]

For the over-the-counter end of topical antifungals and what those products are and are not labelled to treat, see [[otc-antifungal-creams-by-labelled-scope]] and [[lamisil-at-cream-terbinafine-1]]; neither is labelled for nails.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined: these are the brand's own consumer-site lines, not findings, and nothing above rests on them.

**The consumer site describes a wider indication than the label does.** jubliarx.com says Jublia "is a prescription medicine used to treat fungal infections of the toenails".[^brand] The prescribing label says onychomycosis of the toenails "due to Trichophyton rubrum and Trichophyton mentagrophytes".[^label] Dropping the two organisms turns a specific indication into a general one, and nail disease has causes those two names do not cover.

"Toenail fungus. It's a contagious infection that won't go away on its own."[^brand] The second half is a claim about natural history offered without a source, on a page selling the treatment.

"Don't Fear the Fungus", beside an image captioned "Not an actual patient".[^brand] Standard pharmaceutical consumer framing, noted because the caption is the part worth reading.

The site also routes visitors to a third-party telemedicine service, disclosing in its own words that the provider "charges a fee for its telemedicine services and does not accept insurance" and that Bausch "does not endorse or recommend" it.[^brand] A prescription funnel is not evidence about the drug.
