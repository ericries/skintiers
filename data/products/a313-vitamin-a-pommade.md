A313 Vitamin A Pommade is a French pharmacy retinoid ointment/cream built around retinyl palmitate, a [[retinyl-esters|retinyl ester]], made by Pharma Développement and sold as a 50 g tube marketed for anti-aging, wrinkles, and acne breakouts.[^1]

> A long-running French pharmacy cult product whose active is the weakest tier of over-the-counter retinoid, not the [[retinol]] or prescription [[tretinoin]] its "vitamin A" branding evokes. Retinyl esters like this one's retinyl palmitate must be converted by the skin to retinol, then to retinoic acid, before doing anything, and the class's own evidence page shows the one human trial of a comparable ester (retinyl propionate) found no benefit over placebo; retinyl palmitate's supportive data come from a mouse study, not a human trial.[^2] No independent test of this exact product was found.[^1][^2]

## Summary

A313 Vitamin A Pommade is a French pharmacy staple whose short ingredient list is built around [[retinyl-esters|retinyl palmitate]], the most common OTC retinoid ester.[^1] It is manufactured by Pharma Développement and, per current retailer listings, sold in a 50 g tube; retailer copy notes the texture has been reformulated from the original ointment ("pommade") to what one listing calls "an advanced cream instead of the previous pomade texture," so buyers should confirm the current texture before assuming the classic ointment feel.[^1]

Retinyl esters sit at the bottom of the retinoid potency ladder: they must be hydrolyzed to [[retinol]] and then oxidized to retinoic acid before they have any effect, and the general case for why that makes them weaker than retinol or [[tretinoin]] is set out in full on [[retinyl-esters]]. That class-level case, not a product-specific trial, underlies the grades below: the supportive anti-aging data point for retinyl palmitate comes from a UVB-irradiated mouse study, and the one human trial of a comparable ester, retinyl propionate 0.15%, "did not demonstrate any statistically significant improvement over placebo."[^2]

The retailer's marketing claims that the product "helps Anti-aging, helps reduce wrinkles" and "helps Prevent acne breakouts" are weighed against that evidence under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^1] It was listed at $39.40 for a 50 g tube (marked down from $64) on FrenchPharmacy.com, the seller that identifies itself as A313's official US brand owner, as of 2026-08-16 (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy)).[^1]

## The Evidence

Retinyl esters, including this product's retinyl palmitate, are the mildest class of over-the-counter retinoid: vitamin A alcohol joined to a fatty acid that the skin must cleave back to [[retinol]] before any retinoid activity begins. The full case, its mechanism, and its limits are set out on [[retinyl-esters]]; the grades above rest on that general evidence, since no published trial tests A313's retinyl palmitate formula itself.[^2]

The retinyl-ester evidence page reports that the supportive anti-wrinkle data for retinyl palmitate specifically come from a mouse model of UVB photoaging, not a human trial. The closest human evidence is for a related ester, retinyl propionate, where the only clinical trial found "topical retinyl propionate cream (0.15%) did not demonstrate any statistically significant improvement over placebo in any of the evaluated histopathological or clinical symptoms of photoaging."[^2] Because an ester must first convert to retinol and then to retinoic acid, it also shows "decreased effectiveness of anti-wrinkle properties... as compared to retinol and retinoic acid."[^2] For acne, no trial of retinyl palmitate or any retinyl ester was identified at all; the retailer's "helps Prevent acne breakouts" claim has no supporting citation on the class page or elsewhere, which is why that use is graded no effect rather than minimal.[^2]

> **Bottom line.** No trial isolates this product. Its active, retinyl palmitate, sits at the weakest tier of OTC retinoids, with its own supportive anti-aging data limited to a mouse study and the closest human trial of a comparable ester finding no benefit over placebo.[^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by the product-specific evidence above:

- **"Helps Anti-aging, helps reduce wrinkles."** A retailer-listing claim resting on the class-level case only; see [The Evidence](#the-evidence) for why the closest human trial found no benefit over placebo.
- **"Helps Prevent acne breakouts."** A retailer-listing claim with no supporting trial at all, per [The Evidence](#the-evidence).
- **"A pharmaceutical-grade retinol cream trusted by skincare professionals across Europe."** Positioning language from distributor marketing, not a cited clinical claim; the distributor does not claim the product is dermatologist-trusted or -tested.[^3]
- **The "200,000 UI" (IU) figure on the retailer listing.** This is a potency unit historically used for the ointment formulation; it is not directly comparable to a percent-by-weight concentration and no trial ties this specific IU figure to a measured clinical outcome.[^1]
