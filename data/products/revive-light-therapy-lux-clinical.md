The Lux Clinical is a $199 cordless LED device sold for both acne and wrinkles, and unlike the other LED Technologies products covered here its brand copy is actually readable. **Bottom line:** the specs are specific and plausible, the modality behind the acne setting is the best-graded one in home devices, and the brand's own storefront files it under a category heading that says something FDA did not do. The modality evidence is on [[home-led-light-therapy]].

> **The brand's own collection page is titled "FDA Approved LED Light Therapy Devices".**[^fdacleared] The URL of that same page ends in `/collections/fda-cleared` and the navigation label reads "FDA Cleared", so the brand clearly knows the right word and uses it elsewhere, including on this product where the spec list says "FDA-cleared acne treatment".[^brand] Approval and clearance are different things, and the difference is the whole subject of [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]]. No device in this family has ever been FDA approved.

## Summary

Priced at $199.00, discounted to $159.20 when this page was written.[^brand] The brand describes it as emitting "Red, infrared, amber, and blue light" with a "Primary wavelength range: 415-880 nm" and "Three treatment settings: Anti-Aging, Acne, and Acne + Anti-Aging", used in "3-minute daily treatments", and calls it an "OTC Class II medical device" and an "FDA-cleared acne treatment".[^brand] Instructions are to hold it still against the skin: "Do not move or wave the device."[^brand] A photograph from the brand's own product page is above.

## The Rubric

**Clearing mild to moderate acne**
- **Effect size: modest.** The brand claims it "Helps clear mild-to-moderate acne".[^brand] A device whose stated range begins at 415nm and which also emits red is delivering the blue plus red combination that earned the best grade any home acne device received in the 2026 review: "blue light-emitting diodes (LED) and combination blue-red LED devices demonstrated moderate quality evidence (Level B) with favorable safety profiles and Grade I recommendations".[^grade]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, and thinner here than on the sibling pages.** That grade is for a modality, from a review naming no manufacturer or model.[^grade] For the dpl and reVive units with clearances, there is at least a public 510(k) Summary to read, and each one states its conclusions are nonclinical. For this device there is no file at all, because no clearance carries this name.[^openfda]

**Improving fine lines and wrinkles**
- **Effect size: minimal.** The brand claims it "Improves the appearance of fine lines and wrinkles" and "Supports collagen and elastin production".[^brand] LED reached Level B for facial rejuvenation in the same review, behind home fractional nonablative lasers at Level A.[^grade] The collagen and elastin claim is a mechanism rather than a visible outcome, and nothing consulted here measured it on this device.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Same reason as above, with no device-level data available.

**The "clinically tested" claim, read precisely**
- **Effect size: not applicable; a wording finding.** The product description says it "delivers clinically tested, multi-wavelength LED light therapy".[^brand] Read where the claim attaches: to LED light therapy, which has indeed been tested, and not to the Lux Clinical. That is the same construction documented on [[revive-light-therapy-lux-spot]], and as written it is defensible.
- **Evidence quality: not applicable.** It is a claim about a field, not a result from this product.

## The Evidence

**No clearance bears this name, and the brand cites no number.** A query of every LED Technologies 510(k) returns 15 records and not one is called Lux Clinical or Lux anything.[^openfda] The spec list calls the device an "FDA-cleared acne treatment" and an "OTC Class II medical device" without naming a K number.[^brand] Its described profile of red, amber, infrared and blue across an acne setting and an anti-aging setting corresponds closely to the two indications cleared under [[revive-light-therapy-wrinkle-and-acne-led-device]], but correspondence is not identity, and nothing available here establishes that the unit on sale is that cleared device under a retail name. This page does not make that inference, for the same reason [[revive-light-therapy-lux-spot]] declines it.

**What the wavelength range does and does not tell you.** A "Primary wavelength range: 415-880 nm" is a span, not a specification.[^brand] It is consistent with the four colours named, and it still does not say how much light reaches skin at any of them. Irradiance is the quantity that decides whether an LED device does anything, and the brand supplies none, exactly as this firm's clearance files supply none, discussed on [[dpl-faceware]]. The gap is visible to buyers: the only mention of irradiance anywhere on the product page is a customer review guessing at it, "maybe at least 50mW/cm2".[^brand]

**The under-eye claim is worth noticing.** The brand says a "Triangular design reaches the under-eye and nose areas".[^brand] Treating close to the eye raises a different safety question from treating a cheek, and no eye-protection instruction appears in the copy quoted here. A customer review on the same page reports buying goggles separately because none were included.[^brand] The brand does sell eye protection separately, listing "Relaxation Goggles Light for Eye Protection" at a regular price of $5.00.[^storefront]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. The brand positions this for mild to moderate acne; moderate to severe or scarring acne warrants a dermatologist rather than a light, and the ladder is on [[acne]]. For any LED device used near the eyes, follow the manufacturer's instructions on eye protection.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined from the graded assessment above. Each is the brand's own wording from its product page, cached 2026-10-21.[^brand]

- "Helps clear mild-to-moderate acne" and "Improves the appearance of fine lines and wrinkles". These restate the two uses graded above.
- "Supports collagen and elastin production" and "Supports firmer-, healthier-looking skin". Mechanism and appearance language, not measured outcomes.
- "FDA-cleared acne treatment" and "OTC Class II medical device". Both plausible for this category, neither tied to a K number the buyer can look up.
- "clinically tested, multi-wavelength LED light therapy". The claim attaches to the therapy, not the device.
- The collection heading "FDA Approved LED Light Therapy Devices", which is the one claim here that is wrong rather than merely narrow.[^fdacleared]
