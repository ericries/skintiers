Differin Gel is an over-the-counter acne treatment whose only active ingredient is adapalene 0.1%, a topical retinoid, sold by Galderma in a fragrance-free aqueous gel.[^label]

> A real acne drug you can buy over the counter. Its active, adapalene 0.1%, treats acne, a medical skin condition, and in 2016 it became the first topical retinoid the FDA cleared for nonprescription sale, at the same strength and once-daily dose as the prescription Differin approved in 1996.[^review][^letter] Dermatologists quoted in Allure and NBC Select rate topical retinoids as a first-line acne treatment and single this one out. The recurring caveat is early irritation, and any anti-aging use is off-label and class-level. It is inexpensive, about $15 a tube.[^allure][^nbc]

## Summary

Differin Gel is a genuine acne medicine sold without a prescription. Its active, [[adapalene]] 0.1%, is a topical [[retinoids|retinoid]] that treats acne, a medical skin condition; Joshua Zeichner of Mount Sinai told Allure that retinoids "help reduce inflammation in the skin and prevent cells from sticking together within the follicle, which then blocks the pores."[^allure] What makes it unusual on a drugstore shelf is regulatory, not cosmetic: in 2016 it became the first topical retinoid the FDA cleared for over-the-counter sale, and it is the same molecule at the same strength as the prescription Differin approved in 1996, [carried over from that prescription record rather than retested](#the-evidence).[^review][^letter] It is a real FDA-approved acne drug, not a cosmetic product borrowing the word "retinol."

Dermatologists treat topical retinoids as a first-line acne option. Zeichner told Allure that "topical retinoids are prescribed for almost all patients in the office," and the board-certified dermatologist Mona Gohara, quoted by NBC Select, calls retinoids like adapalene "the gold standard" for "treating acne for all ages."[^allure][^nbc] Allure, which gave Differin Gel a Best of Beauty award, calls it "one of the best over-the-counter treatments for acne," and NBC Select names it the best overall drugstore acne treatment for teens, with board-certified dermatologist Samer Jaber recommending people "start using Differin gel at bedtime."[^allure][^nbc]

How it is used is set by the manufacturer's OTC Drug Facts label, which directs once-daily application of a thin layer to the affected area and states that results may take up to three months.[^label] Allure's reviewer likewise describes twelve weeks as "the recommended amount of time to see true results."[^allure]

What it is good at is acne, its only FDA-approved use, where its case rests on the regulatory record and on the wider retinoid evidence set out below and on [[adapalene]]. Used off-label for anti-aging it is an over-the-counter retinoid, milder than prescription [[tretinoin]], and that use rests on the broader evidence for topical retinoids, strongest for prescription tretinoin in photoaging, which is set out on [[retinoids]].[^review] The common caveat is early irritation: the label warns that redness, itching, dryness and burning are "more likely to occur" in the first few weeks, and Allure's reviewer reported "mild peeling during the first couple of weeks of use."[^label][^allure] It is inexpensive, about $15 for a 15 g tube (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28; NBC Select lists it between about $13 and $19 across retailers).[^nbc]

## The Evidence

Topical adapalene is an FDA-approved acne retinoid that, in head-to-head trials, works about as well as [[benzoyl-peroxide]] and [[tretinoin]] while being better tolerated than tretinoin; that trial-level case is set out on [[adapalene]], and where adapalene sits in the retinoid family on [[retinoids]]. The facts specific to this product are regulatory. Differin Gel reached shelves through a prescription-to-OTC switch, not a fresh efficacy program. The FDA review states that "the efficacy of adapalene gel 0.1% for the treatment of acne has been established in the prescription setting," that "it is expected that the product would have similar efficacy in the OTC setting, as the acne indication is the same in the prescription and OTC settings," and that "the proposed OTC dosing is the same as the approved prescription dosing."[^review]

> **Bottom line.** The over-the-counter gel is the same 0.1% adapalene at the same once-daily dose as the former prescription Differin, so its acne benefit is the prescription product's benefit, inherited rather than measured again.[^review]

The switch made adapalene the first topical retinoid sold without a prescription. The FDA benefit-risk summary describes the approval as "a first in class switch for a topical retinoid product and the first new active ingredient for the treatment of acne available OTC for more than 40 years."[^review] Because efficacy carried over from the prescription record, the application turned on whether people can use the gel safely on their own. It was supported by a maximal-use pharmacokinetic trial and by "three consumer studies": a label comprehension study, a self-selection study focusing on pregnant and lactating women, and an actual use study.[^review] The approval letter names Galderma's "Juno trial, your actual use study that evaluated consumer behavior," and records that the July 8, 2016 supplement "provides for the over-the-counter use of Differin Gel (adapalene), 0.1%, for the treatment of acne in adults and children ages 12 years and older."[^letter][^daf]

> **Bottom line.** The OTC approval rested on studies of whether consumers can select and apply the gel correctly without a prescriber, not on new efficacy trials. The acne benefit is the one already established for the prescription drug.[^review][^letter]

The only use the FDA approved for this product is acne. Its approved indication reads "for the treatment of acne," and when Galderma proposed a reworded OTC acne claim, the agency found "there were no new efficacy data submitted as part of this application," so that wording "was not supported and was withdrawn."[^review][^label] Using adapalene for photoaging is off-label, and the retinoid photoaging evidence is class-level, on [[retinoids]] and [[adapalene]]; as an over-the-counter retinoid it is milder than prescription tretinoin ([[tretinoin-0-025-cream]]).

> **Bottom line.** Differin's approved use is acne. Any anti-aging use is off-label and rests on class-level retinoid evidence, not on a trial of this product.[^review]

## What's In It

The active and full inactive list as declared on the manufacturer's OTC Drug Facts label.[^label] The active with graded efficacy links to its own pages; the rest are the gel base and preservative system.

Active:

- [[adapalene]] 0.1%, a topical [[retinoids|retinoid]], the only ingredient with graded efficacy. The label lists it as "Adapalene 0.1% (retinoid)" with the purpose "acne treatment."[^label]

Base and preservative system: carbomer 940 (gelling agent), edetate disodium (chelator), methylparaben (preservative), poloxamer 182 (surfactant), propylene glycol (humectant and solvent), purified water, and sodium hydroxide (pH adjuster), with hydrochloric acid to adjust pH.[^label]

The gel's only active is adapalene. It contains no benzoyl peroxide and no added fragrance, unlike adapalene-plus-benzoyl-peroxide combination gels.[^label]

## Common Marketing Claims

Manufacturer claims not established by the product-specific evidence above:[^differin]

- **"Rx-strength acne retinoid treatment"** and **"previously prescription only but now available over the counter."** Accurate as a regulatory description: the OTC gel is the same 0.1% adapalene at the same strength as the former prescription Differin.[^differin][^review]
- **"Treat & help prevent acne with the clinically proven Differin Adapalene Gel."** Positioning for the approved acne use. Adapalene's acne efficacy was established in the prescription setting, per the FDA review.[^differin][^review]
- **"A multi-benefit, dermatologist-recommended retinoid."** "Multi-benefit" gestures beyond acne; the product is FDA-approved only for acne, and any anti-aging use is off-label and class-level (see [[retinoids]]).[^differin][^review]
- **A reworded acne claim.** When Galderma proposed "clears up acne pimples and acne blemishes," the FDA found no new efficacy data supported it and it was withdrawn; the approved indication is simply "for the treatment of acne."[^review][^label]
