Sorilux Foam is a prescription-only topical drug made by Mayne Pharma, a 0.005% calcipotriene foam applied twice daily to treat plaque psoriasis of the scalp and body in adults and children 4 years of age and older.[^dailymed] Calcipotriene is a synthetic vitamin D3 analog, and the label states plainly that the exact mechanism behind its effect on psoriasis is unknown even though it has a receptor-binding affinity similar to natural vitamin D3.[^dailymed]

> A genuine first-line psoriasis drug, not a cosmetic scale-softener, but its own registration trials showed a modest absolute response: on the body, 14% and 27% of foam-treated subjects reached treatment success across two trials, against 7% and 16% on inert vehicle, and on the scalp 41% reached success against 24% on vehicle.[^dailymed] That is a real, statistically meaningful edge over vehicle, not a high responder rate in absolute terms, and in one trial subjects with only "mild" baseline disease actually did worse on the foam than on vehicle.[^dailymed] The most common side effects, application-site redness (2%) and pain (3%), are reported at 1% or higher and above vehicle, and the foam's propellant is flammable.[^dailymed]

## Summary

Sorilux is the brand name for a 0.005% calcipotriene foam, one of the FDA-labeled first-line topical drug classes for plaque psoriasis alongside topical corticosteroids and the newer nonsteroidals roflumilast and tapinarof; the full ranking of psoriasis treatments is on [[psoriasis]].[^dailymed] Calcipotriene itself carries an initial U.S. approval date of 1993, and Sorilux is a specific foam vehicle formulation of that drug rather than a new molecule.[^dailymed] This is a health-claim product only, there is no cosmetic angle: the label is explicit that psoriasis is the sole indication, applied twice daily, avoiding the face and eyes.[^dailymed]

The foam delivers 50 mcg of calcipotriene per gram of an aqueous-based emulsion, dispensed from a pressurized aluminum can with a flammable hydrocarbon propellant (propane, n-butane, isobutane).[^dailymed] Systemic absorption is minimal: in a pharmacokinetic comparison, 15 of 16 subjects using the foam had plasma calcipotriene below the limit of quantitation, versus 5 of 16 on calcipotriene ointment, and pediatric pharmacokinetic studies likewise found no quantifiable plasma levels.[^dailymed] The label's one serious warning is for calcium metabolism: elevated serum calcium has occurred with calcipotriene use, so the drug is contraindicated in patients with known hypercalcemia and should be stopped if calcium rises out of range, though dedicated pediatric calcium-metabolism substudies found no significant effect on calcium indices.[^dailymed]

## The Evidence

Sorilux's efficacy rests on four vehicle-controlled trials described in its own FDA label, not on a separate literature review; the general case for vitamin D analogs as a first-line topical psoriasis class is made on [[psoriasis]].[^dailymed]

**Body trials.** Two multicenter, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trials randomized a combined 659 subjects with mild-to-moderate plaque psoriasis 2:1 to Sorilux Foam or vehicle, applied twice daily for 8 weeks; success was defined as reaching "clear" or "almost clear" on a 5-point Investigator Static Global Assessment with at least a 2-grade improvement from baseline.[^dailymed] In Trial 1, 31 of 223 foam-treated subjects (14%) reached success versus 8 of 113 (7%) on vehicle. In Trial 2, 58 of 214 (27%) reached success versus 17 of 109 (16%) on vehicle.[^dailymed] Breaking those results down by baseline severity, subjects who started with only "mild" disease did notably worse on the foam than moderate-baseline subjects, and in Trial 1 the mild-baseline foam group (2.7%) actually underperformed the mild-baseline vehicle group (8.8%); moderate-baseline subjects showed the larger, more consistent advantage (19.3% vs 6.3% in Trial 1; 31.6% vs 16.7% in Trial 2).[^dailymed]

> Bottom line: against inert vehicle, Sorilux roughly doubled the treatment-success rate for body plaques in subjects starting with moderate disease, but the absolute numbers stayed under a third of patients, and it did not clearly outperform vehicle in subjects with only mild disease.[^dailymed]

**Scalp trial.** A separate multicenter, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial randomized 363 subjects with moderate scalp and body plaque psoriasis 1:1 to Sorilux Foam or vehicle, applied twice daily for 8 weeks; success on the scalp was defined as reaching "clear" or "almost clear" on a 6-point scale.[^dailymed] At Week 8, 74 of 181 foam-treated subjects (41%) reached success versus 44 of 182 (24%) on vehicle.[^dailymed]

> Bottom line: on the scalp, Sorilux more than doubled the vehicle response and reached the largest absolute success rate of the four registration trials, 41%, still leaving most treated patients short of the trial's clear-or-almost-clear bar at 8 weeks.[^dailymed]

The label adds one caveat that applies across all four trials: "the contribution to efficacy of individual components of the vehicle has not been established," so some share of the foam's effect versus an inactive vehicle cannot be cleanly separated to calcipotriene alone by these trials.[^dailymed]

## What's In It

- **Active: calcipotriene 0.005%** (50 mcg per gram), a synthetic vitamin D3 analog. The label states the exact mechanism of its psoriasis efficacy is unknown, despite calcipotriene's receptor-binding affinity being similar to natural vitamin D3.[^dailymed]
- **Vehicle:** cetyl alcohol, dibasic sodium phosphate, dl-alpha-tocopherol, edetate disodium, isopropyl myristate, light mineral oil, polyoxyl 20 cetostearyl ether, propylene glycol, purified water, stearyl alcohol, and white petrolatum, as declared on the FDA label, dispensed with a propane/n-butane/isobutane propellant.[^dailymed]

Contraindicated in patients with known hypercalcemia. Not for oral, ophthalmic, or intravaginal use.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Vitamin D analog" framing implying a gentle, natural-sounding treatment.** The label does classify calcipotriene as a vitamin D3 analog, but it is a prescription drug with a defined contraindication (hypercalcemia) and a monitored side effect (serum calcium elevation), not an over-the-counter vitamin supplement.[^dailymed]
- **Twice-daily foam positioned as a convenient scalp-and-body option.** The label does support both scalp and body use in one product for adults and children 4 and older, and the scalp trial showed the largest absolute success rate of the four registration trials (41% vs 24% on vehicle); the body trials showed a smaller and, in mild disease, inconsistent advantage over vehicle.[^dailymed]
