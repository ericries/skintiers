Tinactin Antifungal Cream is Bayer's 1% tolnaftate cream, an over-the-counter antifungal for athlete's foot and body ringworm. Its purpose on the label is "Antifungal", and its stated uses are that it is "proven clinically effective in the treatment of most athlete's foot (tinea pedis) and ringworm (tinea corporis)".[^dailymed] The active's full evidence case is on [[tolnaftate]].

> **It is the only OTC antifungal here whose label is allowed to claim prevention.** The Uses section includes "helps prevent most athlete's foot with daily use", and the Directions give it a separate, lighter schedule: "to prevent athlete's foot, apply once or twice daily (morning and/or night)".[^dailymed] [[clotrimazole]] has no equivalent claim. What no source cited here provides is a prevention RATE, because the trial evidence behind tolnaftate concerns treatment rather than prophylaxis, which is set out on [[tolnaftate]].

## Summary

Tolnaftate 1% in a cream base, marketed by Bayer HealthCare under monograph citation M005 with a marketing start date of 23 September 1993.[^dailymed] It treats athlete's foot and ringworm on a four-week course, claims to help prevent athlete's foot on a lighter one, and states plainly that it "is not effective on the scalp or nails".[^dailymed]

## The Rubric

**Treating athlete's foot and body ringworm**
- **Effect size: notable, and the label says "proven clinically effective".** That is regulated Drug Facts wording rather than marketing copy.[^dailymed] The randomised evidence behind the active, and the important caveat about how its Cochrane figure cannot be compared across drug classes, are on [[tolnaftate]].
- **Evidence quality: monograph-grade for this product, solid at class level.** The marketing category is OTC Monograph Drug M005, so the FDA accepted the active without requiring trials of this cream.[^dailymed] Contrast [[butenafine]], sold in the same aisle under an approved NDA.

**Preventing athlete's foot**
- **Effect size: not established by any source cited here.** The claim and its dedicated dosing schedule are both on the label.[^dailymed] A monograph permission is a real regulatory fact, not marketing invention, and it is still not a measured prevention rate.
- **Evidence quality: monograph-permitted, unquantified.**

**Scalp or nails**
- **Effect size: none, by the manufacturer's own statement.** "this product is not effective on the scalp or nails"; "Do not use on children under 2 years of age except under the advice and supervision of a doctor."; "For external use only"; "avoid contact with the eyes".[^dailymed]
- **Evidence quality: not applicable; an explicit exclusion rather than an untested use.**

## The Evidence

**What the label directs, and the four-week decision point.** Wash and dry the area, "apply a thin layer over affected area twice daily (morning and night)", and "use daily for 4 weeks".[^dailymed] Four weeks is both the course and the stopping rule, since the label tells you to stop and ask a doctor if there is "no improvement within 4 weeks".[^dailymed] Prevention uses the lighter schedule quoted above, which is the only place the two purposes diverge in practice.

**Where this product sits against its shelf-mates.** Three OTC antifungals on this site occupy three different regulatory positions. This one is an OTC Monograph Drug under M005, so no trials of this cream were required.[^dailymed] [[clotrimazole]] is also M005. [[butenafine]] is approved under an NDA, which required FDA review of evidence for that drug. All three sit in the same pharmacy aisle, and the ladder is on [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

**Safety is short.** "For external use only", "avoid contact with the eyes", and "Do not use on children under 2 years of age except under the advice and supervision of a doctor."[^dailymed] The only listed reason to stop early is irritation.[^dailymed]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. A rash that is not actually tinea can look very like it, and treating the wrong thing with an antifungal wastes four weeks.

## What's In It

Tolnaftate 1% as the active.[^dailymed] Inactive ingredients as declared: "ceteth-20, cetostearyl alcohol, chlorocresol, mineral oil, propylene glycol, purified water, sodium phosphate monobasic, white petrolatum".[^dailymed] A conventional cream base; chlorocresol is the preservative and is a recognised contact allergen, which matters only if an apparent treatment failure is actually a reaction.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"proven clinically effective in the treatment of most athlete's foot (tinea pedis) and ringworm (tinea corporis)"**. Regulated Drug Facts wording permitted under monograph M005.[^dailymed] The hedge in "most" is the label's own.
- **"helps prevent most athlete's foot with daily use"**. The distinguishing claim, permitted by the monograph, and unquantified by anything cited here.[^dailymed]
- **"for effective relief of itching, burning and cracking"**. A symptom claim rather than a cure claim.[^dailymed]

## Where to Buy

**No price is stated because none was verified.** Tinactin is a mass-market pharmacy product; the label itself carries no price, and no retailer figure was confirmed. The package sizes recorded in the SPL are a 15 g tube (NDC 11523-1190-1) and a 30 g tube (NDC 11523-1190-2).[^dailymed]
