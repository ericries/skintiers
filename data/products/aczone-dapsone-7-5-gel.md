Aczone is prescription dapsone 7.5% topical gel, distributed by Almirall, LLC (the brand was developed under Allergan) and FDA-approved as follows: "ACZONE (dapsone) Gel, 7.5%, is indicated for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris in patients 9 years of age and older."[^dailymed] This page covers the once-daily 7.5% strength; an older 5% Aczone gel, applied twice daily, is a separate product with its own label.[^dailymed-5]

> A genuine third path for topical acne treatment: dapsone is a sulfone, so Aczone is neither an antibiotic like [[clindamycin-topical|clindamycin]] nor a retinoid like [[differin-gel-adapalene|adapalene]], which makes it a real option for patients who cannot tolerate those classes, and a pooled analysis suggesting women respond better than men is covered on [[dapsone-topical]]. But be clear-eyed about the size of the win: in both of its phase 3 trials, 30% of Aczone patients reached clear-or-almost-clear versus 21% on the inert vehicle gel, and the added inflammatory-lesion reduction over vehicle was fewer than 2 lesions. A useful niche drug, not a first-line powerhouse. The broader condition is [[acne]].

## Summary

Aczone Gel, 7.5% contains 75 mg of [[dapsone-topical|dapsone]] per gram in an aqueous gel, dispensed from an airless pump as an off-white to yellow gel with suspended particles.[^dailymed] Dosing is once daily: "After the skin is gently washed and patted dry, apply approximately a pea-sized amount of ACZONE Gel, 7.5%, in a thin layer to the entire face once daily," with a thin layer permitted on other affected areas; the label adds that "if there is no improvement after 12 weeks, treatment with ACZONE Gel, 7.5% should be reassessed."[^dailymed] The indication, quoted above, carries an age floor of 9 years; the registration trials enrolled subjects 12 and older, and use down to age 9 is supported by an open-label pharmacokinetic and safety study of 100 subjects age 9 to 11.[^dailymed] The general dapsone story, mechanism, the 5% versus 7.5% programs, and the gender-response signal are owned by [[dapsone-topical]] and not re-derived here.

The product-specific case rests on two identically designed vehicle-controlled phase 3 trials, detailed under [The Evidence](#the-evidence). The short version: Aczone beat vehicle on every co-primary endpoint in both trials, but the absolute margins were narrow, a 9-percentage-point gap on Global Acne Assessment Score success (30% versus 21% in both trials) and fewer than 2 extra inflammatory lesions cleared versus vehicle.[^dailymed]

Because oral dapsone can cause hemolysis, especially in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, the label carries hematologic warnings even though topical systemic exposure is low, about 1% of a 100 mg oral dose by the label's own pharmacokinetic estimate.[^dailymed] The most common adverse reactions were application site dryness (1.1% versus 1.0% on vehicle) and pruritus (0.9% versus 0.5%).[^dailymed] It is prescription-only; see a prescriber for use and dosing, and see [[acne]] for the broader condition context. Generic dapsone gel exists in both the older 5% strength and, more recently, the 7.5% strength.[^dailymed-generic]

## The Evidence

The general evidence base for dapsone as an acne active, including the pooled analyses and the original 5% registration trials, is covered on [[dapsone-topical]]; this section covers only what the Aczone 7.5% label itself reports.[^dailymed]

The label describes two 12-week multicenter, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trials assessing a combined 4,340 subjects 12 years of age and older, the majority with moderate acne vulgaris (20 to 50 inflammatory and 30 to 100 non-inflammatory lesions at baseline).[^dailymed] Treatment response was defined as the proportion of subjects rated "none" or "minimal" with at least a two-grade improvement from baseline on the Global Acne Assessment Score (GAAS), plus mean absolute change from baseline in inflammatory and non-inflammatory lesion counts.[^dailymed]

Results at Week 12, from the label's Table 2:[^dailymed]

| | Trial 1 (N=1044 vs 1058) | Trial 2 (N=1118 vs 1120) |
|---|---|---|
| GAAS success (score 0 or 1), Aczone vs vehicle[^dailymed] | 30% vs 21% | 30% vs 21% |
| Inflammatory lesions, mean absolute reduction | 16.1 vs 14.3 | 15.6 vs 14.0 |
| Inflammatory lesions, mean percent reduction | 56% vs 49% | 54% vs 48% |
| Non-inflammatory lesions, mean absolute reduction | 20.7 vs 18.0 | 20.8 vs 18.7 |
| Non-inflammatory lesions, mean percent reduction | 45% vs 39% | 46% vs 41% |

Read those numbers for what they are. The consistency across two large trials is a strength, and every endpoint favored Aczone. But the vehicle gel alone cleared 14.3 and 14.0 inflammatory lesions and got 21% of subjects to GAAS success; Aczone's added benefit over that was 1.8 and 1.6 inflammatory lesions and 9 percentage points of GAAS success.[^dailymed] Most of what a patient experiences on Aczone in these trials would also have happened on the drug-free vehicle.

> **Bottom line.** Two large, consistent phase 3 trials show once-daily Aczone 7.5% reliably beats vehicle, and its non-antibiotic, non-retinoid mechanism earns it a real niche. The absolute effect is modest: a 9-point edge in reaching clear-or-almost-clear and under 2 extra inflammatory lesions cleared versus the vehicle gel.[^dailymed]

On the hematologic question: dapsone is a sulfone, and "oral dapsone treatment has produced dose-related hemolysis and hemolytic anemia," with G6PD-deficient individuals more prone.[^dailymed] For the topical route the label states: "In clinical trials, there was no evidence of clinically relevant hemolysis or hemolytic anemia in subjects treated with topical dapsone. Some subjects with G6PD deficiency using dapsone gel, 5%, twice daily developed laboratory changes suggestive of hemolysis."[^dailymed] A dedicated randomized crossover study of 64 G6PD-deficient subjects with acne (using the 5% gel) found laboratory changes suggestive of hemolysis in some subjects but "no evidence of clinically significant hemolytic anemia."[^dailymed] The label also reports postmarketing cases of methemoglobinemia with resultant hospitalization in association with twice-daily 5% dapsone gel, and says to avoid Aczone in patients with congenital or idiopathic methemoglobinemia, in patients taking oral dapsone or antimalarials, and to watch the trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole combination in G6PD deficiency.[^dailymed] Systemic exposure from the 7.5% gel is low: after 28 days of once-daily use over face, upper chest, upper back, and shoulders, mean Cmax was 13.0 +/- 6.8 ng/mL, which the label says "is expected to be about 1% of that from a 100 mg oral dose."[^dailymed]

Adverse reactions in the two trials (N=2,161 on Aczone) were sparse: application site dryness in 1.1% versus 1.0% on vehicle, and application site pruritus in 0.9% versus 0.5%.[^dailymed] The contraindications section reads "None."[^dailymed]

## What's In It

The active and inactive ingredients as declared on the FDA-approved prescribing information.[^dailymed]

Active:

- [[dapsone-topical|Dapsone]] 7.5% (75 mg per gram), a sulfone, in an aqueous gel.[^dailymed]

Inactive ingredients: diethylene glycol monoethyl ether, methylparaben, acrylamide/sodium acryloyldimethyl taurate copolymer, isohexadecane, polysorbate 80, and purified water.[^dailymed]

The label states "the mechanism of action of dapsone gel in treating acne vulgaris is not known."[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

No brand marketing page was reviewed for this product; the claims below come from the FDA label's own characterization rather than promotional copy.

- **Once-daily dosing.** Accurate for the 7.5% strength, and its practical advantage over the older twice-daily 5% gel.[^dailymed]
- **Effective for acne.** Supported versus vehicle in both phase 3 trials, but the absolute margin is modest (30% versus 21% GAAS success), and the label never compares Aczone to any active comparator, so nothing on the label shows it matches retinoids, benzoyl peroxide, or combination therapy.[^dailymed]

## Where to Buy

Prescription only; obtain through a dermatologist or prescriber. Aczone Gel, 7.5% is supplied in 30 g, 60 g, and 90 g airless pumps (NDC 16110-526-30, -60, -90).[^dailymed] No official list price appears on a durable primary source, and per house policy aggregator price-comparison sites are not cited, so no price is stated here. Generic dapsone gel is available: multiple manufacturers list the older twice-daily 5% strength on DailyMed, and at least one (Acrux DDS Pty Ltd) lists a generic once-daily 7.5% gel; generics generally cost less than the brand.[^dailymed-generic]
