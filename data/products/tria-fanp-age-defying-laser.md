A $700 home fractional laser whose cleared indication is one thing and whose marketing is several. **Bottom line:** Tria publishes more than most home-device makers, including an actual clinical study, and the study was open-label with no control and measured equivalence to Tria's own earlier device. Category evidence is on [[home-fractional-nonablative-laser]].

> **FDA cleared it for crow's feet, and nothing else.** The 510(k) indication reads in full: "The FANp is an over-the-counter device indicated for the treatment of periorbital wrinkles (crow's feet)."[^k141868] The brand's current storefront claims reduced pigmentation, improved skin tone, dark spots, new skin cells, collagen production and skin elasticity.[^brand] Those are not in the clearance.

## Summary

Cleared 2014-12-30 as K141868, applicant Tria Beauty, Inc., product code ONG, regulation 21 CFR 878.4810, a Traditional 510(k) published as a Summary.[^openfda][^k141868] FDA's classification for ONG is "Powered Laser Surgical Instrument With MicrobeamFractional Output", Class II.[^openfda] The device is described in its own Summary as "a semiconductor diode laser system that delivers infrared light at a wavelength of 1450 nm ± 50 nm", confirming the 1450 nm figure.[^k141868]

**Credit where it is due: Tria discloses well.** All five of its FDA clearances are published as Summaries rather than Statements, so the basis is readable in every case: the acne system K090312, hair removal K120737, the FAN System K130459, this device, and Tria Sapphire K160821.[^openfda] On the disclosure axis used at [[home-led-devices-by-what-the-file-publishes]], that is a clean record.

**What is sold today carries a different name.** Tria's storefront lists a "Tria FRX Skin Rejuvenating Fractional Laser (2nd Gen)" at $699.99, and a bundle with a gel at $693.99.[^brand] No Tria clearance is named FRX, and none is named Age-Defying Laser; the two fractional ONG clearances are the FAN System and this FANp.[^openfda] So this page does not assert that the FRX on sale is the device cleared here. Match a product's stated wavelength and indication rather than its retail name, the same problem documented at [[silkn-titan-allways]] and [[sciton-zoomscan]].

## The Rubric

**Periorbital wrinkles (crow's feet)**
Graded modest on preliminary evidence. This is the cleared use and the only one with a study behind it. The Summary reports "A 45-subject, prospective, open-label safety and effectiveness study was conducted to evaluate the Tria FANp for the treatment of periorbital wrinkles", and its conclusion is that the device "was found to be as safe and effective as the predicate non-ablative fractional device".[^k141868] Read the design: open-label, no control arm, no sham, and the endpoint is equivalence to another device rather than improvement over no treatment.

**Pigmentation, skin tone and dark spots**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. The brand's own copy claims "84% of users see reduced pigmentation and improvements in skin tone" and names "dark spots" among the concerns it treats.[^brand] The cleared indication is periorbital wrinkles only.[^k141868] What does work on pigment is on [[hyperpigmentation]].

**Collagen production and skin elasticity**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. The storefront states the device is "Scientifically proven to create new skin cells, accelerate natural collagen production and improve skin elasticity".[^brand] Read in full, the listing names no study, author, journal or sample size for any of those three outcomes.

## The Evidence

**The efficacy case is a ladder, and no rung is a controlled trial.** The FANp's study found it equivalent to its predicate, the Tria FAN System (K130459).[^k141868] That predicate's own Summary reports "A60-subject, prospective, open-label safety and effectiveness study" and concludes the FAN System "was found to be as safe and effective as the predicate non-ablative fractional" devices, which it lists as the Palomar LOI System, the Cynosure Luminage Diode Laser, the Cynosure Affirm Laser Family and the Solta Fraxel Re:Fine Laser.[^k130459] So: home device equivalent to earlier home device, equivalent to in-office clinic lasers. Both Tria studies were open-label, neither had a control arm, and the top of the ladder is a set of professional machines, not a placebo.

**Which makes one marketing line interesting.** The storefront says "Fractional laser technology mirrors clinical laser treatments".[^brand] In a narrow regulatory sense the predicate chain really does reach Fraxel and Affirm.[^k130459] Substantial equivalence is a legal finding about intended use and technology, not a measurement that a battery-powered handheld delivers what a clinic laser delivers. The distinction is set out on [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

**There is animal data, and it is about tissue response rather than wrinkles.** The Summary records "A GLP compliant histology study was conducted. Hairless guinea pigs were treated with the Tria FANp and tissue sampling occurred immediately, 5 days, and 14 days post-treatment. The tissue response was found to be equivalent to the predicate non-ablative fractional device."[^k141868] That supports the device doing something measurable to skin. It is an equivalence result again, in guinea pigs.

**The percentages have an asterisk and no visible footnote.** All three user-satisfaction figures, 89%, 84% and 80%, carry an asterisk in the brand's own listing, and the listing text available here contains no corresponding substantiation.[^brand] Read as what they are: reported user perception, not a clinical endpoint, from an unstated number of people under unstated conditions.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice. A 1450 nm laser is a real laser and periorbital skin is thin and close to the eye; the cleared indication is specifically the area around the eye, and eye protection and following the manufacturer's instructions matter more here than with a cream. Anyone with melasma or a pigmentary condition should note that heat and light can worsen pigment, which is the opposite of what the pigmentation claim implies.

## Common Marketing Claims

**"Get clinically proven skincare results with Tria FRX's patented fractional laser technology."** A study did happen, which is more than many competitors can say.[^k141868] It was open-label with no control and its endpoint was equivalence to Tria's previous model.[^brand][^k141868]

**"84% of users see reduced pigmentation and improvements in skin tone, 80% see a reduction in fine lines and wrinkles , and 89% see smoother skin ."** Quoted with the brand's own spacing. User-reported perception, asterisked, with no substantiation in the listing.[^brand]

**"Scientifically proven to create new skin cells, accelerate natural collagen production and improve skin elasticity."** Three mechanism claims, no citation, and none of them is the cleared indication.[^brand][^k141868]

**"The new Tria FRX is safer and longer lasting , featuring updated technology throughout".** A comparative safety claim against an unnamed earlier version.[^brand] Note that no clearance exists under the FRX name, so what regulatory record covers the updated technology cannot be established from the device index.[^openfda]
