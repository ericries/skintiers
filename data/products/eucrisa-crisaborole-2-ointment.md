Eucrisa is the brand name for prescription crisaborole 2% ointment, a nonsteroidal phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitor made by Pfizer, FDA-approved for the topical treatment of mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis "in adult and pediatric patients 3 months of age and older."[^dailymed]

> A genuine steroid-free option for eczema, not a dramatic drug. In the two registration trials behind approval, roughly a third of Eucrisa-treated patients cleared or nearly cleared by Day 29, versus roughly a fifth to a quarter on the inert vehicle ointment alone, a real but modest edge. The tradeoff for skipping a steroid is application-site burning or stinging, reported in 4% of Eucrisa patients versus 1% on vehicle. Its niche is sensitive or steroid-limited skin, not superior clearance; the underlying PDE4 mechanism is covered on [[crisaborole]] and the condition on [[atopic-dermatitis]].

## Summary

Eucrisa is generic-name [[crisaborole]], formulated as a 2% ointment (20 mg of crisaborole per gram) in a petrolatum-based base, applied twice daily to affected areas.[^dailymed] The label states it is "indicated for topical treatment of mild to moderate atopic dermatitis in adult and pediatric patients 3 months of age and older," an age floor that a March 2020 FDA supplemental approval ("Efficacy-New Patient Population") expanded from the drug's original December 2016 approval;[^fda-history] this page cites only the current, revised label.[^dailymed] Dosing is "a thin layer of EUCRISA twice daily to affected areas," and the label notes that "once clinical effect is achieved, consider reducing application to once daily."[^dailymed] It is for topical use only, not for ophthalmic, oral, or intravaginal use, and it is prescription-only.[^dailymed] The general PDE4-inhibitor mechanism and how crisaborole compares to other nonsteroidal options is owned by [[crisaborole]]; the broader eczema landscape, including where steroid-sparing drugs like this one fit against [[topical-corticosteroids]], is owned by [[atopic-dermatitis]].

The product-specific case rests on two identically designed registration trials, described in full under [The Evidence](#the-evidence): at Day 29, treatment success (an Investigator's Static Global Assessment, or ISGA, of clear or almost clear with at least a 2-grade improvement from baseline) was reached by 32.8% of Eucrisa patients versus 25.4% on vehicle in Trial 1, and 31.4% versus 18.0% on vehicle in Trial 2.[^dailymed] That is a real, statistically meaningful edge over an inert ointment in both trials, but the gap is narrow, especially in Trial 1, where roughly a quarter of vehicle-only patients also improved.

Tolerability is the other side of the ledger. In the pooled 4-week trial data, application-site pain, described in the label as "skin sensations such as burning or stinging," occurred in 4% (45 of 1,012) of Eucrisa-treated subjects versus 1% (6 of 499) on vehicle, and it is the drug's most common adverse reaction.[^dailymed] Less common reactions include contact urticaria, and allergic contact dermatitis has been identified in postmarketing use.[^dailymed] This is a prescription drug; see a prescriber for use and dosing, and see [[atopic-dermatitis]] for the broader condition context.

## The Evidence

The general case for crisaborole as a PDE4 inhibitor, and how it compares to other steroid-sparing drugs, is covered on [[crisaborole]] and [[atopic-dermatitis]]; this section covers only what the Eucrisa label itself reports for this product.

Two multicenter, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trials (the label's Trial 1 and Trial 2) treated 1,522 subjects age 2 to 79 with mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis on 5% to 95% treatable body surface area.[^dailymed] Subjects were randomized 2:1 to Eucrisa or vehicle, applied twice daily for 28 days, with the primary endpoint being the proportion reaching ISGA success (clear or almost clear with at least a 2-grade improvement from baseline) at Day 29.[^dailymed] Trial 1 enrolled 503 Eucrisa and 256 vehicle subjects and found success in 32.8% versus 25.4%; Trial 2 enrolled 513 Eucrisa and 250 vehicle subjects and found success in 31.4% versus 18.0%.[^dailymed]

A third trial (Trial 3) assessed once-daily Eucrisa over 52 weeks for maintenance. In an open-label period, 497 subjects age 3 months and older applied Eucrisa twice daily for up to 8 weeks; 254 who reached both ISGA success and at least 50% improvement in eczema severity (EASI50) were then randomized 1:1 to once-daily Eucrisa or vehicle for 52 weeks or until a flare.[^dailymed] The label reports the percentage of subjects maintaining clear-or-almost-clear status through Week 52 in a figure rather than a single summary statistic, and states that adverse reactions with once-daily maintenance dosing "were similar to vehicle."[^dailymed]

> **Bottom line.** In both registration trials, Eucrisa beat an inert vehicle ointment on a strict clearance threshold, a real effect, but most treated patients, including a meaningful share of the vehicle arm, did not reach that threshold at Day 29.[^dailymed]

## What's In It

The active and inactive ingredients as declared on the FDA-approved prescribing information.[^dailymed]

Active:

- [[crisaborole]] 2% (20 mg per gram), a prescription topical PDE4 inhibitor, in a petrolatum-based ointment.[^dailymed]

Inactive ingredients: white petrolatum, propylene glycol, mono- and di-glycerides, paraffin, butylated hydroxytoluene, and edetate calcium disodium.[^dailymed]

The label states Eucrisa is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to crisaborole or any component of the formulation, and warns that hypersensitivity reactions, including contact urticaria, have occurred; severe pruritus, swelling, or erythema at the application site or a distant site should prompt discontinuation.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"100% steroid-free."** Accurate as a formulation fact: crisaborole is a PDE4 inhibitor, not a corticosteroid, per Pfizer's own brand site.[^brand] It does not follow that it is free of side effects; application-site burning and stinging are common, and it carries its own hypersensitivity warning.[^dailymed]
- **"Can be used almost everywhere on almost everybody."** Pfizer's brand site frames this around the lack of steroid-related skin-thinning limits.[^brand] The label itself is narrower: it is for topical use only, not for ophthalmic, oral, or intravaginal use, and its efficacy evidence is specific to mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis, not a general-purpose claim.[^dailymed]
- **"Can be part of a long-term treatment plan."** The label's own long-term data support this only for once-daily maintenance dosing after an initial twice-daily response, tested in a single 52-week trial; it is not evidence that twice-daily use indefinitely was studied.[^dailymed][^brand]
