Innisfree No-Sebum Mineral Powder is an inexpensive Korean loose setting powder built on oil-absorbing silica and modified corn starch, sold with a puff to dab over makeup or bare skin so it soaks up surface oil and leaves a matte finish.[^1][^2]

> A cheap loose powder that absorbs the oil sitting on the skin's surface for a temporary matte look. That surface oil-absorption is a cosmetic effect: silica and starch powders take up oil and mattify skin (see [The Evidence](#the-evidence)). It does not lower how much sebum the skin's glands produce, so despite the "No-Sebum" name it is not a treatment for oily skin or acne.[^1][^3][^5]

## Summary

Innisfree No-Sebum Mineral Powder is a finely milled loose powder aimed at oily and combination skin. Its job is cosmetic: it soaks up the oil already sitting on the skin's surface and leaves a soft matte, translucent finish. That is a finish effect rather than a health one. Absorbing surface oil does not reduce the amount of sebum the sebaceous glands make, which is a hormone-driven process, so the powder does not treat oily skin or acne as a medical condition (the surface-versus-production distinction is set out in [The Evidence](#the-evidence)).[^3][^5]

On the category, oil-absorbing powders built on silica and starch are a well-characterized way to mattify skin: a cosmetic-science study of common powder ingredients found that silica, mica, kaolin, talc and starch take up oil and produce a measurable mattifying effect when applied to human skin ([see The Evidence](#the-evidence)).[^3] On the product, this is an unusually cheap example of that category. What separates it from a plainer translucent setting powder is mainly price and the bundled puff rather than a distinctive active; the ingredients doing the oil absorption, [[silica]] and modified corn starch, are the standard ones ([see What's In It](#whats-in-it)).[^2] The manufacturer's directions describe using it "at the last stage of makeup routine or any time your skin is oily."[^2]

It is inexpensive. The 5 g loose size is listed at about $5 to $9 at retailers, and the current 10 g version at $19 on the manufacturer's site ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28).[^1][^2] The "No-Sebum" name and the manufacturer's "absorbs excess sebum" description both refer to oil on the skin's surface; they are separated out under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^1]

## The Evidence

No published clinical trial tests this particular powder. What is established is the behavior of the oil-absorbing powder ingredients it is built on, and the difference between soaking up oil on the surface and changing how much oil the skin produces. Silica is a well-characterized oil-absorbing powder ingredient; its general profile sits on [[silica]].

Bamford 2021 characterized common powder cosmetic ingredients, comparing a new material against silica, mica, kaolin, talc and starch. The powder ingredients were, in the authors' words, "applied on human skin and analysed for short- and long-term mattifying effect," were run through "oil and moisture uptake tests," and are described as materials "currently used as fillers and absorbers in powder cosmetics."[^3]

> **Bottom line.** The silica and starch this powder is built on are established oil-absorbing powder ingredients that mattify skin. That is a surface, cosmetic effect.

Kircik 2013, a review of an oil-absorbing topical delivery system, reported that formulations built for oil absorption gave "a more significant reduction in facial shine" than a comparison product, along with a "reduction in facial sebum accumulation relative to control."[^4]

> **Bottom line.** Absorbing oil from the skin's surface reduces visible shine. It acts on the oil already on the skin, not on how much the glands make.

Sebum production is a separate, gland-level process. Pierard-Franchimont and Pierard 2002 used a Sebumeter to measure "the casual sebum level and the sebum excretion rate" on the forehead, tracking sebaceous output under endocrine control, and Maia Campos 2019 characterized oily skin as skin showing "excessive production of sebum" by the sebaceous glands.[^5][^7]

> **Bottom line.** How much sebum skin makes is set by the sebaceous glands and hormones, measured as a sebum excretion rate. Absorbing or blotting oil off the surface does not change that rate.

Topical products that do lower measured sebum production are a different category. Detudom 2023 tested creams containing l-carnitine and epigallocatechin gallate in 90 subjects over 4 weeks and found "mean sebum reduction from baseline was statistically significant in all treatment groups" (p<0.01).[^6] A mineral powder carries no such active; its effect is on oil at the surface.

## What's In It

The ingredient list as displayed on the retailer and manufacturer listings.[^1][^2] The oil-absorbing components are the functional part; the rest is the silicone base, finish, and preservative system.

Oil-absorbing powder ingredients:

- [[silica]], the main oil-absorbing powder ingredient
- modified corn starch (and, in some versions, aluminum starch octenylsuccinate), a starch-based oil absorber
- mica, for a soft-focus finish

Base, finish, and preservative system: dimethicone and related silicones, caprylic/capric triglyceride, mineral salts, glyceryl caprylate, butylene glycol, ethylhexylglycerin, 1,2-hexanediol, and phenoxyethanol, plus wild mint (Mentha arvensis) and green tea (Camellia sinensis) leaf extracts.[^2]

The exact ingredient list varies by region and version: some listings add aluminum starch octenylsuccinate, tocopherol (vitamin E), and fragrance, while others do not.[^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing terms not established as health effects by the evidence above:

- **"No-Sebum" name and "absorbs excess sebum."** The manufacturer describes the product as a "shine-control essential" that "absorbs excess sebum and leaves skin with a translucent finish."[^1] The powder takes up sebum already on the skin surface, a cosmetic effect. It does not reduce the sebaceous glands' sebum output (Pierard-Franchimont 2002).[^5]
- **"Soft-matte," "translucent," and "blurred" finish.** Cosmetic finish claims,[^1] consistent with the mattifying effect measured for oil-absorbing powder ingredients on human skin (Bamford 2021).[^3]
