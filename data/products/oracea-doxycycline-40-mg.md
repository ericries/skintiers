Oracea is a prescription-only oral capsule made by Galderma, containing 40 mg of doxycycline (30 mg in an immediate-release bead and 10 mg in a delayed-release bead) taken once daily for the inflammatory lesions of [[rosacea-papulopustular|papulopustular rosacea]].[^dailymed] It is a tetracycline-class drug, but the label is explicit that the dose is deliberately sub-antibacterial: the plasma concentrations it produces are, in the FDA's own words, "less than the concentration required to treat bacterial diseases," and the label separately instructs prescribers not to use it for infections.[^dailymed]

> An oral anti-inflammatory dose of a familiar antibiotic, not an antibiotic dose repurposed for skin. In the two registration 16-week trials, ORACEA roughly doubled mean lesion-count reduction versus placebo and raised the rate of clear-or-almost-clear skin to 1.6 to 2.3 times the placebo rate, a real but modest effect against an oral capsule's own side-effect profile: the label lists no boxed warning, but it carries the full class warnings for tetracyclines (photosensitivity, tooth discoloration and bone-growth inhibition in children and in utero, pseudotumor cerebri, C. difficile-associated diarrhea) that a topical rosacea drug does not.[^dailymed] It explicitly did not improve the visible redness of rosacea in trials, so it does not substitute for a redness-targeted treatment.[^dailymed] The mechanism, full cross-formulation evidence base, and resistance rationale for this sub-antimicrobial dosing strategy are covered on [[doxycycline-oral-subantimicrobial]].

## Summary

Oracea is Galderma's branded, once-daily, 40 mg modified-release doxycycline capsule for the inflammatory papules and pustules of rosacea, dosed below the threshold used to treat bacterial infections.[^dailymed] Each capsule mixes two bead types, 30 mg immediate-release and 10 mg delayed-release, that together deliver 40 mg of anhydrous doxycycline; the label states plainly that ORACEA capsules are "not bioequivalent to other doxycycline products," so a generic antibacterial-dose doxycycline capsule is not a substitute for this specific release profile.[^dailymed] The mechanism by which this dose controls rosacea's inflammatory lesions is, per the label, unknown, though the sub-antimicrobial dosing strategy and its rationale (limiting antibiotic-resistance pressure while still delivering an anti-inflammatory effect) are graded and explained on [[doxycycline-oral-subantimicrobial]].[^dailymed]

This is a health-framed claim, not a cosmetic one: the FDA indication is limited to inflammatory lesions, and the label states subjects on ORACEA "did not demonstrate significant improvement in erythema when compared to those treated with placebo," so it does not address the background redness or visible vessels covered on [[rosacea-erythematotelangiectatic]].[^dailymed] Dosing is a single capsule taken once daily in the morning on an empty stomach, at least one hour before or two hours after meals, because a high-fat meal cut absorption (Cmax and AUC) by about 45% and 22% respectively in the label's food-effect study.[^dailymed]

## The Evidence

**Two registration placebo-controlled trials (FDA label, Clinical Studies section).** Two randomized, placebo-controlled, multicenter, double-blind, 16-week phase 3 trials enrolled 537 subjects (269 on ORACEA, 268 on placebo) with 10 to 40 papules and pustules and two or fewer nodules, mean baseline lesion counts of 20 to 21 in both arms.[^dailymed] Co-primary endpoints at week 16 were mean reduction in lesion count and the share reaching a static Investigator's Global Assessment of clear or almost clear (1 to 2 small papules or pustules).[^dailymed]

| Endpoint (FDA label Clinical Studies)[^dailymed] | Study 1 ORACEA (N=127) | Study 1 Placebo (N=124) | Study 2 ORACEA (N=142) | Study 2 Placebo (N=144) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean change in lesion count | -11.8 | -5.9 | -9.5 | -4.3 |
| Clear or almost clear (IGA) | 39 (30.7%) | 24 (19.4%) | 21 (14.8%) | 9 (6.3%) |

The label adds one explicit negative finding: "Subjects treated with ORACEA did not demonstrate significant improvement in erythema when compared to those treated with placebo."[^dailymed]

> Bottom line: against placebo, once-daily ORACEA roughly doubled lesion-count reduction and produced a clear-or-almost-clear rate that was 1.6 to 2.3 times the placebo rate, but did not visibly reduce background redness.

Two limits matter for reading these numbers. First, the trials ran 16 weeks in mild-to-moderate rosacea (10 to 40 lesions, two or fewer nodules); the label does not report longer-term or more-severe-disease data in this section. Second, this is a single-drug label report, not an independent cross-trial synthesis; broader context on how sub-antimicrobial doxycycline compares across formulations and to other oral and topical rosacea treatments lives on [[doxycycline-oral-subantimicrobial]].

## What's In It

- **Active: doxycycline, USP, 40 mg** (as anhydrous doxycycline), split as 30 mg immediate-release beads and 10 mg delayed-release beads inside one hard gelatin capsule.[^dailymed] See [[doxycycline-oral-subantimicrobial]] for the graded mechanism and cross-formulation evidence base.
- **Inert ingredients:** hypromellose, iron oxide red, iron oxide yellow, methacrylic acid copolymer, polyethylene glycol, Polysorbate 80, sugar spheres, talc, titanium dioxide, and triethyl citrate, as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed]

The label's only contraindication is hypersensitivity to doxycycline or other tetracyclines.[^dailymed] Because it is an oral tetracycline, not a topical, it carries the full class warnings: inhibition of bone growth and permanent tooth discoloration if used during the second/third trimester of pregnancy or in children up to age 8, photosensitivity, pseudotumor cerebri, Clostridium difficile-associated diarrhea, and autoimmune syndromes, none of which apply to a topical rosacea drug.[^dailymed] The label carries no boxed warning.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Sub-antimicrobial" or "anti-inflammatory dose" framing.** This is not marketing spin, it is the label's own pharmacology: Section 12.4 states plasma concentrations achieved with ORACEA "are less than the concentration required to treat bacterial diseases," and Section 1.2 instructs prescribers not to use it for infections, prophylaxis, or reducing bacterial counts.[^dailymed] The claim is well supported by the label itself.
- **A once-daily oral option that also clears redness.** Not supported: the label explicitly reports no significant erythema improvement over placebo in the registration trials, so any redness claim for this product would go beyond what the trials showed.[^dailymed]
