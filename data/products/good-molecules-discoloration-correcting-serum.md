Good Molecules Discoloration Correcting Serum is a budget serum from Good Molecules built around 4% niacinamide and cetyl tranexamate mesylate, an ester derivative of tranexamic acid, sold in a 1 fl oz (30 ml) bottle.[^1][^2]

> Niacinamide has modest evidence, much of it from small or manufacturer-run trials, for evening facial hyperpigmentation and supporting the skin barrier; that case sits on [[niacinamide]]. The serum's second active is not tranexamic acid itself but cetyl tranexamate mesylate, an ester derivative, at an undisclosed concentration; tranexamic acid's own evidence for melasma is real but strongest via the oral route, with thinner and more mixed evidence for topical use, on [[tranexamic-acid]]. At 4%, the niacinamide dose sits inside the concentration range actually used in the trials behind its evidence, unlike the higher, above-range 10% dose in Good Molecules' own [[good-molecules-niacinamide-serum]]. No trial tests this exact formula. It is listed at $11.99 for 1 fl oz ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-07).

## Summary

Good Molecules Discoloration Correcting Serum pairs niacinamide, labeled at 4% on the product listing, with cetyl tranexamate mesylate, an ester derivative of tranexamic acid whose concentration is not declared.[^2] Good Molecules positions it to target dark spots, uneven skin tone, age spots, acne scars, hyperpigmentation, and sun damage.[^2]

Topical niacinamide is well tolerated and has modest evidence, much of it from small or manufacturer-run studies, for supporting the skin barrier and lowering water loss in dry skin, for lightening facial hyperpigmentation, and for mild inflammatory acne; that general case is set out on [[niacinamide]]. This product's 4% niacinamide sits within the 2% to 5% range actually used in the trials behind that evidence, unlike the higher, above-range 10% dose in the brand's own [[good-molecules-niacinamide-serum]].

The second active is not tranexamic acid itself but its ester, cetyl tranexamate mesylate, and the declared ingredient list does not state a concentration for it.[^2] Tranexamic acid is a synthetic antifibrinolytic drug used off-label for melasma, with modest evidence for the oral route that is durable only while it is taken, and thinner, more mixed evidence for topical use; see [[tranexamic-acid]]. No published study isolates cetyl tranexamate mesylate specifically, so how closely this ester behaves like the parent molecule is not established by the cited sources.

The full ingredient list runs to eleven entries: water, propanediol and glycerin (solvents and humectants), niacinamide, cetyl tranexamate mesylate, cetearyl alcohol (emollient), ceratonia siliqua (carob) gum and tamarindus indica seed gum (thickeners), caprylyl glycol and ethylhexylglycerin (preservative system), and tocopherol (vitamin E).[^2] At $11.99 for 1 fl oz (30 ml), it sits in the same budget tier as the brand's [[good-molecules-niacinamide-serum]].[^2]

Good Molecules' directions read: "Apply a few drops in the morning and at night before oils and moisturizers. Can be used twice daily for faster results."[^2] The label's other claims are weighed against the evidence under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Topical niacinamide is a well-tolerated active with modest evidence for supporting the skin barrier, lightening facial hyperpigmentation, and mild inflammatory acne; how well each use holds up, and how much of it comes from small or manufacturer-run trials, is set out on [[niacinamide]]. Tranexamic acid is a synthetic antifibrinolytic drug with real but modest evidence for melasma, strongest via the oral route and thinner via the topical route; see [[tranexamic-acid]]. No published clinical trial tests this exact formula, and none isolates cetyl tranexamate mesylate, the specific ester derivative declared on the label rather than tranexamic acid itself, so the grades below rest on the category evidence for niacinamide and for tranexamic acid at the concentrations and forms that were actually studied.[^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing terms not established by the graded evidence above:

- **"This lightweight serum targets dark spots and uneven skin tone using an advanced tranexamic acid form and niacinamide to improve appearance of age spots, acne scars, hyperpigmentation, and sun damage."** Niacinamide's better-evidenced topical use is facial hyperpigmentation broadly, not age spots, acne scars, or sun damage specifically, set out on [[niacinamide]]; tranexamic acid's evidence is specific to melasma, set out on [[tranexamic-acid]]; the age spot, acne scar, and sun damage claims are the product's own positioning.[^2]
- **"Prevent appearance of freckles and hyperpigmentation caused by inflammation."** Neither niacinamide's nor tranexamic acid's cited evidence covers freckles or a preventive claim specifically.[^2]
- **The undisclosed concentration of cetyl tranexamate mesylate.** The ingredient list does not state how much of the tranexamic acid derivative the formula contains, so its dose cannot be compared with the concentrations tested in the studies on [[tranexamic-acid]].[^2]
- **"Can be used twice daily for faster results."** This is the brand's own directions; no cited trial tests a twice-daily schedule against a less frequent one for this formula.[^2]
