ZoomScan is Sciton's tunable erbium:YAG resurfacing handpiece, a professional in-office device rather than anything a consumer buys. **Bottom line:** the hardware is real and its published specifications are unusually specific, and everything claimed for it clinically is either borrowed from the erbium:YAG modality or asserted with nothing published behind it. The modality's evidence is on [[acne-scar-procedures-by-evidence]].

> **If you came here looking for the Contour TRL, this is the page the brand serves you.** The URL sciton.com/contour-trl/ returns a live page titled "ZoomScan™ by Sciton" whose canonical address is sciton.com/zoomscan-by-sciton/, and the words Contour and TRL appear nowhere in its body.[^brand] What that URL does is route the retired name to the current product. It is not a statement from Sciton about succession, and this page does not make one.

## Summary

The published specifications, verbatim from the brand's own table: laser type and wavelength "Er:YAG 2940 nm", maximum power "40 W", a pulse duration spanning 100 microseconds to 50 milliseconds, dual mode pulsing "Ablate / Coagulate", ablation depth "0 - 300 μm", coagulation depth "0 - 130 μm", and a scan area of "30 x 30 mm".[^brand] That is more hardware detail than most device makers publish, and it is worth saying so plainly.

Neither ZoomScan nor Contour TRL is a device name in FDA's 510(k) index. A device-name query for ZoomScan returns the error message "No matches found!", and a query for Contour TRL returns no results either.[^openfda] What Sciton holds is 23 clearances filed under platform names, every one of them published as a 510(k) Summary: the Image and Profile systems, Joule and mJOULE, and a 2001 entry named "CONTOUR PROFILE LASER", the only Sciton clearance whose name contains the word Contour.[^openfda][^k010285] Handpieces are marketed by name and cleared as part of a platform, which is why searching the database for the name on the clinic's price list comes up empty. The general point is on [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

No price is published. The brand page offers a "Build My Machine" configurator rather than a figure, which is normal for physician capital equipment, and no dealer quotation was obtained.[^brand]

## The Rubric

**Deep resurfacing for wrinkles and scars**
Graded notable on mixed evidence, and the grade is inherited rather than earned. Ablative erbium:YAG resurfacing is an established modality with a real trial literature, ranked against its alternatives on [[acne-scar-procedures-by-evidence]], and none of that literature tested this handpiece. On the brand's own page the supporting material is four before-and-after cases contributed by named physicians, of which two are labelled "5 months post 1 NanoLaserPeel™ treatment" and "3 months post 1 NanoLaserPeel™ treatment", naming a treatment protocol rather than the handpiece.[^brand] Courtesy photographs from four clinicians are not a trial, whoever took them.

**Skin laxity and tightening**
Graded minimal on anecdotal evidence. The claim is the brand's and it is already hedged: ZoomScan "may improve skin laxity".[^brand] The reasoning offered for it, "With virtually no heat deposition", sits on the same page as a spec table listing dual-mode pulsing as "Ablate / Coagulate" with a coagulation depth of "0 - 130 μm".[^brand] A device cannot coagulate 130 microns of tissue and deposit virtually no heat. One of those two statements is doing marketing work.

**Pigmented lesions**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. Here the brand drops the hedge: spot treatments "safely and effectively reduce pigmented lesions".[^brand] Nothing is published behind it, no case in the gallery is presented as a pigment case, and the only Sciton clearance whose indication language reaches pigmented lesions is for a different wavelength, the 1927 nm system cleared for "treatment of benign pigmented lesions such as, but not limited to lentigos (age spots), solar lentigos (sun spots) and ephelides (freckles)".[^k182173]

## The Evidence

**"Studies have shown" names no study.** On CO2 comparison the page states that "Studies have shown that erbium lasers produce far fewer side effects such as the prolonged erythema and hypopigmentation that are common with CO2 lasers".[^brand] Read in full, the page cites no study, names no author and links to no paper. The underlying physics argument it gives is sound and conventional, that erbium's higher water absorption vaporises tissue with less thermal conduction, and the sentence that follows converts physics into a comparative clinical claim without producing the comparison. Worth noting against it: on [[actinic-cheilitis]], CO2 laser holds the highest pooled clearance proportion among the treatments covered there. Fewer side effects and better clearance are different questions, and a page that argues the first should not be read as settling the second.

**All skin types, with no skin-type data.** Asked directly whether it can treat every skin type, the page answers "All skin types can be treated with ZoomScan", and elsewhere states that it "treats all skin types on the face and body".[^brand] The same page offers up to 300 μm of ablation per pass. Checked by reading the page in full, it publishes no breakdown by Fitzpatrick type, no post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation rate and no adverse-event table. The claim may well be defensible in trained hands at conservative settings. It is unsupported on the page that makes it, and darker skin is where ablative resurfacing carries its best-known risk.

**The specificity cuts both ways.** "In one versatile handpiece, ZoomScan offers infinite treatment options", the page says, while its own table bounds the device at 0 to 300 μm of ablation and 0 to 130 μm of coagulation.[^brand] The bounded numbers are the useful part. A continuum between two published limits is a genuine engineering achievement and it is not infinite, and a reader who takes the specifications seriously is better served than one who takes the adjective seriously.

**Downtime is stated honestly.** The page says downtime "can range from 1 to 14 days depending on the skin's condition and depth of treatment", with as little as 24 hours for the lightest peel and up to 10 days for aggressive depth.[^brand] That is a real and useful disclosure, it is the kind of thing device pages usually bury, and it belongs in any conversation about whether ablative resurfacing is the right choice. Compare the no-downtime home devices on [[home-use-skincare-devices-by-evidence]], which also do far less.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice. Ablative laser resurfacing is a physician-performed procedure with a recovery period and a real risk of pigmentary change and scarring, and the settings matter more than the brand name. Anyone considering it should discuss their own skin type and history with a board-certified dermatologist or plastic surgeon.

## Common Marketing Claims

**"the pinnacle of resurfacing laser technology".** The brand's description of dual-mode erbium:YAG.[^brand] It ranks a technology class rather than reporting an outcome for this handpiece.

**"Even the deepest wrinkles and rhytides are no match for ZoomScan, which can rejuvenate skin up to 300 microns deep".** The 300 micron figure is in the specification table and is the one checkable part of the sentence.[^brand] Rhytides are wrinkles, so the phrase names the same thing twice.

**"Each treatment can be tuned to be as light as microdermabrasion, as thorough as a chemical peel, and as complete as full-field resurfacing".** A range claim about the tuning, and the most informative sentence on the page for a patient deciding what they are agreeing to.[^brand]

## Where to Buy

Not available to consumers. Sciton sells to physician practices through a configurator rather than a price list, and treatments are purchased from clinics that own the platform.[^brand] No price is quoted here.
