A $1,499 handheld red and infrared LED device. **Bottom line:** it is cleared by the FDA for wrinkles around the eyes, the submission contained no clinical data, and it is sold on a promise about the whole face.

> **Cleared for the eye area only.** The Indications for Use read in full: the device "is an over the counter device indicated to emit energy in the red and IR region of the spectrum for use in dermatology for the treatment of periorbital wrinkles".[^k] Periorbital means around the eyes. The file also states plainly that "Clinical data was not included in this submission."[^k]

## Summary

### What the clearance actually establishes

K152332, decided 2016-02-25 as substantially equivalent to a 2011 predicate, the Silk'n FX from Home Skinovations.[^k] Product code OHS, which is one of the FDA codes that does define its own scope, as [[home-devices-by-whether-the-code-defines-the-claim]] sets out. The non-clinical testing was bench work against electrical safety standards.[^k]

Against that predicate the device is near-identical. Wavelengths are listed as "633 ±5nm, 830 ±5nm" on both sides.[^k] The energy source differs slightly: "25 LEDs over 17cm" against the predicate's "24 LEDs over 12 cm2", reproduced here with the units as the file prints them, which are not consistent between the two columns.[^k] The treatment regime is listed identically for both: three times a week for 15 to 20 minutes during the first four weeks, five to seven minutes per treatment zone, then once weekly for maintenance.[^k]

## The Rubric

**The gap between the indication and the pitch.** The clearance covers periorbital wrinkles. The brand's description says "PEREFCTIO™ WILL REJUVENATE YOUR SKIN'S APPEARANCE AND STRUCTURE USING DUAL ACTION TECHNIQUES, RED LED LIGHT, AND TOPICAL HEAT", with the brand's own spelling of its product name.[^brand] Appearance is a cosmetic claim. Structure is not, and the file supports neither beyond the eye area.

**A wavelength discrepancy worth noting.** The brand states the device uses red and infrared LEDs in a range it gives as 640 to 840 nm.[^brand] The FDA file specifies two discrete wavelengths, 633 ±5 nm and 830 ±5 nm.[^k] Those are not the same statement: a range implies continuous output between its bounds, and 633 ±5 nm reaches at most 638 nm, which falls outside the range the brand gives. This page records the discrepancy and does not resolve it, since no measurement of the shipped device was available.

**Price against the field.** $1,499 for a handheld unit treating one zone at a time.[^brand] [[currentbody-skin-led-light-therapy-mask-series-2]] is cleared under the same product code for full-face wrinkles and costs a fraction of that, and its file is equally free of clinical data.

**No clinical evidence specific to this device.** Stated by the file itself. Whether red light at these wavelengths does anything is a separate question answered at [[home-led-light-therapy]].

## The Evidence

None for this product. The 510(k) contains no clinical study and says so, and no trial of the Perfectio was sought or found for this page.[^k] That is not unusual for an OHS-code device and is exactly why the clearance should not be read as efficacy.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined, not endorsed. Beyond the rejuvenation claim above, the brand states that "Perfectio ™ is an FDA Class II medical device", which is accurate and means less than it sounds: Class II is a risk classification, and the clearance route used here establishes similarity to an existing device rather than effectiveness.[^brand] The brand also offers a two-year warranty and applies "A 15% restocking fee" to returns.[^brand]

## Who it is for

Someone who wants a red and near-infrared device for the eye area, has read what the clearance does and does not say, and is content paying $1,499 for 25 LEDs delivering the same two wavelengths as a 2011 predicate. Anyone buying it to rejuvenate their face is buying something the FDA file does not describe.
