The portable sibling of [[dpl-ii-panel]], cleared four days later and for a much smaller patch of face. **Bottom line:** its file publishes its irradiance, narrows the indication to the area around the eyes, and says in the same breath that the indication does not change. Whether LED does anything for wrinkles is on [[home-led-light-therapy]].

> **Cleared for the eye area only, from a predicate cleared for the whole face.** The indication reads "The dpl® SpectraLite is an Over-the-Counter (OTC) device intended for use in treating wrinkles within the periorbital region", while the predicate chart in the same document describes its predicate as "intended for the use in treating full-face wrinkles".[^k171386] Two pages later the file states that "The indication for use does not change and is not expanded."[^k171386] Narrowing is not expanding, so the sentence is defensible, but the indication plainly did change.

## Summary

Cleared 2017-08-07 as K171386, applicant LED Technologies, Inc. of Greenwood Village, Colorado, product code OHS under 21 CFR 878.4810, a Traditional 510(k) published as a Summary.[^openfda] It was received the same day as the dpl II Panel and cleared four days after it.[^openfda]

**What it is.** A handheld with "four types of LEDs: 605nm amber, 625nm red, 660nm red, and 880nm infrared", shipping with a USB charging cord and a storage case.[^k171386] Like its sibling it has no controls: "The treatment time is controlled by the user. There are no user settings or adjustments required."[^k171386]

**It is the battery version, and the file says so.** "The dpl® SpectraLite is battery powered (LI-Ion Battery 5V USB & 3.7 V Battery)", and the submission explains the change in plain terms: "The change in energy (the addition of battery) is usually part of a redesign to provide a portable version of a predicate device."[^k171386] A TPE silicone surface was added "for user comfort".[^k171386]

**The numbers it publishes.** Energy Level "61.59 mW/cm²" over a "28 cm²" treatment area at "3 minutes per treatment", with the regimen "five days a week for eight weeks".[^k171386] At 180 seconds that is about 11.1 J/cm2 per session, which is this page's arithmetic on the file's figures rather than a number the file states.

**No price is quoted here.** No brand storefront page was fetched and no retailer was consulted.

## The Rubric

**Periorbital wrinkles, the cleared indication (cosmetic)**
Graded modest on mixed evidence, inherited from the modality rather than earned here. Red and infrared light for facial rejuvenation is the better-evidenced half of the LED case, ranked on [[home-led-light-therapy]] and [[home-use-skincare-devices-by-evidence]]. This device adds nothing to it: its file contains no clinical study. The periorbital limit is the part most likely to be lost in retail copy, and it is the same narrowing documented on [[theraface-led-light-ring]].

**Anything this device itself demonstrated**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. "A Usability Study was conducted with 15 participants", reporting that "100% of the participants were able to demonstrate the light sensitivity test." and "100% of the participants were able to use the device successfully."[^k171386] The file's own framing is that "The conclusions drawn from nonclinical tests demonstrate that the device is safe, as effective, and performs as well as the legally marketed devices."[^k171386]

## The Evidence

**It completes the identification of a chart this site had already read.** The later perioral filing K172662 lists three predecessor devices at 62.07, 70.16 and 61.59 mW/cm2 with treatment areas of 30, 415 and 28 cm2, without naming them. All three are now matched to their own filings: 62.07 over 30 cm2 is the dpl Nüve for Wrinkles, 70.16 over 415 cm2 is [[dpl-ii-panel]], and 61.59 over 28 cm2 is this device, confirmed here against its own predicate chart.[^k171386] The disclosure ranking is on [[home-led-devices-by-what-the-file-publishes]].

**The boilerplate that is wrong on the sibling is right here.** Both filings carry the identical line that the device "Has the same/similar output (i.e., 62 mW/cm²) as the predicate device".[^k171386] On this device that is accurate, since 61.59 rounds to 62. On the dpl II Panel, whose own chart says 70.16, the same sentence attaches the predicate's figure to the subject, which reads like a copy-paste from this submission rather than a considered claim.

**A real difference between the siblings, in one digit.** This device lists 625 nm red where the dpl II Panel lists 630 nm, with the other three wavelengths identical.[^k171386] Nothing in either file argues the 5 nm matters, and nothing here claims it does; it is recorded because the two are otherwise described in near-identical language.

**What was tested.** Functional performance testing, software validation and user safety testing, against IEC 60601-1 (2012), IEC 62471 (2006) for photobiological safety of lamps, and IEC 60601-1-2 (2014), plus a long CE, RoHS, REACH, phthalate, battery and FCC list, and a biocompatibility test against 21 CFR 177.1810 for the added silicone.[^k171386] The photobiological safety standard is worth noting on an eye-area device; the sibling panel's file does not list it. None of it measures a wrinkle.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice. This device is cleared for use around the eye, where a light source sits close to the eye itself, and the file's one human study tested whether people could complete a light sensitivity check. Follow the manufacturer's eye-protection instructions.

## Common Marketing Claims

**No brand marketing copy is quoted here.** No storefront page was fetched, so the only claims assessed are the regulated ones in the FDA file.

**Periorbital is the word to carry into any shop listing.** The cleared use is wrinkles around the eyes.[^k171386] A device photographed against a whole face is not thereby cleared for one, and the gap between a cleared indication and the marketing around it is the subject of [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].
