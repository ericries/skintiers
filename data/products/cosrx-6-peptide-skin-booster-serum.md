COSRX The 6 Peptide Skin Booster Serum is a lightweight, fragrance-free leave-on serum that combines six peptides (led by acetyl hexapeptide-8 and copper tripeptide-1) with niacinamide and a glycerin, hyaluronic acid and amino-acid humectant base, sold mainly in a large 150 mL bottle.[^1]

> A broad, cheap peptide serum. Whether topical peptides visibly reduce lines or firm skin is a preliminary question, and for acetyl hexapeptide-8, the peptide named first, a 2025 review found its skin penetration is limited.[^2] If you want to try a large peptide blend, though, including the better-studied copper peptide (GHK-Cu), this is an unusually broad and inexpensive way to do it: six peptides plus niacinamide in a hydrating base, about $25 for 150 mL, several times the volume of a typical peptide serum.[^1] Allure's Sarah Han names it a favorite; the benefit with the clearest support is hydration. The peptide evidence is on [[peptides]] and in [The Evidence](#the-evidence).[^6]

## Summary

The 6 Peptide Skin Booster Serum is a watery, fragrance-free leave-on serum that COSRX positions as a first-step booster after cleansing. What sets it apart is breadth and price rather than a novel active: it puts six peptides in one bottle (led by acetyl hexapeptide-8 and copper tripeptide-1, also called GHK-Cu), adds niacinamide, and builds the base on glycerin, sodium hyaluronate and skin-identical amino acids.[^1] The 150 mL bottle is listed at about $25 ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28), several times the volume of a typical 30 mL peptide serum, which makes it one of the cheapest ways to try a large peptide blend, copper peptide included.

Whether topical peptides do much for aging is a separate and more skeptical question. Peptides are well-tolerated ingredients with a modest, mostly preliminary measured effect on the look of aging; more is on [[peptides]]. For acetyl hexapeptide-8, the peptide this serum names first, [a 2025 review found its skin penetration is limited](#the-evidence) and its delivery to the sites it targets uncertain.[^2] Those anti-aging endpoints, the appearance of fine lines and firmness, are cosmetic. The benefit with the clearest support here is hydration: humectants like glycerin and hyaluronic acid draw water into the outer skin and reliably improve short-term hydration, a modest, well-established effect measured against a plain humectant serum; more is on [[moisturizing]].[^1]

Among the six, copper tripeptide-1 (GHK-Cu) is one of the more-studied cosmetic peptides. [Decades of research](#the-evidence) report it supports wound healing and, in the lab, stimulates collagen, elastin and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, though much of that work sits in cell and animal models and the leading reviews are by the peptide's discoverer.[^5] The Ordinary sells a dedicated copper-peptide serum; see [[the-ordinary-multi-peptide-copper-peptides-serum]].

On reception, Allure commerce editor Sarah Han names it among her favorite COSRX products, writing that it is "alllll about elasticity-boosting peptides" and that "the glow I'm left with is unreal," and praising the gel-like texture; Allure lists it at $25.[^6] Cosmetic chemists keep expectations lower for peptides as a class: the formulators at Chemist Confessions describe a good peptide as "a really solid secondary character that helps your other actives do their job better" and say "it's worth keeping expectations grounded."[^7]

The product-specific evidence and its limits are in [The Evidence](#the-evidence); the brand's clinical-trial percentages and the "Glamour Approved 2026" badge are separated out under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Peptides are well-tolerated skincare ingredients with a modest, mostly preliminary measured effect on the look of aging; how that evidence holds up is set out on [[peptides]]. What is relevant to this serum sits at the level of its individual peptides and its humectant base.

Copper tripeptide-1 (GHK-Cu), second on the peptide list, is one of the more-studied cosmetic peptides. Pickart and Margolina (2018) review it and report that GHK "increases collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, as well as supports the function of dermal fibroblasts," and that its "ability to improve tissue repair has been demonstrated for skin, lung connective tissue, boney tissue, liver, and stomach lining."[^5] Much of this rests on cell, animal and gene-expression work rather than trials of a leave-on cosmetic serum, the authors frame the actions as health-positive only "according to our current knowledge," and the peptide's principal reviews are by Loren Pickart, who discovered GHK.[^5]

> **Bottom line.** GHK-Cu has a long research record in wound healing and, in the lab, drives collagen and skin-matrix synthesis. That evidence is mostly experimental, and its leading reviews are by the peptide's discoverer.[^5]

Acetyl hexapeptide-8 (Argireline) is the "botox-like" peptide this serum names first. Zdrada-Nowak and colleagues (2025) reviewed its skin permeability and efficacy and reported that, "due to its hydrophilic nature and relatively large molecular size, AH-8 faces limited permeability through the lipophilic stratum corneum, making effective dermal delivery challenging," that its ability to reach neuromuscular junctions "remains uncertain," and that the mechanism behind its effects "remain[s] incompletely understood." They wrote that "preclinical and clinical studies indicate that AH-8 may reduce wrinkle depth, improve skin elasticity, and enhance hydration."[^2]

> **Bottom line.** Even for the peptide this serum leads with, the 2025 review found topical delivery is limited and the anti-wrinkle evidence is preliminary and conditional ("may reduce wrinkle depth").[^2]

Gorouhi and Maibach (2009) group topical cosmetic peptides into four types (signal, enzyme-inhibitor, neurotransmitter-inhibitor, and carrier peptides) and review the controlled ex vivo and in vivo efficacy studies behind them.[^3] This serum draws from several of those types, and copper tripeptide-1 is a carrier peptide.

> **Bottom line.** Topical peptides are organized by their proposed mechanism, and this serum combines peptides from several of those groups.[^3]

Griffiths, Watson and Langton (2023) review topical treatments for aging skin and call topical tretinoin "a prescription-only medicine considered to be the clinical 'gold standard'." They note that a "cosmeceutical" category "at the midpoint of the spectrum of cosmetics and pharmaceutical" has emerged, among which they review retinoids, peptides, and antioxidants.[^4]

> **Bottom line.** In the dermatology literature's own framing, the strongest topical anti-aging evidence is for tretinoin; peptides sit among the reviewed cosmeceutical-tier options.[^4]

The serum's base is built on humectants: glycerin, sodium hyaluronate, and skin-identical amino acids and natural-moisturizing-factor components (serine, glycine, sodium PCA, sodium lactate).[^1] Humectants draw water into the outer skin, a well-established short-term hydration benefit covered on [[moisturizing]].

## What's In It

The full ingredient list (INCI) as declared by the manufacturer.[^1] The ingredients with a functional role are linked to their own pages; the rest are the base, texture, and preservative system.

Peptides and actives:

- six [[peptides]]: acetyl hexapeptide-8 (Argireline), copper tripeptide-1 (GHK-Cu), sh-polypeptide-121, dipeptide diaminobutyroyl benzylamide diacetate, oligopeptide-68, and palmitoyl tripeptide-8

Label and marketing claims not established by the product-specific evidence above:

- **"Blends six powerful peptides to address fine lines, uneven texture, and loss of firmness"** and the claims to **"boost elasticity, refine the appearance of pores, and leave skin smoother and stronger."** The six peptides appear in the ingredient list.[^1] Topical peptide anti-aging evidence is preliminary, and for acetyl hexapeptide-8 the reviewed evidence describes limited skin penetration and uncertain delivery.[^2][^4]
- **Manufacturer clinical-trial figures such as "+162.50% hydration," "+76.42% skin texture," "+37.9% brightness," and "+22.22% density."** These come from COSRX's own testing of the product; the independent published evidence for topical peptides is preliminary and for acetyl hexapeptide-8 describes limited delivery.[^1][^2]
