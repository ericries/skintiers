Loprox Shampoo was the original branded name for ciclopirox 1% shampoo, a prescription topical antifungal applied twice weekly to treat seborrheic dermatitis of the scalp in adults.[^drugsfda] FDA records show the Loprox-brand shampoo (NDA 021159, most recently sponsored by Bausch) is now discontinued, but the same drug remains available and FDA-approved as a generic, sold under labels that still carry the identical formula and trial data the Loprox brand was built on.[^drugsfda][^dailymed]

> A twice-weekly antifungal shampoo, not a daily wash. Across its two registration 4-week trials, it beat an inert vehicle shampoo on a combined clear-or-near-clear, erythema, and scaling endpoint, though the effect size varied a lot between the two studies (58% vs 31% in one, 26% vs 13% in the other), and the label states efficacy was not demonstrated for Black patients, a subgroup of only 53 patients across both trials.[^dailymed] Side effects were mild and uncommon: increased itching and application-site burning, redness, or itching each occurred in about 1% of the 626 shampoo-treated patients.[^dailymed] Since the branded product is discontinued, buying it now means buying the generic, which [GoodRx lists at $29.12 for a 120 mL bottle](#where-to-buy) as of this writing.[^goodrx]

## Summary

Loprox Shampoo, ciclopirox 1%, is a prescription-only leave-on-then-rinse antifungal shampoo indicated for the topical treatment of seborrheic dermatitis of the scalp in adults.[^dailymed] It is not a cosmetic dandruff shampoo: the FDA approval and the label's own clinical-studies section are built entirely around clearing the health condition [[seborrheic-dermatitis]], the scaly, itchy, sometimes red scalp rash linked to *Malassezia* yeast overgrowth, not around cosmetic hair or scalp feel.[^dailymed]

The active, ciclopirox, is a hydroxypyridone antifungal that works by a different mechanism than azole antifungals like ketoconazole: it chelates the iron and aluminum cations that fungal cells need for peroxide-degrading enzymes, rather than blocking ergosterol synthesis.[^dailymed] The label itself is candid that "the relevance of this property for the indication of seborrheic dermatitis is not known," and that "the clinical significance of antifungal activity in the treatment of seborrheic dermatitis is not known" either, the mechanism is plausible but not proven to be why the shampoo works.[^dailymed] The broader, category-level evidence base for ciclopirox 1% against seborrheic dermatitis, including the 2015 Cochrane review of 51 trials, lives on [[ciclopirox]]; this page covers only the trial data specific to this shampoo's own FDA label.

Loprox as a brand name is discontinued. FDA drug-approval records list NDA 021159 for Loprox Shampoo, ciclopirox 1%, most recently sponsored by Bausch, with a marketing status of "Discontinued."[^drugsfda] The underlying drug did not disappear: several manufacturers, including Taro Pharmaceuticals, Actavis, Padagis, Sun Pharmaceutical, and Teligent, currently market FDA-approved generic ciclopirox 1% shampoo carrying the same indication, dosing, and (per the Taro label reviewed here) the same registration-trial data that originally supported Loprox.[^dailymed]

## The Evidence

The FDA label (reviewed here via a current generic ciclopirox 1% shampoo listing, which carries the same clinical-studies section) describes two randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trials in patients 16 and older with seborrheic dermatitis of the scalp.[^dailymed] Patients applied ciclopirox shampoo or its vehicle twice weekly for 4 weeks. At week 4, investigators scored the overall status of the seborrheic dermatitis and the severity of erythema/inflammation and scaling on a 0-5 scale; "effective treatment" required a simultaneous score of 0, or 1 if the baseline score was 3 or higher, across all three measures.[^dailymed]

**Effective treatment rates at week 4:**
- Study 1: 220/380 (58%) with ciclopirox shampoo vs 60/192 (31%) with vehicle
- Study 2: 65/250 (26%) with ciclopirox shampoo vs 32/249 (13%) with vehicle[^dailymed]

The label states ciclopirox shampoo was statistically significantly more effective than vehicle in both studies, but the raw response rates above show the effect size differed substantially between the two trials, roughly double the vehicle response in Study 1 (58% vs 31%) and roughly double in Study 2 as well by ratio (26% vs 13%), though the absolute clearance rate in Study 2 was much lower overall.[^dailymed] The label separately flags that "Efficacy for black patients was not demonstrated," noting only 53 Black patients were enrolled across the two studies, a small-subgroup caveat rather than a finding of ineffectiveness.[^dailymed]

> Bottom line: twice-weekly use for 4 weeks roughly doubled the rate of clearing (or near-clearing) scalp seborrheic dermatitis versus an inert vehicle shampoo, but the absolute clearance rate ranged widely between the two registration trials (58% down to 26%), and the trials under-enrolled Black patients.[^dailymed]

**Safety.** Among 626 patients treated with the shampoo twice weekly across the two registration studies, the most frequent adverse events were increased itching (1%) and application-site reactions such as burning, redness, or itching (also 1%); other events occurred only in individual patients.[^dailymed] Adverse events leading to early discontinuation occurred in 1.5% (26/1,738) of shampoo-treated patients and 2.0% (12/661) of vehicle-treated patients across the full safety database, with seborrhea itself the most common reason for stopping in either group.[^dailymed]

Category-level evidence for the ciclopirox active, including the Cochrane meta-analysis and head-to-head data against ketoconazole, is graded separately on [[ciclopirox]].

## What's In It

- **Active: ciclopirox 1%** (10 mg ciclopirox per gram, equivalent to 0.96 mL, of shampoo).[^dailymed] See [[ciclopirox]] for the graded mechanism and category-level evidence base.
- **Shampoo base:** disodium laureth sulfosuccinate, laureth-2, purified water USP, sodium chloride USP, and sodium laureth sulfate, as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed]

The label states the shampoo is not for ophthalmic, oral, or intravaginal use, and is contraindicated in individuals who have shown hypersensitivity to any of its components.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

Because the Loprox brand itself is discontinued, there is no active brand marketing to adjudicate here. The claims below are the kind of framing this drug class carries in generic listings and pharmacy materials, checked against the label:

- **"Antifungal shampoo for dandruff."** The label's actual indication is narrower and more specific: seborrheic dermatitis of the scalp in adults, a diagnosed inflammatory skin condition, not generic cosmetic dandruff.[^dailymed]
- **"Clinically proven."** True in the narrow sense that the label cites two statistically significant vehicle-controlled trials, but the trials' own absolute effectiveness rates varied widely (58% vs 26% between studies) and the label itself says the antifungal mechanism's relevance to the actual therapeutic effect is not known.[^dailymed]
