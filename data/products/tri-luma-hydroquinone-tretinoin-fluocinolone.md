Tri-Luma is the brand name for a prescription triple-combination cream made by Galderma, containing fluocinolone acetonide 0.01% (a corticosteroid), hydroquinone 4% (a melanin-synthesis inhibitor), and tretinoin 0.05% (a retinoid) in a single hydrophilic base, applied once daily to treat melasma of the face.[^dailymed] It is the only FDA-approved triple-combination product for melasma, first approved in 2002, and its own label states plainly that it is indicated for short-term use only.[^dailymed]

> The best-evidenced prescription option for melasma: in the trials that supported approval, it beat each of the three possible two-ingredient combinations of its own actives, a stricter comparator than an inert vehicle.[^dailymed] But "short-term" is not a marketing qualifier, it is the label's own limit: Tri-Luma "is NOT indicated for the maintenance treatment of melasma," the fluocinolone component is a corticosteroid that can cause skin atrophy, striae, and telangiectasia with prolonged use, and the hydroquinone component carries a risk of exogenous ochronosis, a blue-black darkening of the skin, with extended use.[^dailymed] Melasma also recurs once treatment stops, and the label itself makes daily sunscreen and sun avoidance a condition of use, not an optional add-on.[^dailymed] The underlying evidence for each active ingredient, and the broader melasma treatment landscape, are covered on [[hydroquinone]], [[tretinoin]], and [[melasma]].

## Summary

Tri-Luma combines three actives that each work on melasma by a different mechanism: fluocinolone acetonide, a mid-potency topical corticosteroid, at 0.01%; hydroquinone, which inhibits melanin production, at 4%; and tretinoin, a retinoid that speeds skin turnover and helps the other two actives penetrate, at 0.05%.[^dailymed] The FDA-approved indication is narrow: "short-term treatment of moderate to severe melasma of the face, in the presence of measures for sun avoidance, including the use of sunscreens."[^dailymed] The full case for hydroquinone and tretinoin as pigmentation- and turnover-modifying ingredients, including their evidence outside this specific triple combination, lives on [[hydroquinone]] and [[tretinoin]]; melasma itself, why it happens and how it is otherwise managed, is covered on [[melasma]] and [[hyperpigmentation]].

What sets Tri-Luma apart from a compounded triple cream or from using hydroquinone and tretinoin separately is that Galderma's own approval trials tested all four possible combinations of its three actives side by side, rather than only triple-combination versus an inert vehicle ([the evidence](#the-evidence)).[^dailymed] The triple combination won in both trials, but the two trials also produced very different absolute results (38% versus 13% complete clearing), a gap the label does not explain and that argues for tempered expectations rather than assuming the higher number is typical.

Corticosteroid exposure is the tradeoff for that added effectiveness. The label groups Tri-Luma with other topical corticosteroids for systemic-absorption risk, warning that continued use "can produce reversible hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis suppression" and, less commonly, manifestations of Cushing's syndrome, hyperglycemia, or glucosuria, and it lists skin atrophy, striae, and telangiectasia among the local reactions that can follow prolonged corticosteroid use.[^dailymed] Hydroquinone adds its own long-use risk: the label warns the product "may produce exogenous ochronosis, a gradual blue-black darkening of the skin," most often reported with prolonged use.[^dailymed] Those two risks, not the tretinoin, are why the label caps this to short-term therapy and states it is not for maintenance.[^dailymed]

Local skin reactions were common in the trials rather than rare: erythema (41%), desquamation (38%), burning (18%), dryness (14%), pruritus (11%), and acne (5%) were each reported at greater than 5% incidence.[^dailymed] A generic version of the same three-active combination exists and has been tested against branded Tri-Luma directly, discussed briefly under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^generic]

## The Evidence

The efficacy case for Tri-Luma's individual ingredients, hydroquinone's pigment-suppressing action and tretinoin's effect on skin turnover, is owned by [[hydroquinone]] and [[tretinoin]]; this section covers only the trial data specific to the triple-combination product itself.

**Two 8-week, four-arm, randomized, controlled trials (641 subjects total, FDA label).** Subjects were adults 21 to 75 years old with Fitzpatrick skin types I-IV and moderate to severe melasma of the face.[^dailymed] Each trial randomized subjects to one of four creams: the Tri-Luma triple combination, or one of three dual combinations built from the same three actives, hydroquinone 4% + tretinoin 0.05%, fluocinolone acetonide 0.01% + tretinoin 0.05%, or fluocinolone acetonide 0.01% + hydroquinone 4%.[^dailymed] Treatment success was defined strictly, as a melasma severity score of zero, meaning lesions were fully cleared of hyperpigmentation, not merely improved.[^dailymed]

By that strict measure, Trial 1 found complete clearing in 32/85 (38%) of the triple-combination group, versus 12/83 (15%) for hydroquinone+tretinoin, 0/85 (0%) for fluocinolone+tretinoin, and 3/85 (4%) for fluocinolone+hydroquinone.[^dailymed] Trial 2 found a much lower clearing rate across all arms: 10/76 (13%) for the triple combination, versus 3/75 (4%) for hydroquinone+tretinoin, 3/76 (4%) for fluocinolone+tretinoin, and 1/76 (1%) for fluocinolone+hydroquinone.[^dailymed]

> Bottom line: the triple combination outperformed every two-ingredient version of itself in both trials, but the size of the effect varied a great deal between the two otherwise identically designed trials (38% versus 13% complete clearing), and even the better-performing trial left roughly 6 in 10 subjects short of complete clearing at 8 weeks.[^dailymed]

The label does not report longer-term data beyond 8 weeks, consistent with its short-term-use indication, and does not report a formal vehicle (fully inert) control arm; every arm in both trials contained at least two of the three actives.[^dailymed]

## What's In It

- **Active: fluocinolone acetonide 0.01%** (0.1 mg per gram), a corticosteroid. See [[topical-corticosteroids]] for the class-wide mechanism and risk profile.[^dailymed]
- **Active: hydroquinone 4%** (40 mg per gram), a melanin-synthesis inhibitor. See [[hydroquinone]] for the graded mechanism and evidence base.[^dailymed]
- **Active: tretinoin 0.05%** (0.5 mg per gram), a retinoid. See [[tretinoin]] for the graded mechanism and evidence base.[^dailymed]
- **Base and preservative system:** butylated hydroxytoluene, cetyl alcohol, citric acid anhydrous, glycerin, glyceryl stearate, magnesium aluminum silicate, methyl gluceth-10, methylparaben, PEG-100 stearate, propylparaben, purified water, sodium metabisulfite, stearic acid, and stearyl alcohol, as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed]

The label states Tri-Luma is contraindicated in individuals with a history of hypersensitivity to the product or any of its components, and it contains tretinoin, a teratogen that may cause embryofetal death, altered fetal growth, congenital malformations, or neurologic deficits, so use in pregnancy is limited to cases where potential benefit justifies the risk to the fetus.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **Generic triple-combination creams claim equivalence to Tri-Luma.** A randomized, placebo-controlled trial in Chinese patients with moderate to severe melasma compared a generic fluocinolone acetonide/hydroquinone/tretinoin cream against branded Tri-Luma directly.[^generic] That trial is independent evidence about the generic specifically; it does not change the branded-product trial numbers reported above, and this page does not adopt its conclusions as verified without reading the full result, so treat any equivalence claim as the generic manufacturer's or that single trial's finding rather than an established fact about Tri-Luma.[^generic]
