The Silk'n Titan AllWays is an over-the-counter radiofrequency and light device cleared in August 2023 under K230013.[^openfda] **Bottom line:** it is the exception in its category, because it submitted a real clinical study instead of bench work alone, and that study still has no control group. Where the category's evidence comes from overall is on [[home-radiofrequency-skin-tightening]].

> **The cleared indication is narrower than the box.** "The Silk'n Titan AllWays is an over-the-counter home use device intended for the non-invasive treatment of mild to moderate facial wrinkles for adult women who have Fitzpatrick skin types I-IV."[^k230013] Three limits sit in that sentence: facial wrinkles only, adult women only, and phototypes I to IV, which excludes the two deepest. The retail page sells treatment of "the face, neck, and decolletage".[^brand]

## Summary

Filed under "Regulation Number: 21 CFR 878.4810" with the regulation name "Laser Surgical Instrument For Use In General And Plastic Surgery And In Dermatology", Class II, carrying two product codes, OHS and PAY.[^k230013] A radiofrequency device sitting under a laser-surgical-instrument regulation is the same filing-versus-function mismatch documented at [[dpl-spotlite]] and [[foreo-faq-302]]; the dual code reflects that this device combines RF with optical light. Its single predicate is the firm's own earlier device, "Silk'n HST" under K162784, and the comparison table gives the RF frequency as 1 MHz for both, marked "SAME".[^k230013]

The retail product is $549.00 and the brand calls it the Titan MultiPlatform; the name AllWays does not appear anywhere in the page text, only in the URL the brand uses for it.[^brand] This page therefore does not assert that the unit shipped today is the unit in the file.

## The Rubric

**Reducing mild to moderate facial wrinkles, the cleared use**
Graded modest on preliminary evidence. The Summary reports a study "conducted at a certified clinic in the US in 32 eligible adult women", with "2 treatment sessions a week for 10 consecutive weeks, and 2 maintenance treatments that were performed 1- and 3-months following treatment end".[^k230013] The result: "a statistically significant average reduction of 1.39 Fitzpatrick scores comparing baseline to 3-month follow-up (p < 0.001), as determined by 3 blinded evaluators".[^k230013] Blinded evaluators are a genuine strength and they control for who reads the photographs, not for what untreated skin would have done over five months. The secondary claim is softer than it sounds: "statistical analysis revealed improvement of at least 1 score according to the Fitzpatrick Wrinkle and Elastosis scale for most patients", with no figure attached to "most".[^k230013]

**Tightening, lifting and fading spots, the marketed benefits**
Graded minimal on anecdote. The brand promises a "Collagen boost to firm and lift facial contours with RF technology" and says the attachment works by "reducing fine lines and wrinkles, enhancing collagen and elastin for tighter skin, improving tone, and fading sun and age spots".[^brand] The study measured a wrinkle and elastosis scale. It did not measure firmness, contour, elastin or pigment, and none of those appears in the cleared indication.

## The Evidence

**It is the exception its category needed and does not resolve.** Most devices under product code PAY argued substantial equivalence and submitted bench testing, and [[home-radiofrequency-skin-tightening]] quotes the CurrentBody Skin RF clearance stating outright that no clinical testing was performed on its device. This one ran a study. That is worth crediting, and an uncontrolled before-and-after design is also the weakest way to run one, because wrinkle scales scored against a patient's own baseline reliably improve. The honest summary is a real measurement with an unmeasured counterfactual.

**The timeline on the box does not match the timeline in the file.** The brand's first bullet is "Visibly reduces wrinkles in just 5 weeks".[^brand] The study's protocol ran twice weekly for ten weeks and read its headline result at the three-month follow-up after that, which is about five and a half months from the first session.[^k230013] A five-week claim is not something the published study reports.

**What was actually bench-tested.** The non-clinical section covers RF parameters and the temperature profile, including overheating safety.[^k230013] The brand describes the same mechanism from the user's side: a "thermal sensor and heat stabilizer pause heating at 43°C (approx. 109.4°F) and resume when the skin cools".[^brand] That is a safety control, not an efficacy feature.

**The word proven is doing a lot of work.** The retail bullet reads "Combines 3 proven technologies: Bi-Polar RF, Low-Level Laser, and Infrared heat".[^brand] The clearance file describes the study as validating "the RF and optical LED combination" as a combination.[^k230013] Nothing consulted here tests the three components separately, so none of the three is shown to contribute anything on its own.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. The cleared indication covers adult women with Fitzpatrick skin phototypes I to IV; deeper phototypes were outside what this device was cleared for, and the file reports thermal testing in a single human volunteer to check that energy deposition "is not likely to raise skin temperature to above safe, acceptable values".[^k230013]

## Common Marketing Claims

**"Visibly reduces wrinkles in just 5 weeks".** The study behind the clearance treated twice weekly for ten weeks and reported its result at a three-month follow-up.[^brand][^k230013]

**Salon technology for home use.** The brand's framing, rendered on the page with a soft hyphen inside the word technology.[^brand] The cleared indication is an over-the-counter home device for mild to moderate facial wrinkles, and nothing in the file compares it against a clinic treatment.[^k230013]

**"Combines 3 proven technologies".** The clinical study validated the combination, not the parts.[^k230013][^brand]
