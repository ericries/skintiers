AlphaRet Overnight Cream is skinbetter science's night cream built on AlphaRet, the brand's name for a retinoid molecule chemically joined to lactic acid. It is sold through physicians and authorised retailers rather than openly on shelves, and the 30 ml size carries no public list price: the retailer listing checked for this page required an account login to see it.[^skinlab]

> **Bottom line.** A genuinely well-formulated night cream whose two active halves, a retinoid ester and an alpha hydroxy acid, both have real evidence behind them as classes (see [[retinoids]] and [[glycolic-acid]]). Two things temper the marketing. No independent trial of this cream is cited, and nothing is dosed on the label. And while the branding is about the retinoid, the declared ingredient list puts free [[glycolic-acid|glycolic acid]] third of 52 while the retinoid ester sits seventeenth, so a good deal of the smoothing is likely acid exfoliation rather than retinoid activity. The claim worth discounting hardest is "a potent blend of antioxidants (Vitamin C, Hyaluronic Acid & CoEnzyme Q10)": hyaluronic acid is a humectant, not an antioxidant.[^skinlab]

## Summary

The product's identity is a single molecule. Per the brand's own description it "combines two gold standard skin rejuvenation ingredients, a retinoid and lactic acid, to create a revolutionary double-conjugated retinoid."[^skinlab] On the declared ingredient list that molecule appears as **ethyl lactyl retinoate**, seventeenth of fifty-two ingredients, which is consistent with a retinoid ester present at a low percentage; the brand does not state the percentage.[^inci]

What the marketing does not foreground is how much ordinary alpha hydroxy acid is in the jar. Sodium glycolate is second and **glycolic acid** third, immediately after water, so free glycolic acid is among the most concentrated things in the formula, well ahead of the retinoid it is sold on.[^inci] The general evidence for each is set out on [[retinoids]] and [[glycolic-acid]] and is not repeated here; the practical point is that this is as much an AHA product as a retinoid one.

The rest of the base is unusually complete for the category: [[tetrahexyldecyl-ascorbate]] (a vitamin C ester), [[niacinamide]], squalane, [[ceramides|ceramide NP]], [[peptides|palmitoyl tripeptide-1 and palmitoyl tetrapeptide-7]], hyaluronic acid, ubiquinone (CoQ10), tocopherol, bisabolol, panthenol, allantoin, cholesterol and the fatty acids linoleic and linolenic acid.[^inci] That is a sensible supporting cast for a retinoid product, and the brand's framing of it as a buffer against retinoid dryness is reasonable formulation logic.

## The Evidence

No independent, published trial of this specific cream is cited here, so its grade rests on the evidence for its two active classes plus what the ingredient order allows. Topical retinoids have the strongest evidence base in cosmetic dermatology for photoaging, and glycolic acid has real evidence for surface smoothing and tone; both are mapped with their numbers and limits on [[retinoids]] and [[glycolic-acid]].

Transferring that to this jar runs into three gaps. First, concentration: neither the retinoid ester nor the glycolic acid is dosed on the label, and effect for both classes is strongly dose-dependent, so a page cannot say how much of either is doing work. Second, the molecule: ethyl lactyl retinoate is a conjugated ester, not tretinoin, and the retinoid evidence on [[retinoids]] is heaviest for tretinoin, so a conjugate inherits a weaker claim than the branding suggests. Third, attribution: with glycolic acid third on the list and the retinoid seventeenth, an improvement in texture after four weeks is at least as consistent with acid exfoliation as with retinoid remodelling, and no cited data separates the two.

The honest summary is a well-constructed product whose actives are well-chosen and whose specific performance is undocumented.

## Common Marketing Claims

Claims quoted from the retailer listing checked for this page, which reproduces the brand's product copy. Treat all of them as unproven unless they also appear under The Evidence.

- **"Reduces the appearance of wrinkles and fine lines in as little as four weeks, with little-to-no irritation."**[^skinlab] No trial is cited for either half. Four weeks is fast for retinoid remodelling, which is part of why the glycolic acid high on the list is the more likely driver of any four-week change.
- **"A unique combination of an alpha hydroxy acid (AHA) and a retinoid ... with little-to-no irritation."**[^skinlab] The combination is real and the conjugate chemistry is a genuine formulation idea, but "little-to-no irritation" is a comparative tolerability claim with no head-to-head trial cited against a retinol or tretinoin product.
- **"A potent blend of antioxidants (Vitamin C, Hyaluronic Acid & CoEnzyme Q10)."**[^skinlab] Partly wrong as written. The formula does contain a vitamin C ester and ubiquinone, which are antioxidants, but **hyaluronic acid is a humectant, not an antioxidant** (see [[hyaluronic-acid]]), so listing it as one misdescribes the ingredient.
- **"A revolutionary double-conjugated retinoid."**[^skinlab] "Revolutionary" is positioning. The molecule is a real conjugate and the approach is legitimate, but novelty is not efficacy, and the conjugate has a thinner published record than the retinoids it is compared to.
- **"An ultra hydrating blend of squalane, niacinamide, ceramides and fatty acids ... to help counteract the dryness associated with traditional retinol-based products."**[^skinlab] The most defensible claim on the list. Those ingredients are present and that is sound formulation reasoning, though it remains reasoning rather than a measured tolerability result.
