Multi-Peptide Moisturizer is Naturium's peptide-led face moisturizer: a mid-weight, fragrance-free cream built on a glycerin, jojoba oil, squalane and dimethicone base with three palmitoylated peptides, niacinamide, a vitamin C derivative and panthenol layered in, sold in a 1.7 oz / 50 g standard size with a 3.4 fl oz jumbo option.[^1]

> A cheap, well-built basic moisturizer wearing a peptide anti-aging label. What is well supported is the boring part: its glycerin, jojoba, squalane, dimethicone and sodium hyaluronate base is made of established moisturizing ingredients that reliably hydrate. The peptide part is the modest, mostly preliminary class case set out on [[peptides]]; no independent trial tests this product, and the brand's "clinically proven" 8-week fine-lines claim is company-run and unpublished. Every claim on the page is cosmetic (appearance and feel); none is a health claim. It is listed at $20 for 1.7 oz / 50 g on Naturium's own site and $19.99 at Target.[^1][^2]

## Summary

Multi-Peptide Moisturizer is the moisturizer in Naturium's Multi-Peptide collection. Its marketed active system is what the brand calls its "multi-peptide blend" (palmitoyl tripeptide-5, palmitoyl tripeptide-1 and palmitoyl tetrapeptide-7), "encapsulated ethylated vitamin C" (3-O-ethyl ascorbic acid) and panthenol, "to target the look of fine lines and wrinkles for a more youthful looking complexion."[^1] Those actives ride on a conventional cream base of glycerin, dimethicone, jojoba seed oil, squalane and fatty alcohols, with sodium hyaluronate and niacinamide mid-list; no concentration is declared for any active.[^1]

The general case for topical peptides, a modest and mostly preliminary measured effect on the look of aging, is set out on [[peptides]]; all three of this product's peptides are palmitoylated signal-type peptides, and none is individually tested in a trial of this product ([see The Evidence](#the-evidence)). The clearest support is more basic: the base is made of well-established moisturizing ingredients, and the product is fragrance free with a brand-stated formula pH of 4.80 to 5.80.[^1]

At $20 for the 1.7 oz standard size, it is priced well below the two comparators named above: [[paulas-choice-pro-collagen-peptide-plumping-moisturizer]] costs $49 for the same 1.7 oz size, and [[cosrx-6-peptide-skin-booster-serum]] costs $25 for 150 ml.

Naturium's headline claim, that the product is "clinically proven to show an improvement in the look of fines lines and wrinkles in 8 weeks," rests on a company-run study described only in a footnote; it and the rest of the label's language, including a retailer description that names peptides not on the ingredient list, are weighed under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims). It is listed at $20 for the 1.7 oz standard size on Naturium's site and $19.99 at Target (see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-17).[^1][^2]

## The Evidence

Topical peptides are well-tolerated ingredients with a modest, mostly preliminary measured effect on the appearance of aging; that class-level case, including the pooled finding that the wrinkle signal is small and largely driven by oral rather than topical peptides, is set out in full on [[peptides]]. Nothing specific to this product was found: no published trial tests Naturium's formula, and its three peptides (palmitoyl tripeptide-5, palmitoyl tripeptide-1, palmitoyl tetrapeptide-7) are signal-type peptides that do not carry the individual controlled trials reviewed there, so the fine-lines grade above rests on the general class evidence.[^1] The brand's own 8-week clinical study is described on the product page without a published paper, sample size, or control arm, and is treated as a marketing claim below.[^1]

The supporting actives cannot be graded much higher. [[niacinamide]] and the vitamin C derivative [[3-o-ethyl-ascorbic-acid]] both appear without a declared concentration, niacinamide sitting eighth on the list, between the fatty alcohols cetyl alcohol and stearyl alcohol, so neither can be weighed against the concentrations their evidence was generated at.[^1] What the formula reliably delivers is hydration: glycerin, sodium hyaluronate and panthenol as humectants, jojoba oil and squalane as emollients, and dimethicone as an occlusive are all established moisturizing ingredients (general evidence on [[moisturizing]] and [[hyaluronic-acid]]).[^1]

> **Bottom line.** No trial isolates this product. Its fine-lines grade rests on the modest, mostly preliminary class case for topical peptides; its hydration grade rests on the separate, solid evidence for its humectant and emollient base. All of its claimed benefits are cosmetic; none is a health claim.[^1]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label, page, and retailer claims not established by product-specific evidence:

- **"Clinically proven to show an improvement in the look of fines lines and wrinkles in 8 weeks."** Naturium's own testing, footnoted only as "an 8-week clinical study on subjects (women & men), representation across all skin types & self-perceived sensitive skin," with no published paper, sample size, or control arm.[^1] Note the endpoint is the "look of" fine lines, a cosmetic claim.
- **"Nutrient-dense peptide moisturizer."** Positioning language; no active in the formula carries a declared concentration.[^1]
- **Target's description names peptides that are not on the ingredient list.** Target's product copy says the blend "includes encapsulated copper peptides and Argireline® Amplified peptide," but neither copper peptides nor acetyl hexapeptide-8 (Argireline) appears in the INCI list that both Naturium and Target themselves publish; Naturium's own page cautions that its ingredient lists "may vary slightly from our packaging and distributor websites."[^1][^2]
- **"Dermatologist Tested."** A manufacturer testing claim with no methodology, sample, or result published.[^1]
- **"Peptides help support the skin's moisture barrier"** (Target copy). The barrier-support case for peptides is thin; the formula's established barrier-relevant ingredients are its ordinary occlusives and emollients, not the peptides (see [[peptides]]).[^2]
