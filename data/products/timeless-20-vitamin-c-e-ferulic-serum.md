## Summary

Timeless 20% Vitamin C + E Ferulic Acid Serum is a morning antioxidant serum aimed at the same job as SkinCeuticals C E Ferulic: sitting under sunscreen to help neutralize everyday UV and pollution damage, alongside a gradual brightening effect. The active doing that work is [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|L-ascorbic acid]], the pure and best-studied form of vitamin C, mapped along with the other forms on [[vitamin-c]]. What distinguishes this product is not the active but the price: at about $28 it lists at well under a fifth of the SkinCeuticals price, while sharing the same three core actives, L-ascorbic acid, vitamin E, and ferulic acid, that the reference formula is built on.[^1]

The manufacturer states the serum is formulated at 20% L-ascorbic acid, "enhanced with Vitamin E & Ferulic Acid," in an opaque glass pump bottle.[^1] That concentration is higher than the 15% SkinCeuticals uses and [the studies behind that reference formula tested](#the-evidence); high-strength L-ascorbic acid can sting or irritate at the low pH these serums need, a general property of the ingredient covered on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]. Neither the manufacturer's page nor the matching Amazon listing discloses exact percentages for the vitamin E or ferulic acid; an independent dupe-list report states them at about 1% each and gives a pH of 2.4, which would put the vitamin E close to the reference formula's 1% but the ferulic acid at roughly double the reference formula's 0.5%, though that figure comes from a blogger's own account rather than a manufacturer disclosure.[^1][^2]

On reputation, Wirecutter names it the budget pick among the vitamin C serums it tested, describing it as "an affordable option with proven ingredients."[^4] Melissa Jane Lee, an independent dupe-list blogger, calls it "often... the best SkinCeuticals C E Ferulic dupe."[^2] The formulator-run blog Chemist Confessions bought it alongside four other budget dupes and put it through an accelerated stability test; [the result is reported in The Evidence](#the-evidence), where it degraded and sputtered faster than several peers but still passed.[^3] That instability is a known limitation of L-ascorbic acid serums generally, and the manufacturer's own product FAQ acknowledges it directly: it recommends using the bottle within 3 to 6 months of opening, states the serum "will slowly appear darker in color due to the natural oxidation process" as it ages, and recommends refrigerating it after opening.[^1]

Timeless lists the 1 oz (30 ml) bottle at $27.95, also selling 1.7 oz and 4 oz sizes; the same price and ingredient list independently appear on Amazon's listing (accessed 2026-08-01, see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy)).[^1]

## The Evidence

Topical L-ascorbic acid is a well-studied antioxidant with characterized roles defending skin against oxidative UV damage and supporting collagen synthesis; the vitamin C forms and their evidence are mapped on [[vitamin-c]], and the reference form's profile is [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]. The fixed-ratio combination of L-ascorbic acid, vitamin E, and ferulic acid this product is compared against was developed and clinically tested by the inventors of [[skinceuticals-c-e-ferulic|SkinCeuticals C E Ferulic]], whose page carries that evidence and its limits. No clinical trial of this product itself has been published.

Formulation research established that L-ascorbic acid needs a low pH, below about 3.5, to penetrate skin, and identified 20% as the concentration of maximal absorption efficiency.[^5] That is a general fact about vitamin C formulation chemistry and says nothing about this serum's actual performance in use.

On composition, the manufacturer confirms 20% L-ascorbic acid with vitamin E and ferulic acid but does not publish their percentages, and the Amazon listing repeats the same undisclosed figures.[^1] Melissa Jane Lee's independent dupe-list teardown reports 1% vitamin E, 1% ferulic acid, and a pH of 2.4; if accurate, that would put the vitamin E close to the reference formula's 1% but the ferulic acid at roughly double its 0.5%, though the figures rest on one blogger's account rather than a manufacturer disclosure.[^2] Both formulas are built on the same three studied actives, L-ascorbic acid, vitamin E, and ferulic acid; the manufacturer's declared list for this serum runs 11 ingredients in total.[^1]

The one product-specific check available is a stability test rather than an efficacy trial. Chemist Confessions, a blog run by two cosmetic formulators, bought this serum along with four other SkinCeuticals-style dupes and ran a three-week accelerated stability test, incubating the products in a heated oven and tracking color change, gas buildup, and packaging condition weekly. This serum showed "more rapid degradation and sputtering (where formula jets out when pumping)" than several of the other dupes tested. The authors also noted disliking "the plastic pump packaging it came in" on the unit they bought, writing that they "would recommend purchasing Timeless's new Vitamin C serum that comes in a glass airless pump on their website vs. purchasing from Amazon which still uses the previous plastic airless pump packaging." Despite the faster degradation, they concluded, "we would still pass this formula."[^3]

> **Bottom line.** A three-week accelerated heat-stability test by two cosmetic formulators found this serum degraded and sputtered faster than several other budget vitamin C dupes, though the testers still passed it. This measures whether the formula holds up in the bottle, not whether it protects or brightens skin, and it is not a clinical trial.[^3]

The manufacturer's current product page confirms the same instability is expected of the formula: it recommends using the serum within 3 to 6 months of opening, states that as it ages it "will slowly appear darker in color due to the natural oxidation process," and recommends refrigerating it after opening to help preserve potency.[^1] A 2026 capture of that page also shows the bottle is now sold in glass rather than the plastic pump Chemist Confessions tested in 2023, matching the manufacturer's statement that "all sizes now come in premium glass bottles."[^1]

## What's In It

The active antioxidants and base as declared on the manufacturer's ingredient list, which matches the Amazon listing verbatim.[^1] Ingredients with a functional role are linked to their own pages; the rest are the solvent, humectant, and preservative system.

Antioxidant actives:

- [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|L-ascorbic acid]] 20%, pure vitamin C[^1]
- [[tocopherol|alpha-tocopherol]] (vitamin E); concentration not disclosed by the manufacturer, independently reported at about 1%[^1][^2]
- [[ferulic-acid]], a plant antioxidant that stabilizes vitamin C and vitamin E in the reference formula; concentration not disclosed by the manufacturer, independently reported at about 1%[^1][^2]
- [[hyaluronic-acid|sodium hyaluronate]], a humectant[^1]

Base, solvent, and preservative system: water, ethoxydiglycol (a penetration solvent), propylene glycol (a humectant), polysorbate 80 (a solubilizer), panthenol (a skin conditioner), and benzyl alcohol with dehydroacetic acid (preservatives).[^1]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing language not established by the graded evidence above:

- **"Comparable key ingredients and concentration for a fraction of the price" versus serums "featuring this antioxidant combination [that] can cost well over $100."** The manufacturer's own comparison. The shared actives are L-ascorbic acid, vitamin E, and ferulic acid, but the manufacturer's page does not name SkinCeuticals or disclose the vitamin E and ferulic acid percentages needed to confirm an exact formula match.[^1]
- **A 21-person, third-party "clinical trial" reporting that after 28 days 81% had "an improved overall complexion," 76% had "noticeably less dull skin," and 71% found skin "noticeably brighter & smoother."** A small, self-reported consumer-perception survey rather than a published controlled trial; it records how testers rated their own complexion, not a biological or dermatologist-graded endpoint.[^1]
- **"Stimulates collagen production" and "promotes healthy cell turnover."** Manufacturer composition claims. The collagen and cell-turnover evidence for L-ascorbic acid generally is graded on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]], not established by a trial of this product.[^1]
