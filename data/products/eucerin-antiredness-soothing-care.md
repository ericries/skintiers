A short, fragrance-free cream built on two Beiersdorf actives aimed at reactive skin. **Bottom line:** the mechanism is real and the clinical evidence behind it is almost entirely the manufacturer's own, run either on provoked irritation or with no control group at all. The active's general case is on [[licochalcone-a]].

> **Read the ingredient list before the range name.** Licochalcone A is not declared on it. What the formula actually names is Glycyrrhiza Inflata Root Extract, the licorice extract that licochalcone A is the active compound within, alongside 4-t-Butylcyclohexanol, a TRPV1 antagonist. Thirteen ingredients, no fragrance and no alcohol, which for a product sold to reactive skin is the right instinct.

## Summary

The full declared list, in order: Aqua, Glycerin, Panthenol (or Dexpanthenol), Butyrospermum Parkii Butter, Cetyl Palmitate, Methyl Palmitate, Olus Oil, Pentylene Glycol, Methylpropanediol, Sodium Polyacrylate, 4-t-Butylcyclohexanol (trans-isomer), Glycyrrhiza Inflata Root Extract, Caprylyl Glycol, as listed by incidecoder, whose server-rendered page for this product is titled "Eucerin Antiredness Soothing Care ingredients (Explained)" and was read on 2026-10-25. incidecoder aggregates label text rather than publishing it, so it is credited here for the ingredient list and the photograph and is not used as evidence for any claim.

Strip the two actives out and what remains is a competent, unfussy moisturiser: water, [[glycerin]], [[panthenol]] and [[shea-butter]] in the first four slots. That matters for reading any before-and-after, because a well-made emollient reduces the look of redness on dry, compromised skin on its own.

## The Rubric

**Immediate stinging and burning relief**
Graded modest on preliminary evidence, and this is the strongest row. Beiersdorf's own development paper reports that "A formulation containing 4-t-butylcyclohexanol showed a significant immediate anti-stinging/anti-burning effect in vivo", from one of "two controlled, single-blind, randomized studies".[^sulzberger] Single-blind and controlled is a real design. The abstract gives no sample size for it, and every author is from the manufacturer's research division, so treat the direction as supported and the magnitude as unpublished.

**Visible facial redness**
Graded minimal on preliminary evidence, because of what was actually measured. For the combination the reported in vivo result is that "a cream base containing a combination of 4-t-butylcyclohexanol and a licochalcone A-rich licorice extract reduced shaving-induced erythema".[^sulzberger] Shaving-induced erythema is provoked irritation that resolves on its own. It is a reasonable model for reactivity and it is not the persistent facial redness this product is sold for.

**Rosacea**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence, despite the largest study here. An "open dermocosmetic study" applied a product with this active pair twice daily for four weeks in 1,221 subjects with "sensitive skin and rosacea stage 0-II", and reported "improvement of all symptoms of sensitive skin and rosacea in a significant number of subjects (P less than 0.001)".[^jovanovic] Open means no control group, no blinding and no randomisation, and the paper notes the product was used "in conjunction with pharmacological treatments". A p-value against baseline under those conditions cannot separate the cream from the drugs, the four weeks or regression to the mean. What actually treats rosacea is on [[rosacea]], [[rosacea-erythematotelangiectatic]] and [[rosacea-papulopustular]].

**Moisturising**
Not graded as product evidence, because none exists for this formula. The base is conventional and sensible; the ingredient cases are on [[glycerin]], [[panthenol]] and [[shea-butter]].

## The Evidence

**The mechanism is the most solid part, and it is in vitro.** The two actives were selected against two different targets: "4-t-butylcyclohexanol significantly reduced TRPV1 activation", the receptor involved in stinging and neurogenic flushing, while "Licochalcone A significantly decreased NFκB signalling and PGE2 secretion".[^sulzberger] That is a coherent pairing, one active on the nerve-signalling side and one on the inflammatory side, and cell data do not establish what a finished cream does on a face.

**Every clinical study here comes from the manufacturer.** All eleven authors of the development paper list Research and Development, Beiersdorf AG, Hamburg, and Beiersdorf owns Eucerin; the paper appeared in a journal supplement.[^sulzberger] One of its authors is also an author on the 1,221-subject rosacea study.[^sulzberger][^jovanovic] None of that makes the findings wrong. It does mean no independent group has reproduced them in the sources read here, and it is why the two redness rows above are graded below where the headline numbers would put them.

**What a sceptical reading leaves you with.** A fragrance-free, alcohol-free, thirteen-ingredient moisturiser whose actives have a plausible mechanism, an immediate anti-stinging effect from a small blinded study, and no controlled evidence for reducing persistent facial redness. For reactive skin that stings, that is a defensible purchase. As a rosacea treatment it is not supported, and [[rosacea]] has the options that are.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice. Rosacea is a medical diagnosis with prescription treatments that work, and persistent facial redness can have other causes. A cosmetic cream is not a substitute for having it assessed.
