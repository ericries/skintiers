A $22 pack of 30 individually wrapped glycolic acid wipes from Surface Deep, sold as a deodorant replacement for underarms, SKU P30.[^brand] **Bottom line:** the idea is coherent and the formula is not thin, but the brand publishes no glycolic percentage, no pH, and no evidence for the product beyond one consumer-perception figure. Glycolic acid's own evidence is on [[glycolic-acid]].

> **The strength is the missing number.** Glycolic Acid sits second on the ingredient list, directly after water, with Sodium Hydroxide fifth to neutralise part of it and Salicylic Acid sixth.[^brand] That ordering says glycolic is present in quantity. It does not say how much, and nowhere in the brand's own product record is a percentage or a finished pH given, which are the two figures that would let anyone judge how much acid is actually working on the skin.

## Summary

The mechanism the brand describes is at least the right shape for the problem. Underarm odor is produced by skin bacteria acting on sweat, and the copy's claim is that glycolic acid "Lowers the skin's pH, creating a cleaner environment so odor-causing bacteria cannot flourish" while lactobacillus is said to promote the presence of the good bacteria, the brand's own wording putting GOOD in quotation marks.[^brand] That is a plausible story rather than a demonstrated one, and the brand offers no measurement of either pH or bacteria to support it.

What it offers instead is one sentence: "In an independent consumer perception test, 91% of the subjects stayed odor free all day and 100% did not experience sensitivity."[^brand] A perception test records what users reported about a product they knew they were using. No sample size, duration, comparison arm or publication is given for it, and a 100% tolerability result from such a test is not a safety finding.

Worth reading the free-from list against the ingredients. The brand says the formula is "Free of aluminum, sulfates, parabens, phthalates, alcohol, silicone, baking soda, PEG and artificial fragrance" and lists "Non-irritating" among its claims.[^brand] The declared list contains Eucalyptus Globulus Leaf Oil and rosemary extract, so it is free of artificial fragrance while carrying fragrant botanicals, and it pairs glycolic with salicylic acid and witch hazel extract on one of the body's thinnest, most occluded patches of skin. The brand's own spray, which shares this ingredient list, warns it "May cause some stinging if used immediately after shaving".[^brand] Treat that non-irritating claim as positioning.

## The Rubric

**Underarm odor control (cosmetic)**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence, because the single perception percentage is the whole evidence base. Nothing published about this product establishes whether it controls odor, in either direction.

**Surface exfoliation of underarm skin (cosmetic)**
Graded unknown on mixed evidence. Glycolic acid at a high ingredient-list position will exfoliate something, and the general evidence sits on [[glycolic-acid]], which the site grades mid. Without a stated strength or pH, the magnitude here is not gradeable, and this product is not comparable on that axis with the leave-on glycolic products covered here that do state a figure.

## The Evidence

No controlled trial of this product was found. The transferable evidence belongs to glycolic acid rather than to these wipes, and it is graded on [[glycolic-acid]] rather than re-argued here; none of the trials behind that grade tested underarm odor.

Two things the site does cover are worth connecting. Glycolic acid on body skin for breakouts and marks is on [[body-acne]] and [[hyperpigmentation]], and the lactobacillus angle belongs with [[probiotics]], where topical ferment evidence is graded. Nothing on those pages supports an odor claim either.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined: these are Surface Deep's own promotional lines, not findings, and nothing above rests on them.

The copy opens its feature list with "Powered by a patented, dermatologist developed blend of fruit based glycolic acids".[^brand] A patent is a claim of novelty, not of efficacy, and no patent number or developer is named in the product record. The phrase "dermatologist developed" describes who formulated it rather than what it was shown to do.

The wipes are sold as "More effective, longer lasting than conventional deodorant without causing irritation".[^brand] A comparative superiority claim with no named comparator, no measurement of odor and nothing behind it but the perception test.

The mechanism bullets, which describe fruit-based glycolic acid as removing bad bacteria and lactobacillus as promoting good ones, with the brand putting both in quotation marks.[^brand] Mechanistic storytelling about the skin microbiome, offered without any microbiological measurement of this product.
