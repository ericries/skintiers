## Summary

Discoloration Defense is SkinCeuticals' dedicated dark-spot serum: a hydroquinone-free formula built around 3% tranexamic acid, 1% kojic acid, and 5% niacinamide in a 1 fl oz (30 ml) bottle.[^1] Each active has its own evidence base, covered in full on [[tranexamic-acid]], [[kojic-acid]], and [[niacinamide]], but what sets this product apart from most category peers is that its exact three-active combination, at these same percentages, matches the formula tested in a published 12-week clinical trial.[^2]

That trial, run in Brazilian women with Fitzpatrick skin types I-IV, reported "a significant improvement in the appearance of PIH, hyperpigmentation, melasma, skin texture, and skin tone homogeneity" beginning at week 2 and continuing through week 12, with melanin index (measured by Mexameter) falling significantly versus both baseline and control by week 12.[^2] The published abstract available for this page does not state how many subjects were enrolled, does not describe what the control comparison consisted of beyond that label, and does not name a funder, limits that keep this short of a gold-standard trial (see [The Evidence](#the-evidence)).

Compared with category peers such as [[good-molecules-discoloration-correcting-serum|Good Molecules' Discoloration Correcting Serum]] (4% niacinamide plus an undisclosed-strength tranexamic acid ester) and [[murad-rapid-dark-spot-correcting-serum|Murad's Rapid Dark Spot Correcting Serum]], this is the only one of the three with a published trial matching its declared formula rather than resting solely on its actives' separate, general evidence. It does not contain hydroquinone, the ingredient kojic acid is most often paired with in kojic acid's own strongest studies.[^1]

The retailer's marketing highlights, "81% decrease in post-acne marks," "60% reduction in brown patches," "59% improvement in uneven skin tone," and "20% improvement in discoloration after 2 weeks," are not the figures reported in the trial abstract itself and are weighed under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).[^1] It is listed at $115.00 for 1 fl oz (30 ml) on Dermstore, priced well above both named comparators (Good Molecules at $11.99, Murad at $88; see [Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-16).[^1]

## The Evidence

Tranexamic acid, kojic acid, and niacinamide each have their own general evidence, set out in full on [[tranexamic-acid]], [[kojic-acid]], and [[niacinamide]]: tranexamic acid's best-documented melasma benefit is oral rather than topical, kojic acid's best-documented role is as an add-on to hydroquinone or glycolic acid rather than used alone, and niacinamide's own hyperpigmentation evidence was generated in that same low-single-digit-percent range this product uses.[^1]

This combination, at these exact percentages, matches a published clinical trial rather than resting only on its actives' separate, general evidence: 3% tranexamic acid, 1% kojic acid, 5% niacinamide. Desai and colleagues evaluated "a novel, topical facial serum containing 3% TXA, 1% kojic acid, and 5% niacinamide" over 12 weeks in Brazilian women with Fitzpatrick skin types I-IV, using expert clinical grading, bio-instrumental measurements (Mexameter), and self-assessment questionnaires.[^2] They reported "a significant improvement in the appearance of PIH, hyperpigmentation, melasma, skin texture, and skin tone homogeneity" from week 2 through week 12, and a significant fall in Mexameter-measured melanin index versus both baseline and control by week 12, concluding the tested serum "is an effective and well-tolerated treatment option for addressing hyperpigmentary conditions, including melasma."[^2]

The published abstract available for this page does not give the number of subjects enrolled, does not describe what the control comparison consisted of beyond that label, and does not state who funded or ran the trial. The tested serum's exact active percentages match this product's declared formula, which makes it likely this trial functioned as the product's own validation research even though the abstract is silent on funding or sponsorship. Those gaps, plus the absence of an independent replication, keep the finding well short of gold-standard even though it is unusually specific to this product's declared formula.

> **Bottom line.** This product's graded actives, at their exact combination and percentages, match a published trial rather than resting only on general category evidence. That trial found a significant improvement in melasma, PIH, and skin tone over 12 weeks, but its abstract omits sample size, a placebo arm, and funding disclosure, and the formula match suggests likely manufacturer involvement, so treat the result as promising but not independently verified against those gaps.[^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and retailer claims not fully established by the product-specific evidence above:

- **"81% decrease in post-acne marks," "60% reduction in brown patches," "59% improvement in uneven skin tone," and "20% improvement in discoloration after 2 weeks."** Retailer-listed figures not stated in the published 12-week trial's abstract, which reports its findings qualitatively ("significant improvement") rather than with these percentages; these numbers may come from a separate, unpublished consumer self-assessment.[^1][^2]
- **"Evens skin tone in as early as 2 weeks."** Consistent in direction with the published trial's finding of improvement "beginning at week 2," but the trial does not quantify skin tone at that timepoint the way this retailer claim implies.[^1][^2]
- **Hydroquinone-free positioning.** Accurate as an ingredient fact, but kojic acid's own best-documented studies pair it with hydroquinone rather than replace it; leaving hydroquinone out is a formulation choice, not a demonstrated efficacy advantage over hydroquinone-containing formulas (on [[kojic-acid]]).
