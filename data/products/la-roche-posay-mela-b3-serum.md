La Roche-Posay Mela B3 Serum is a dark-spot serum from La Roche-Posay built around 10% niacinamide and Melasyl (2-mercaptonicotinoyl glycine), a pigment-blocking compound the brand describes as its own, sold in a 1 fl oz (30 mL) bottle at $44.99.[^target]

> Niacinamide has modest evidence, much of it from small or manufacturer-run trials, for lightening facial hyperpigmentation; that case sits on [[niacinamide]], and this product's 10% dose runs double the 4-5% concentrations used in the melasma trials behind it. Melasyl is the newer, second half of the formula: a compound La Roche-Posay's parent company L'Oreal calls proprietary to its own laboratories, for which this profile located no independently published clinical trial, only the manufacturer's own claims (see [[la-roche-posay]]).[^target]

## Summary

Mela B3 Serum pairs niacinamide, labeled at 10% on the product listing, with Melasyl, the INCI name for which is 2-mercaptonicotinoyl glycine, in a formula La Roche-Posay markets for "persistent dark spots" including sunspots, age spots, and post-acne marks.[^target]

Topical niacinamide is well tolerated and has modest evidence, much of it from small or manufacturer-run studies, for lightening facial hyperpigmentation; that general case, including a small independent trial in which 4% niacinamide performed comparably to hydroquinone 4% for melasma, is set out on [[niacinamide]]. The concentration tested in that hyperpigmentation evidence runs 4% to 5%; this product's declared 10% is double that range.

Melasyl is not an established, independently studied skincare ingredient the way niacinamide is. It is a compound L'Oreal's own annual report and registration filings describe as developed in the company's laboratories and used across La Roche-Posay's Mela B3 range; those are the manufacturer's own claims, reported on [[la-roche-posay]].[^target] This profile did not locate any clinical trial of Melasyl published independently of the manufacturer, so its contribution to this product's effect on dark spots rests on that manufacturer-driven evidence alone.

Directions on the product listing read: "Apply 3-4 drops on clean skin in the morning and evening. In the morning use with Mela B3 UV Sunscreen SPF 30."[^target] The label's other claims are weighed against the evidence under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Topical niacinamide is a well-tolerated active with modest evidence for lightening facial hyperpigmentation, much of it from small or manufacturer-run trials; the concentrations and comparators behind that evidence, and how they compare with other pigmentation actives, are set out on [[niacinamide]]. No published clinical trial of this exact formula was located.

Melasyl's evidence base, as far as this profile could establish, is entirely manufacturer-generated: L'Oreal's own corporate filings describe extensive internal research behind the compound, but this profile located no clinical trial of Melasyl in an independently published, peer-reviewed source. Those manufacturer claims and their sourcing are documented on [[la-roche-posay]] rather than repeated here, because they are the company's own positioning rather than independently verified findings.

> **Bottom line.** The niacinamide half of this formula rests on the same modest, small-trial evidence covered on [[niacinamide]], at a concentration (10%) double the range tested for hyperpigmentation. The Melasyl half rests on evidence generated and reported by the manufacturer, with no independent trial located for this profile.[^target]
