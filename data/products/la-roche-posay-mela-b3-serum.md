La Roche-Posay Mela B3 Serum is a dark-spot serum from La Roche-Posay built around 10% niacinamide and Melasyl (2-mercaptonicotinoyl glycine), a pigment-blocking compound the brand describes as its own, sold in a 1 fl oz (30 mL) bottle at $44.99.[^target]

> Niacinamide has modest evidence, much of it from small or manufacturer-run trials, for lightening facial hyperpigmentation; that case sits on [[niacinamide]], and this product's 10% dose runs double the 4-5% concentrations used in the melasma trials behind it. Melasyl is the newer, second half of the formula: a compound La Roche-Posay's parent company L'Oreal calls proprietary to its own laboratories. Peer-reviewed trials of it do exist, but every one is authored or funded by L'Oreal, run by its contracted research organization, or co-authored by consultants disclosing L'Oreal fees; a company-run RCT even tested this exact serum non-inferior to hydroquinone 4% for melasma, though the serum's 10% niacinamide means that result cannot be credited to Melasyl alone (the full evidence account is on [[melasyl]]).[^target]

## Summary

Mela B3 Serum pairs niacinamide, labeled at 10% on the product listing, with Melasyl, the INCI name for which is 2-mercaptonicotinoyl glycine, in a formula La Roche-Posay markets for "persistent dark spots" including sunspots, age spots, and post-acne marks.[^target]

Topical niacinamide is well tolerated and has modest evidence, much of it from small or manufacturer-run studies, for lightening facial hyperpigmentation; that general case, including a small independent trial in which 4% niacinamide performed comparably to hydroquinone 4% for melasma, is set out on [[niacinamide]]. The concentration tested in that hyperpigmentation evidence runs 4% to 5%; this product's declared 10% is double that range.

Melasyl is not an established, independently studied skincare ingredient the way niacinamide is. Peer-reviewed, PubMed-indexed trials of it now exist, but each traces back to the manufacturer, being authored by L'Oreal or La Roche-Posay staff, run by their contracted research organization, or co-authored by consultants who disclose L'Oreal fees; the full account, study by study, is on [[melasyl]]. Most relevant to this product, a manufacturer-run randomized trial in 109 women found this Mela B3 serum non-inferior to hydroquinone 4% for facial melasma. Because the serum combines Melasyl with 10% niacinamide, which has its own hyperpigmentation evidence, that trial measures the finished product and cannot isolate what Melasyl itself contributes.

Directions on the product listing read: "Apply 3-4 drops on clean skin in the morning and evening. In the morning use with Mela B3 UV Sunscreen SPF 30."[^target] The label's other claims are weighed against the evidence under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Topical niacinamide is a well-tolerated active with modest evidence for lightening facial hyperpigmentation, much of it from small or manufacturer-run trials; the concentrations and comparators behind that evidence, and how they compare with other pigmentation actives, are set out on [[niacinamide]]. No published clinical trial of this exact formula was located.

Melasyl's evidence base is peer-reviewed but entirely manufacturer-tied: the published trials are authored or funded by L'Oreal and La Roche-Posay, run by their contracted research organization, or co-authored by fee-disclosing consultants, with no trial independent of the company located. That literature, and the manufacturer's corporate claims, are documented study by study on [[melasyl]] rather than repeated here. The trial that speaks to this specific product, non-inferiority to hydroquinone 4% in 109 women, tested the finished Mela B3 serum, so its 10% niacinamide, not Melasyl alone, could account for the result.

> **Bottom line.** The niacinamide half of this formula rests on the same modest, small-trial evidence covered on [[niacinamide]], at a concentration (10%) double the range tested for hyperpigmentation. The Melasyl half rests on peer-reviewed but entirely manufacturer-tied evidence, including a company-run trial of this exact serum against hydroquinone that its niacinamide content prevents crediting to Melasyl alone (see [[melasyl]]).[^target]
