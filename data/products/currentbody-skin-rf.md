The CurrentBody Skin RF is a $385.99 home radiofrequency device cleared in March 2024 under K232424.[^k232424][^brand] **Bottom line:** its own clearance file states that no clinical testing was performed on it, and the brand's page puts a percentage on a benefit the device is not cleared to treat. The category's evidence, which traces to a different device entirely, is on [[home-radiofrequency-skin-tightening]].

> **The file is unusually candid.** "No clinical testing was performed with the subject device, as it is substantially equivalent to the predicate device, and thus requires no additional clinical data to prove its safety and efficacy."[^k232424] What was submitted instead is a bench experiment: porcine belly skin instrumented with temperature probes, where "Measurements showed comparable (near identical) spatio-temporal tissue heating between the subject and predicate devices."[^k232424] That is a valid equivalence argument about heat. It is not a measurement of anyone's skin.

## Summary

Filed under 21 CFR 878.4420 with the regulation name "Electrosurgical Device For Over-The-Counter Aesthetic Use", Class II, product code PAY, General and Plastic Surgery panel.[^k232424] The predicate is another company's device, the "Pollogen Stop U Model UXV device, cleared under 510(k) number K220322 on May 4th, 2023".[^k232424] That predicate is covered at [[pollogen-stop-u-tripollar]], and its own file points onward to a prescription device for efficacy. Published specifications: "Frequency: 1±0.05 MHz" and "Maximal temperature allowed: 40.5 ± 0.50C", the last rendered that way in the original where degrees Celsius is meant.[^k232424]

The brand's temperature claim matches the file, which is worth saying because it often does not: the page says the device heats skin "up to 104°F" and that its "Skin Sense Technology continuously measures skin temperature and automatically adjusts to your skin's resistance to keep skin at 104°F".[^brand] 104°F is 40°C, inside the cleared ceiling.

## The Rubric

**Reducing mild to moderate facial wrinkles, the cleared use**
Graded minimal on anecdote, because the file supplies no clinical evidence and says so. The cleared indication is narrow: "The CurrentBody Skin RF (ST030) device is an over-the-counter home-use device intended for non-invasive treatment of mild to moderate facial wrinkles for adults with Fitzpatrick skin types I-IV."[^k232424] Facial wrinkles, and phototypes I to IV, which excludes the two deepest. For contrast, [[silkn-titan-allways]] holds a clearance in the same product code and did run a study, 32 women with blinded evaluators, which is uncontrolled but is a great deal more than a porcine bench test.

**Tightening loose or sagging skin, the marketed benefit**
Graded minimal on anecdote. The brand leads with "Radio Frequency helps tighten loose, sagging skin - with 89% seeing an improvement in just 8 weeks", and repeats it as "89% saw an improvement in skin tightness (elasticity) in just 8 weeks".[^brand] Checked by string search of the page, no study, sample size, design, duration detail or citation accompanies that figure anywhere; the nearest supporting phrase is the unattributed "clinically proven, science and dermatologist backed".[^brand] A percentage without a denominator is not a result, and this one sits on a device whose clearance file states no clinical testing was performed.

## The Evidence

**The name says tightening three times over, and the indication says wrinkles.** The product is sold as the "CurrentBody Skin RF Radio Frequency Skin Tightening Device", the brand calls it an "at-home skin tightening alternative", and FDA's own Common/Usual Name field in the file reads "OTC device for skin tightening based on RF".[^brand][^k232424] None of that is the cleared indication, which stops at mild to moderate facial wrinkles. The gap between a device's name and its indication is a recurring pattern in this category and is set out at [[home-radiofrequency-skin-tightening]].

**The marketed treatment areas run past the indication.** The brand lists "forehead, frown lines, crow's feet, cheeks, smile folds, chin, jawline and neck".[^brand] The neck is not part of a facial-wrinkle indication.

**What the submission did and did not include.** Beyond the porcine bench work, the file records "No animal testing was performed with the subject device" as a separate line, and describes the device as "a non-sterile, reusable device, intended for a single user".[^k232424] Its equivalence conclusion is thermal: the device "can be regarded as thermally equivalent to the approved Pollogen STOP U Model UXV device".[^k232424]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. The cleared indication covers adults with Fitzpatrick skin phototypes I to IV, so deeper phototypes were outside what this device was assessed for, and the device works by heating tissue to a controlled ceiling.

## Common Marketing Claims

**"89% seeing an improvement in just 8 weeks".** No study, sample size or design is published alongside it on the brand page, and the device's clearance file states that no clinical testing was performed with it.[^brand][^k232424]

**"FDA cleared non-invasive at-home skin tightening alternative".** The clearance is real; what it covers is mild to moderate facial wrinkles, not tightening.[^brand][^k232424] Why cleared is not approved, and what either word buys, is on [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

**"Clinic-trusted RF technology".** A statement about the modality rather than this device. The modality's own evidence, including the single uncontrolled study the whole category rests on, is on [[home-radiofrequency-skin-tightening]].
