An $85 lactic acid serum from Sunday Riley, 30 ml, SKU GGL-30-US-FG, also sold at 15, 50 and 100 ml.[^brand] **Bottom line:** the acid this product is named and priced for sits eighth on its own ingredient list, no strength or pH is published, and the one claim the brand calls clinically proven is attached to no study. Lactic acid's own evidence is on [[lactic-acid]].

> **Eighth.** On the declared list, as INCIDecoder transcribes it, Lactic Acid comes after Water, prickly pear extract, blue agave leaf extract, lady's slipper orchid extract, cactus extract, aloe leaf extract and yeast extract. The brand calls it "High potency, purified grade lactic acid" and "super-concentrated", and publishes no percentage and no finished pH anywhere in its own product record.[^brand] Position eight is not nothing, but it is not where the active sits in a product built around it.

## Summary

The three-minute claim is the one worth slowing down on. The brand says Good Genes is "Clinically proven to immediately and significantly plump the look of fine lines and boost radiance in just 3 minutes."[^brand] Nowhere in the product record is there a study, a sample size, a design, a comparator or a publication. The endpoint is also carefully drawn: it is the look of fine lines, not the lines, which is a cosmetic appearance measure that a well-formulated hydrating serum can move on its own.

**The creator who sent this page to the queue called it a glycolic acid treatment, and she was not wrong.** Sunday Riley sells two products under this name, listed side by side in its own catalogue at the same $85 for 30 ml: the Lactic Acid Treatment, whose US SKUs carry a GGL prefix, and a Good Genes Glycolic Acid Treatment whose SKU reads GGG-30-UK-FG.[^brand] Same name, same price, different acid, different market code. Anyone comparing reviews of Good Genes is possibly comparing two formulas.

The glycolic version's copy makes a claim the lactic one does not. It says its glycolic "penetrates the surface of the skin with tiny glycolic acid molecules, sinking deeper into the skin than other forms of AHA", and describes the acid as "clean, ultra-pure, formaldehyde-free".[^brand] Glycolic is indeed the smallest common AHA, which is on [[glycolic-acid]]; framing an acid as formaldehyde-free implies a contaminant problem in competitors that the copy does not substantiate.

One label tension worth naming: the lactic version is sold as "Fragrance-Free" while its declared list includes lemongrass oil.[^brand] That is free of added fragrance compounds in the regulatory sense while carrying a fragrant essential oil.

On price, at $85 for 30 ml this works out near $2.83 a millilitre. The leave-on glycolic products covered here run about 9 cents a millilitre for [[the-ordinary-glycolic-acid-7-toning-solution]] and about 12 cents for [[pixi-glow-tonic]], both of which state their acid strength.[^brand] Those are toners rather than serums and a different acid, so this is a cost comparison rather than a like-for-like one; the point is that the cheaper products tell you how much acid you are buying and this one does not.

**The brand's three-minute plumping claim**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. Clinically proven, with no clinical study identified.

**Brightening the look of dark spots (cosmetic)**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. The brand attributes it to lactic acid with licorice root, which is 22nd of 28 on the list.

## The Evidence

No trial of this product was found, and the brand names none. What transfers is the evidence for the active, which is graded on [[lactic-acid]] rather than re-argued here.

The practical reading: this is a competently built, heavily extract-forward serum whose exfoliating claim rests on an undisclosed amount of a mid-graded acid, sold at roughly twenty to thirty times the per-millilitre price of leave-on acid products that publish their strength. Whether that is worth it is a spending decision rather than an evidence one.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined: these are Sunday Riley's own promotional lines, not findings, and nothing above rests on them.

"Clinically proven to immediately and significantly plump the look of fine lines and boost radiance in just 3 minutes."[^brand] The strongest claim on the page and the least supported, with no study named.

"High potency, purified grade lactic acid" and "super-concentrated".[^brand] Potency language standing in for a percentage.

"Good Genes visibly brightens the appearance of dark spots and discolorations".[^brand] Appearance claims, correctly hedged as the appearance rather than the pigment.
