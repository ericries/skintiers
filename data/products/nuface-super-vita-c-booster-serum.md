A $65 vitamin C serum built on ascorbyl glucoside, sold as a companion to NuFACE's microcurrent devices. **Bottom line:** better built than its sibling in the one way that matters on a label, because the active it is named for is genuinely near the top of the list. The evidence offered for the finished product is weaker than the sibling's, which is the opposite of what you would expect.

> **The named active is where it should be.** Ascorbyl glucoside is seventh of 34 ingredients, and the list is concentration-ordered through position nine before switching to alphabetical from "Acrylates/C10-30 Alkyl Acrylate Crosspolymer" at ten through "Zingiber Officinale Root Extract" at thirty.[^brand] That ordering matters: on [[nuface-super-peptide-booster-serum]] the peptides sit in the alphabetical tail where position says nothing. Here the vitamin C derivative sits in the part of the list that still carries dose information.

## Summary

NuFACE sells it at **$65.00 for 1.0 fl oz / 30 mL**, and a two-pack at **$130.00**, which is exactly twice the single price and therefore no saving at all.[^brand] The brand describes it as "This radiance-revealing serum is packed with Vitamin C and IonPlex ® to visibly fade dark spots, even skin tone, and boost your microcurrent glow"[^brand] The bottle reads "SUPER VITA-C BOOSTER" under "BRIGHTEN + CORRECT".[^photo]

The declared list runs 34 ingredients and begins "Water/Aqua/Eau, Propanediol, Glycerin, Caprylic/Capric Triglyceride, Betaine, Niacinamide, Ascorbyl Glucoside".[^brand]

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: modest, and the form is the question.** The vitamin C here is [[ascorbyl-glucoside]], a derivative, not L-ascorbic acid. Its evidence is on its own page and is thinner than the parent acid's. No percentage is published, so the page can say it is present in quantity but not how much.
- **Effect size: modest, from the ingredient that is not in the name.** [[niacinamide]] is sixth, one position ahead of the vitamin C derivative, and has the better-evidenced case for the same brightening claim. Two NuFACE serums in a row put niacinamide high and name the bottle after something else.
- **Record finding: the disclosure is worse here than on the sibling.** The two figures the brand publishes are "95% saw brighter & more even skin tone" and "83% saw improvement in appearance of dark spots", footnoted only as "Clinically-proven results based on independent clinical study".[^clinpanel] No sample size, no duration, no age range. The peptide serum's panel gives all three. Same brand, same badge, two standards.
- **Record finding: the two-pack saves nothing.** $130.00 for two 30 mL bottles against $65.00 for one works out at $2.17 a millilitre either way.[^brand] A duo listing usually implies a discount; this one is the single price doubled.
- **IonPlex is a brand term, not an ingredient.** It appears on the bottle and in the copy and not in the ingredient list, so there is nothing to check it against.[^brand][^photo]

## The Evidence

None independent. Two self-reported percentages with no study behind them is the whole of what the brand offers. The ingredient arguments are at [[ascorbyl-glucoside]] and [[niacinamide]], and [[vitamin-c]] covers the family; none is restated here.

> **Bottom line.** The better of the two NuFACE boosters on composition, and the worse on disclosure. If you want vitamin C, the question is whether you want it as a derivative at an undisclosed strength for $65.

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted from the brand's own page and **not** endorsed here.

- "packed with Vitamin C and IonPlex ® to visibly fade dark spots, even skin tone, and boost your microcurrent glow"[^brand] Packed is not a measurement, and no percentage is given anywhere.
- "95% saw brighter & more even skin tone"[^clinpanel] Self-report, with the study's size and length undisclosed.
- "Clinically-proven results based on independent clinical study"[^clinpanel] The only description of the study offered.
