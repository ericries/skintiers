REMEDY's 15% Azelaic Acid Complex Advanced Clarifying Serum is a 1 fl oz solubilised azelaic serum from REMEDY SCIENCE, the brand founded by dermatologist Dr. Muneeb Shah.[^brand] It sits alongside the other over-the-counter options on [[best-azelaic-acid-products]].

> **Bottom line.** The most transparent azelaic serum profiled here, and the number still needs reading. The brand states plainly that the 15% is a **complex**, "pairing 10% azelaic acid with 5% glycinated azelaic acid", which is more disclosure than its competitors offer.[^brand] But the trials behind azelaic acid used **15% and 20% of the pure acid**, so 10% pure plus 5% of a derivative is not the same thing as 15% azelaic acid. The brand also publishes three third-party studies with report numbers, which deserves credit, and **none of them measures acne, redness or pigment in people**: they measure skin pH, a gene, and a protein.[^brand] The genuine win is texture, since a fully dissolved formula avoids the grit of suspension products.

## Summary

The declared ingredient list runs water, **azelaic acid**, ethoxydiglycol, sodium hydroxide, propanediol, pentylene glycol, **potassium azeloyl diglycinate**, **niacinamide**, butylene glycol, 1,2-hexanediol, acetyl glucosamine, glycerin, beta-glucan, sodium hyaluronate, xanthan gum, centella asiatica extract, sodium metabisulfite, green tea leaf extract, licorice root extract, cyanocobalamin, tocopherol, phenoxyethanol and ethylhexylglycerin.[^brand]

Azelaic acid second is consistent with the stated 10%, and potassium azeloyl diglycinate seventh with the stated 5%.[^brand] **Ethoxydiglycol third** is the formulation story: it is a solvent, and it is how a famously poorly soluble acid ends up in a clear serum rather than a gritty suspension. **Niacinamide is eighth** with no percentage stated, so treat it as a supporting ingredient rather than a second active at a known dose; its own case is on [[niacinamide]].

Two absences are worth noting because the brand highlights them and they check out against the list: there is no fragrance and no propylene glycol in the declared ingredients.[^brand]

## The Evidence

No published clinical trial tests this serum, so its grades rest on the azelaic evidence plus what the label and the brand's own testing support.

**The complex is the thing to understand.** Azelaic acid's evidence, set out on [[azelaic-acid]] and [[finacea-azelaic-acid-15-gel]], comes from 15% and 20% formulations of the pure acid. This product delivers 10% pure acid, which is below that range, plus 5% potassium azeloyl diglycinate, a water-soluble derivative.[^brand] Adding those to "15%" is arithmetically fair and pharmacologically optimistic: a derivative is a different molecule with its own absorption and its own evidence, not a drop-in substitute for the acid. The honest reading is that this is a 10% azelaic serum with an extra ingredient, and the comparison that matters is against other 10% products rather than against the prescription 15% gel.

**The derivative's own support is thin, and the brand's citation shows why.** REMEDY references a study of a "trans-4-(aminomethyl) cyclohexanecarboxylic acid/potassium azeloyl diglycinate/niacinamide topical emulsion".[^brand] That is tranexamic acid, this derivative and niacinamide together in one product, so it cannot establish what potassium azeloyl diglycinate contributes on its own. Citing it is honest; it just does not carry the weight of a single-ingredient result.

**The three brand-commissioned studies are real data about the wrong endpoints.** Each is labelled with a method, a sample and a report number, which is more rigour than most brands show. Study 1 is in vivo pH-metry on human facial skin, n=11, reporting that "skin-surface pH settled to 5.2 within 60 minutes of application, versus an untreated site that held flat".[^brand] Studies 2 and 3 are ex vivo human skin: tyrosinase gene expression down 65.2% by RT-qPCR in UV-exposed cultures across "three replicates", and kallikrein-5 production down 39.5% by immunofluorescence under inflammatory stress.[^brand] All three are mechanistic or biophysical. A gene-expression change in cultured skin is several steps from a visible change in pigment, and skin-surface pH is a formulation property rather than an outcome. Nothing here reports lesion counts, redness grading or pigment measurement in people over time, which is what the prescription azelaic evidence rests on.

**What the formula does clearly win on is texture.** Crystalline azelaic suspensions are gritty and awkward to layer, which is a real adherence problem; see [[the-ordinary-azelaic-acid-suspension-10]]. A fully solubilised serum removes that, and cosmetic elegance genuinely affects whether people keep using an active.

## Common Marketing Claims

Claims quoted from the brand's own store page; treat each as unproven unless it also appears under The Evidence.

- **"A multi-tasking serum powered by a fully solubilized 15% azelaic acid complex that targets blemishes, redness, and dark spots in one step."**[^brand] The direction is supported at the ingredient level for all three (see [[azelaic-acid]], [[acne]], [[rosacea]], [[hyperpigmentation]]), but "targets" is not a measured result for this product, and the 15% is the complex rather than the acid.
- **"pairing 10% azelaic acid with 5% glycinated azelaic acid in a fully solubilized serum that helps reduce visible redness, fade dark spots, and improve blemish-prone skin"**[^brand] The composition half of this is verifiable and matches the ingredient order. The benefit half is the ingredient's reputation, not a trial of this serum.
- **"3rd Party Validated"**[^brand] True as stated and worth more than most brand claims, provided it is read for what it is. The validated studies measure pH, tyrosinase gene expression and kallikrein-5, not clinical improvement.
- **"making it easier than ever to use an active this strong every day"**[^brand] The tolerability and texture argument is the believable part. The phrase "an active this strong" leans on the 15% framing the rest of this page qualifies.
