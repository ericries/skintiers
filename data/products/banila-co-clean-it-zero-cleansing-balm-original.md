Banila Co Clean It Zero Cleansing Balm Original is a Korean oil-to-milk cleansing balm, used to dissolve makeup and sunscreen as the first step of a [[double-cleansing|double cleanse]]. Banila Co lists the 100 mL jar at $21.00 (a 25 mL mini at $8.00 and a 180 mL size at $29.00 are also sold).[^banila]

> A cleansing balm is judged by how well it removes what a water-based cleanser leaves behind, and this one is built for exactly that: emollient oils to dissolve makeup and PEG emulsifiers so it rinses off, with the removal evidence for oil cleansers set out on [[double-cleansing]]. The rest is marketing dressing: the brand's "Zero Balance Technology" is a name for that emulsifier-and-oil blend, and the "Vitamin E and Vitamin C" it advertises come from vitamin E plus an acerola extract that sits near the bottom of the list, so treat those as trace additions rather than active treatment. One practical caveat: unlike many cleansing balms, this one contains added fragrance, which is worth knowing if your skin is reactive.

## Summary

Banila Co markets Clean It Zero as its bestseller that "melts away makeup in seconds," is "Sold every 3.1 seconds globally," and is "the world's best-selling K-beauty cleansing balm," built on a proprietary "ZERO BALANCE TECHNOLOGY" the brand describes as a "proprietary blend of actives that effectively cleanses skin deeply without disturbing the skin's natural moisture balance."[^banila] You massage it onto dry skin, add water to emulsify it into a milk, then rinse.

The formula leads with two emollient esters (ethylhexyl palmitate and cetyl ethylhexanoate) as the cleansing oils, followed by PEG-20 glyceryl triisostearate and PEG-10 isostearate, the emulsifiers that let an oil balm rinse clean, plus synthetic wax for the solid balm texture, hot-spring water, vitamin E, several botanical extracts, and added fragrance. It is listed at $21.00 for the 100 mL jar at Banila Co ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-09-25).[^banila]

## The Evidence

The product-specific claim worth checking is the removal one, and it is directionally right for the format: as set out on [[double-cleansing]], an oil-based cleanser removes water-resistant sunscreen that a foaming water-based cleanser leaves largely behind, so an oil balm is a sound first step on heavy-sunscreen or long-wear-makeup days. That controlled evidence is a single small study, and no independently published trial of this exact product is cited here; its case rests on the format. The brand's "Zero Balance Technology" and "without disturbing the skin's natural moisture balance" are its own positioning, and the advertised "Vitamin C" is an acerola fruit extract low on the ingredient list rather than a meaningful vitamin C treatment (the vitamin C evidence, and what a real dose looks like, is on [[vitamin-c]]).

> **Bottom line.** A competent, pleasant oil-to-milk cleansing balm whose real value is dissolving makeup and sunscreen, a job the double-cleanse evidence supports. It is a cleanser, not a treatment; the "vitamin C for a healthy complexion" framing overstates a trace ingredient, and the added fragrance makes it a worse pick than a fragrance-free balm for easily-irritated skin.
