## Summary

A fragrance-free drugstore anti-ageing cream sold on three named actives, Pro-Retinol, Hyaluronic Acid and Vitamin C, at $27.99.[^brand] **Bottom line:** the ingredient list is more interesting than the marketing, because the three actives on the front of the box are 25th, 30th and 31st of 41, the ingredient sitting fifth is one the brand barely mentions, and part of the brightening benefit is declared on the list as colour-index pigment. The one clinical study the brand cites for "clinically proven" could not be located in PubMed.

> **The advertised actives are near the bottom; the unadvertised one is near the top.** In the declared order, [[niacinamide]] is fifth, ahead of every active the brand names.[^brand] [[capryloyl-salicylic-acid-lha]] is 19th, caffeine 24th, [[ascorbyl-glucoside]] 25th (the Vitamin C), sodium hyaluronate 30th (the Hyaluronic Acid) and retinyl palmitate 31st (the Pro-Retinol).[^brand] Ingredient order is not concentration, but it does bound it, and a formula whose headline trio begins at position 25 is selling a story its own list ranks last.

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: unknown, for the headline wrinkle claim.** The claim is that it "visibly reduces wrinkles and fine lines, improves firmness and elasticity, and brightens dull skin in just one week", and the page labels that clinically proven.[^brand] Its cited support is a paper titled "Multi-Faceted Evaluation of a Facial Cream Across Aging and Targeted GLP-1 Users" in the Journal of Dermatology and Skin Science.[^brand] That journal has an NLM Catalog record and four articles indexed in PubMed, and a PubMed search for that title returned nothing, so the study could not be read and none of its numbers are reproduced here.[^pubmed] This is not a claim that the study is bad or absent. It is a statement that a shopper cannot check it from what the brand published.
- **Evidence quality: anecdotal.** What is checkable on the page is perception: "97% of women considering their 1st procedure were satisfied" in a consumer test after four weeks, and three out of four GLP-1 users agreeing their skin looks firmer in a consumer study after four weeks of twice-daily use.[^brand] Satisfaction and agreement are self-reports, and the page gives no sample size for either.
- **Effect size: minimal, for Pro-Retinol.** Pro-Retinol here is retinyl palmitate, a retinyl ester, 31st of 41 declared ingredients.[^brand] The evidence for that ingredient is on [[retinyl-palmitate]], graded weak, and no percentage is published for it. Anyone buying this expecting what retinol does should compare it with [[loreal-paris-revitalift-0-3-pure-retinol-serum]], the same brand's product that states a retinol percentage on the label.
- **Composition note: some of the brightness is pigment.** The list declares CI 77891 / titanium dioxide at position 22, synthetic fluorphlogopite at 16 and tin oxide near the end.[^brand] CI 77891 is a colour index designation, and synthetic fluorphlogopite is a synthetic mica. Those are light-scattering materials that change how skin looks the moment the cream is on, which is a different mechanism from the gradual pigment effect [[ascorbyl-glucoside]] is graded weak for. A cream can honestly do both; this page separates them because the marketing does not.
- **Effect size: not applicable; a record finding.** The fragrance-free claim holds on the list, which declares no fragrance or parfum.[^brand] Claims about tolerance are a different matter: the page says "Dermatologist-tested" and "Suitable for all skin types, including sensitive", and publishes no protocol or result for either.[^brand]

## The Evidence

There is no independent trial of this product that could be read for this page. The brand's own cited study was not retrievable, and the rest of the support is consumer perception.

What can be said comes from the ingredients, each of which has its own page here, so none of it is re-argued: niacinamide carries the strongest case of anything in the formula and is the one the marketing does not lead with; [[capryloyl-salicylic-acid-lha]] and [[ascorbyl-glucoside]] are both graded weak; [[retinyl-palmitate]] is graded weak; [[caffeine]] is graded weak and appears at 24th. [[hyaluronic-acid]] has real evidence as a humectant, and sodium hyaluronate at position 30 of 41 is not where a formula puts its workhorse.

The honest summary is that this is a competent, fragrance-free, niacinamide-forward moisturiser wearing an anti-ageing trio on the front of the box. If the niacinamide is what you want, it is unusually well placed here. If Pro-Retinol is what you came for, it is the 31st thing in the jar.

## Common Marketing Claims

> The claims in this section are quoted from L'Oreal Paris's own product page and are **not** endorsed here. They are reproduced so a reader can see what is being asserted and what supports it.

- "A clinically proven anti-aging face moisturizer that visibly reduces wrinkles and fine lines, improves firmness and elasticity, and brightens dull skin in just one week."[^brand] The cited study could not be located in PubMed.[^pubmed]
- "Visibly Reduces up to 10 years of wrinkles" in four weeks.[^brand] No measurement method, scale or sample is given for how years of wrinkles were counted.
- "Formulated with dermatologist-recommended, science-backed ingredients."[^brand] Neither phrase is defined on the page.
- "I recommend Triple Power to anyone looking to visibly fight signs of aging. It is clinically proven to reduce wrinkles and fine lines and improve skin quality."[^brand] This is attributed on the page to Dr. Michelle Henry, Board-Certified Dermatologist. It appears in the brand's own marketing for its own product, which makes it a promotional endorsement rather than an independent assessment, and nothing further about the arrangement is stated on the page or asserted here.
- "97% of women considering their 1st procedure were satisfied" and "3/4 GLP-1 Users Agree Skin Looks Firmer", footnoted on the page to a consumer test and a consumer study, both after four weeks.[^brand] Perception, not measurement.
