## Summary

A $9.99 petrolatum-based foot ointment built on salicylic acid and urea.[^brand] **Bottom line:** the keratolytic pairing is sound and the brand has a genuine randomised trial in its history, but neither fact is quite what the current label offers. The one trial tested a formulation stated as 5% salicylic acid and 10% urea; today's product publishes no strength for either, and the figures on its page are consumer self-report presented under the words "clinical study".

> **The strengths that made this product worth studying are no longer on the label.** A 1998 randomised paired comparison set a Kerasal ointment described as "5% salicylic acid and 10% urea ointment" against 12% ammonium lactate, and that trial is covered on [[keratolytics-compared]].[^trial] The current product page gives "Ingredients: White petrolatum, PEG-8, glycerin, urea, PEG-40 sorbitan peroleate, salicylic acid, PEG-40 stearate, polysorbate-80" and no percentage anywhere.[^brand] Urea is fourth on that list and salicylic acid sixth, both behind white petrolatum, PEG-8 and glycerin.

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: modest.** Both actives have evidence of their own, [[salicylic-acid]] graded good and [[urea]] graded mid, and white petrolatum leading the list means the base is doing occlusive work regardless. For thickened foot skin that combination is a reasonable thing to reach for, and [[calluses-and-corns]] covers what keratolytics do and do not achieve there.
- **Evidence quality: mixed, and the good evidence is historical.** The randomised trial in this lineage is real, and it is the reason the product has a place on this site at all. What it tested was a stated-strength formulation. A reader cannot check whether today's ointment matches it, because today's ointment states nothing.[^brand][^trial]
- **Effect size: unknown, for the one-day claim.** "See visible results in 1 day with a formula featuring clinically proven ingredients" is the headline.[^brand] The support offered is that "In a clinical study, over 90% of people saw improvement after just 1 use, and 93% said their feet felt more hydrated after 1 use", and separately that "In a study, 96% of users said they would recommend this product to a friend".[^brand] People saying their feet felt more hydrated is self-report; people saying they would recommend a product is satisfaction, not efficacy at all. No study is named and no sample size is given.
- **Record finding: this is labelled as a cosmetic, not a drug.** The page presents a plain "Ingredients:" list with no Drug Facts panel, no active-ingredient heading and no purpose line.[^brand] Searches of openFDA's drug listing directory for the brand name Kerasal, and for its labeller, return no record of this product; the only Kerasal drug listing found is an athlete's foot soak whose actives are aluminium sulfate and calcium acetate, and none of the labeller's fourteen returned records is this ointment.[^ndc] Salicylic acid and urea at drug strengths would normally sit in a Drug Facts panel; here they sit in a cosmetic list.
- **Claim worth naming: a seal sits beside the efficacy figures.** "Approved by the American Podiatric Medical Association" appears alongside them.[^brand] That is a seal programme rather than a trial result, and nothing read for this page connects it to any measured outcome.

## The Evidence

The 1998 trial is the whole of it, and it belongs to [[keratolytics-compared]], which records its design, its attrition and its result; it is not re-argued here. Two things about it bear on this page. It compared a Kerasal ointment against [[ammonium-lactate]] rather than against nothing, so it speaks to relative performance among keratolytics. And it tested stated strengths, which is exactly what the current product withholds.[^trial]

Nothing found for this profile tests the formulation now being sold.

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted from Kerasal's own product page and **not** endorsed here.

- "See visible results in 1 day with a formula featuring clinically proven ingredients designed to moisturize, exfoliate, and smooth dry skin."[^brand] Clinically proven attaches to the ingredients, not to this product.
- "In a clinical study, over 90% of people saw improvement after just 1 use, and 93% said their feet felt more hydrated after 1 use."[^brand] Unnamed study, no sample size, and the second figure is explicitly about how feet felt.
- "In a study, 96% of users said they would recommend this product to a friend."[^brand] A recommendation rate is a satisfaction measure.
- "Approved by the American Podiatric Medical Association"[^brand] A seal programme, placed beside efficacy figures.
