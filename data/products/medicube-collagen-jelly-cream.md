Medicube's Collagen Jelly Cream is a viral K-beauty gel moisturizer, the sibling of the brand's Triple Collagen Serum, built on a water-and-glycol base thickened into a jelly texture, with three collagen forms, niacinamide, adenosine, and a long tail of trace botanical extracts further down the list. YesStyle, an authorized Medicube retailer, lists the 110 mL jar at $22.90, marked down 41% from a $39.00 list price.[^yesstyle]

> The "jelly" glow is a humectant-and-polymer effect, not a collagen effect. Water leads the list, followed by four glycols, propanediol, dipropylene glycol, butylene glycol, and methylpropanediol, then a gel-forming acrylate crosspolymer eighth on the list; that combination is what gives the cream its bouncy, light-reflecting texture and the transient "glass skin" look. Three collagen forms, collagen, hydrolyzed collagen, and soluble collagen, sit between thirteenth and seventeenth on the list, but topical collagen, whatever its form, is a molecule too large to penetrate to the dermis and rebuild the skin's own collagen network. A board-certified dermatologist reviewing this exact cream reaches the same conclusion, and also warns that its occlusive film could trap sebum on oily, acne-prone skin.[^sugai] The one well-evidenced active here is niacinamide, ninth on the list (see [[niacinamide]]); adenosine sits much further down with only thin evidence of its own (see [[adenosine]]), and the rest of the list is a long run of trace "superfood" botanical extracts, chlorella, garlic, oat, broccoli, tomato, spinach, blueberry, salmon egg, and wine among them, that read as label decoration at their list positions rather than evidenced actives.

## Summary

Medicube markets Collagen Jelly Cream as "an anti-aging jelly cream formulated with collagen-focused ingredients to support firmer-looking skin, improved elasticity and more defined facial contours," describing it as "infused with freeze-dried collagen with a low molecular weight to help the formula absorb more efficiently into the skin" and "powered by niacinamide to brighten dull-looking skin, even skin tone, minimize the appearance of pores and promote a smooth, glass-skin glow."[^yesstyle] It is also sold as "enriched with a moisture-boosting trio of hydrolyzed elastin, squalane and hyaluronic acid to deliver deep hydration and keep dryness in check."[^yesstyle] Read against the ingredient list, the collagen forms sit in the middle of a 48-ingredient formula, after a humectant-and-glycol base and a gel-forming polymer, and the "hyaluronic acid" in that trio is hydroxypropyltrimonium hyaluronate, a conditioning-agent form of the ingredient rather than plain hyaluronic acid ([full list below](#whats-in-it)).

The formula leads with water, then four glycols (propanediol, dipropylene glycol, butylene glycol, methylpropanediol) and two more humectant/solvent ingredients, before an acrylate crosspolymer thickener eighth on the list gives the formula its jelly set. Niacinamide is ninth. Collagen, hydrolyzed collagen, and soluble collagen run from thirteenth to seventeenth, interleaved with a long run of trace botanical extracts, chlorella, a traditional Chinese herb (Cynanchum atratum), hollyhock flower, garlic, oat, Brazil nut, broccoli, green tea seed, salmon egg, tomato, spinach, blueberry, and wine, that continues to position twenty-seven. Adenosine appears thirty-fifth, and squalane, hydroxypropyltrimonium hyaluronate, soluble proteoglycan, and hydrolyzed elastin are concentrated near the end of the list, ahead of the thickeners carbomer and xanthan gum and a closing fragrance.

It is listed at $22.90 for the 110 mL jar (41% off a $39.00 list price) at YesStyle, an authorized Medicube retailer; a 50 mL mini is also sold separately at $16.90 ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-09-22).[^yesstyle]

## The Evidence

No independently published controlled trial tests this exact cream. Medicube's marketing states that "the formula is dermatologist tested," a claim referring to testing the brand has not published for outside review.[^yesstyle]

The ingredient-level evidence is set out on the linked ingredient pages. Niacinamide, ninth on the list, has general evidence at a mid tier for its studied uses; this product discloses no concentration, so the strength of that general evidence cannot be assumed to transfer at whatever dose is used here (see [[niacinamide]]). Adenosine's topical anti-wrinkle evidence comes from two small trials run or funded by the companies selling the product tested, both measuring surface appearance rather than a biological change in skin (see [[adenosine]]). Hyaluronic acid in its plain forms has strong general evidence as a surface humectant, but the form here, hydroxypropyltrimonium hyaluronate, is a cationic derivative used mainly as a conditioning and film-forming agent rather than the plain-acid form the strongest evidence covers (see [[hyaluronic-acid]]).

Collagen itself, whatever the form, is a large protein or protein fragment that does not cross the stratum corneum to rebuild the skin's own dermal collagen network; its cosmetic role is as a humectant and film former on the surface, the same limitation covered generally on [[peptides]] for other collagen-signaling ingredients. A board-certified dermatologist's review of this exact cream reaches the same conclusion: its collagen ingredients are not signal peptides and cannot penetrate to the dermis to rebuild lost collagen the way a retinoid can, so any firming effect is short-term surface plumping rather than structural change; the same review also cautions that the cream's occlusive, film-forming texture could trap sebum underneath on oily or acne-prone skin.[^sugai]

> **Bottom line.** A fun, glowy, sensorial jelly moisturizer whose glassy plumping effect comes from its glycol-and-polymer base, not from its low-list collagen ingredients, which cannot reach the dermis. The one active with meaningful independent evidence is niacinamide, at an undisclosed concentration, and the brand's own elasticity and firmness figures are short-term, brand-run measurements rather than an independent trial. Retinoids are the topical actives with demonstrated collagen-stimulating effect, which this cream does not contain (see [[retinoids]]); the dermatologist's caution about the occlusive film applies to oily or acne-prone skin.[^sugai]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing terms not established by the graded evidence above:

- **"Infused with freeze-dried collagen with a low molecular weight to help the formula absorb more efficiently into the skin."** A smaller molecular weight helps collagen sit on the surface more evenly; it does not make the molecule small enough to cross the stratum corneum and reach the dermis.[^yesstyle][^sugai]
- **"An anti-aging jelly cream formulated with collagen-focused ingredients to support firmer-looking skin, improved elasticity and more defined facial contours."** No evidence here shows this formula's collagen ingredients change the skin's structure; the effect described is consistent with surface hydration and a temporary, light-reflecting film.[^yesstyle]
- **"The formula is dermatologist tested."** A brand claim referring to testing that is not publicly available for independent review; no published controlled trial of this exact product exists.[^yesstyle]
- **"Enriched with a moisture-boosting trio of hydrolyzed elastin, squalane and hyaluronic acid to deliver deep hydration and keep dryness in check."** The "hyaluronic acid" here is hydroxypropyltrimonium hyaluronate, a conditioning-agent derivative that sits near the end of the ingredient list, not the plain-acid form with the strongest independent hydration evidence.[^yesstyle]
