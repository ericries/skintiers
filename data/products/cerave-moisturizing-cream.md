CeraVe Moisturizing Cream is a fragrance-free cream whose ingredient list includes petrolatum, glycerin, three ceramides (ceramide NP, AP, EOP), and hyaluronic acid (as sodium hyaluronate), sold inexpensively in a tub or pump.[^3]

> A cheap, fragrance-free barrier moisturizer for dry and sensitive skin. Keeping dry, barrier-impaired, and eczema-prone skin moisturized reduces flares and spares topical steroids, one of dermatology's better-evidenced routines, and this is a faithful example of that kind of formula (see [[moisturizing]]). Skin doctors quoted in the press recommend it and call it budget-friendly. The one study of this exact cream was too small to prove its own effect, and in the closest controlled test the ceramide angle did not beat a plain moisturizer.[^1][^2][^6]

## Summary

CeraVe Moisturizing Cream is a thick, fragrance-free moisturizer made for dry and sensitive skin. Its main job is a health one: slowing the water skin loses through its surface and helping a dry or damaged barrier hold onto moisture. It does that with a familiar mix of an occlusive ([[petrolatum]]), humectants ([[glycerin]] and [[hyaluronic-acid]]), and three [[ceramides]], the fatty molecules that make up part of the skin's own barrier. Keeping dry, barrier-impaired, and eczema-prone skin moisturized this way is one of the best-evidenced routines in dermatology; that general case is set out on [[moisturizing]]. The soft, cushioned feel it leaves is cosmetic, a secondary benefit rather than the point.

What sets it apart from plain petroleum jelly or a basic lotion is the ceramide content and the dermatologist backing, not a unique active. It is heavily recommended by skin doctors. A Florida dermatologist quoted by NewBeauty counts it among the products she has "sworn by" for over a decade "to protect and reinforce the skin barrier," and a New York dermatologist in the same piece says she prefers "a cream over a lotion for treating dry skin"; a Boston facial plastic surgeon there calls it "a nice budget-friendly hydrating moisturizer."[^6] It also holds the National Eczema Association's Seal of Acceptance, a voluntary program for products shown suitable for eczema and sensitive skin ([see Tolerability](#tolerability)).[^4] These are the sources' own opinions and the NEA's program criteria, attributed here, not a SkinTiers verdict.

The thinner part of the case is the ceramide angle specifically. The one published trial of this exact cream (Leshem 2020) found its effect on the skin barrier too small to be statistically significant, and the trial was underpowered; the closest controlled test of a ceramide cream (Spada 2021) improved a barrier measurement but reduced eczema no more than a plain vehicle did. Those product-specific limits are in [The Evidence](#the-evidence), and the general case for a ceramide-and-humectant moisturizer is on [[moisturizing]]. It is inexpensive: the manufacturer's product page lists it at about $15 ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28).

## The Evidence

Keeping dry, barrier-impaired, and eczema-prone skin moisturized reduces flares and spares topical corticosteroids, one of dermatology's better-evidenced routines; that general case, including the roles of occlusives, humectants, and barrier lipids like [[ceramides]], is set out on [[moisturizing]] and in the [[van-zuuren-2017-emollients-moisturisers-eczema|2017 Cochrane review]], which found most moisturizers gave some benefit for eczema and that no single moisturizer was shown to beat another. What is specific to this product is narrower.

Leshem 2020 was a within-patient, observer-blind, controlled RCT of four OTC moisturizers (Cetaphil, Aveeno, CeraVe Moisturizing Cream, and Vaseline) in atopic dermatitis. For barrier function, "mean TEWL improved in the treated forearm and worsened in the untreated one, but the difference was not significant," and "the study was underpowered as recruitment fell short."[^1]

> **Bottom line.** In the one study of this exact cream, its own effect on the skin barrier was too small to be statistically significant, and the study was too small to draw a firm conclusion.

Spada 2021 was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (n=100, 28 days) of a ceramide-dominant cream against a vehicle, industry-funded by the cream's maker (Ego Pharmaceuticals). The ceramide cream improved transepidermal water loss versus the vehicle (mixed-model P=.0342). The change in eczema severity between groups was not significant (EASI, P=.7804).[^2]

> **Bottom line.** A ceramide cream improved a barrier measurement, but it did not reduce eczema any more than a plain vehicle did. This was a trial funded by the cream's own maker.

## What's In It

The full ingredient list (INCI) as declared by the manufacturer [^3]. The ingredients with a functional role are linked to their own pages; the rest are the base, texture, and preservative system.

Barrier and hydration ingredients:

- [[petrolatum]] and [[dimethicone]], occlusives that slow water loss
- [[glycerin]] and sodium hyaluronate ([[hyaluronic-acid]]), humectants
- three [[ceramides]] (ceramide NP, AP, EOP), plus [[cholesterol]] and [[phytosphingosine]], the formula's barrier lipids
- [[tocopherol]] (vitamin E), an antioxidant

Base, texture, and preservative system: water, caprylic/capric triglyceride (emollient), cetearyl alcohol and cetyl alcohol (emulsifiers), ceteareth-20, behentrimonium methosulfate and sodium lauroyl lactylate (emulsifiers), carbomer and xanthan gum (thickeners), phenoxyethanol and ethylhexylglycerin (preservatives), disodium EDTA (chelator), and potassium phosphate and dipotassium phosphate (pH buffers).

Not in the formula: no [[niacinamide]] (which some other CeraVe products contain), no added fragrance or essential oils, and no drying alcohol (the cetearyl and cetyl alcohols are emollient fatty alcohols, not the drying kind).[^3]

## Tolerability

CeraVe Moisturizing Cream holds the National Eczema Association Seal of Acceptance.[^4] The NEA describes the Seal as a voluntary program: the manufacturer applies and submits ingredient details and clinical safety-test reports for sensitivity, irritation, and toxicity, and an accepted product must be fragrance-free and free of the ingredients on the NEA "Ecz-clusion List." The NEA states the Seal reflects suitability for eczema and sensitive skin, and that "a product that works for one may not work for another."[^5]

CeraVe labels the product non-comedogenic and allergy-tested.[^3] These are manufacturer label claims, not independently tested outcomes.
