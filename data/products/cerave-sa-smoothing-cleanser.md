CeraVe SA Smoothing Cleanser is a fragrance-free facial wash containing salicylic acid, three ceramides (NP, AP, EOP), niacinamide, and hyaluronic acid, sold on CeraVe's own site for $15.99 and, in an 8 fl oz size, for $12.99 on Target.[^1][^2]

> A cheap, fragrance-free cleanser that pairs a genuine OTC acne and keratolytic active, salicylic acid, with CeraVe's usual ceramide-and-niacinamide base, marketed for rough and bumpy skin rather than for wrinkles or dark spots.[^1][^2] Salicylic acid has real evidence as a second-tier acne treatment and a stronger case as a keratolytic, [set out on [[salicylic-acid]]](#the-evidence), but this formula does not disclose its concentration, and a rinse-off wash gives the acid and the barrier ingredients only brief contact with the skin.[^1][^2]

## Summary

CeraVe SA Smoothing Cleanser is a fragrance-free facial wash built around salicylic acid, marketed for "bumpy and rough skin," which it is said to help "soften and smoothen," a narrower positioning than an anti-wrinkle or brightening claim.[^2] It is sold for $15.99 on CeraVe's own site, though that page does not state a size; Target sells an 8 fl oz bottle of the same formula for $12.99.[^1][^2] Both sources describe it as fragrance-free and non-comedogenic; Target's listing also describes it as oil-free and paraben-free.[^1][^2]

The labeled active is [[salicylic-acid]], the oil-soluble beta-hydroxy acid that sits in the US OTC drug monograph as an acne active at 0.5% to 2% and as a wart-removal keratolytic at higher concentrations; it has real but second-tier evidence for acne (behind benzoyl peroxide and topical retinoids) and its strongest evidence as a keratolytic, [detailed under The Evidence](#the-evidence). Neither the manufacturer nor Target discloses this formula's salicylic acid percentage.[^1][^2]

Around the acid, the formula carries CeraVe's familiar barrier base: three [[ceramides]] (NP, AP, EOP), niacinamide, and hyaluronic acid (as hydrolyzed hyaluronic acid), the same combination CeraVe uses across its cleanser line. A cleanser's job is mostly a rinse-off one, so how much of that barrier support survives contact with the skin before it goes down the drain is not something a trial of this product has measured, [discussed under The Evidence](#the-evidence).

The manufacturer's own directions call for wetting skin with lukewarm water, massaging the cleanser in "a gentle, circular motion," and rinsing.[^1]

## The Evidence

Whether salicylic acid works, and how well, depends on the use: it is a real but second-tier acne active behind benzoyl peroxide and topical retinoids, and its best-evidenced use is as a wart-removal keratolytic, both covered in detail on [[salicylic-acid]]. No published trial tests this exact cleanser, or its combination with ceramides, niacinamide, and hyaluronic acid.

> **Bottom line.** The salicylic acid case for this cleanser rests on the category-level evidence for the ingredient, not on a trial of the product itself, and because neither source discloses a concentration, there is no way to know how its dose compares to the 0.5% to 2% range the acne monograph covers.[^1][^2]

The marketed claim that the cleanser helps "soften and smooth" rough, bumpy skin is a manufacturer positioning claim,[^1] corroborated in substance by Target's independent listing describing the same "bumpy and rough skin" use and the near-identical wording "soften and smoothen,"[^2] but neither source reports an independent trial of this cleanser measuring skin texture or bumpiness.[^1][^2]

For the ceramides, the closest controlled test is a manufacturer-sponsored randomized trial of a *different* brand's ceramide-dominant cream, which improved a barrier measure (transepidermal water loss) but did not reduce eczema severity more than a plain vehicle; that trial, its industry funding, and its limits are set out on [[ceramides]]. Niacinamide's barrier evidence (raised stratum-corneum lipids and lower water loss in dry skin) comes from a single manufacturer research group; that work and its limits are on [[niacinamide]]. Neither ingredient's percentage is disclosed in this formula.

## What's In It

The full ingredient list (INCI), declared identically on the manufacturer's own product page and Target's retailer listing.[^1][^2] The ingredients with a functional role are linked to their own pages; the rest are the base, texture, and preservative system.

Actives and support ingredients, in the order declared:

- Water, cocamidopropyl hydroxysultaine (mild surfactant), glycerin (humectant), sodium lauroyl sarcosinate (mild surfactant)
- [[niacinamide]], fifth on the list, a form of vitamin B3
- Gluconolactone, a polyhydroxy acid/humectant
- PEG-150 pentaerythrityl tetrastearate and sodium methyl cocoyl taurate, mild surfactants
- Zea mays (corn) oil, an emollient
- Three [[ceramides]] (ceramide NP, AP, EOP), plus cholesterol and phytosphingosine later in the list, the formula's barrier lipids
- Carbomer, a thickener
- Calcium gluconate and sodium chloride, thickening/stabilizing agents
- [[salicylic-acid]], sixteenth on the list, no concentration disclosed
- Sodium benzoate, sodium lauroyl lactylate
- Cholecalciferol (vitamin D)
- Hydrolyzed hyaluronic acid, a humectant

Base, texture, and preservative system: phenoxyethanol (preservative), disodium EDTA and tetrasodium EDTA (chelators), xanthan gum (thickener), and ethylhexylglycerin.[^1][^2]

The formula is free of added fragrance, confirmed independently on both the manufacturer's and Target's listings.[^1][^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing terms not established by the graded evidence above:

- **"Provides gentle, non-irritating exfoliation without harsh microbeads."**[^1] A formulation/positioning claim; neither source reports an independent trial of this cleanser's tolerability against a microbead-based scrub.
- **"Helps soften and smooth the skin without disrupting its protective barrier."**[^1] Corroborated in substance by Target's independent "bumpy and rough skin" / "soften and smoothen" description, but neither source reports an independent trial of this cleanser measuring texture or barrier function.[^2]
- **"Enhanced with antioxidant vitamin D."**[^1] Cholecalciferol appears near the end of the ingredient list; neither source reports an independent trial of this cleanser's antioxidant effect.
- **"Developed with dermatologists."**[^2] A development-credential claim on Target's listing; neither source names the dermatologists or describes their role.
- Niacinamide is not accompanied by a specific marketing claim on either page; its better-evidenced topical use is barrier support, covered on [[niacinamide]].
