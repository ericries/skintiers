Ketoconazole shampoo, 2% is the prescription-strength version of the antifungal shampoo sold over the counter as [[nizoral-anti-dandruff-shampoo-ketoconazole-1|Nizoral 1%]], twice the concentration of active drug in the same class of leave-on-then-rinse antifungal shampoo.[^dailymed] It is a single-use or short-course treatment, not a routine wash: the current FDA label directs "one application of the shampoo should be sufficient."[^dailymed]

> A real prescription strength-up from the OTC product, not merely a marketing distinction. The FDA label's own placebo-controlled trial data show a single application clearing the fungal infection in 78% of cases (versus 11% on placebo) and a three-day regimen clearing it in 84% (versus 11%), for the drug's actual approved use, tinea versicolor, not dandruff.[^dailymed] The catch for anyone buying this to treat dandruff or seborrheic dermatitis instead: that is off-label. It is well-supported by class evidence, including a head-to-head Cochrane comparison showing 2% beats 1% for that condition too, but it is not what this product is FDA-labeled to treat.[^cochrane]

## Summary

The active drug is the same [[ketoconazole|ketoconazole]] found in the OTC 1% Nizoral shampoo, just at double the concentration, and here it is regulated as a prescription drug rather than an OTC monograph product. Today's DailyMed labels for 2% ketoconazole shampoo, filed by generic manufacturers such as ANI Pharmaceuticals and Padagis, describe it plainly: "Ketoconazole Shampoo, 2% is a red-orange color liquid for topical application, containing the broad spectrum synthetic antifungal agent ketoconazole in a concentration of 2% in an aqueous suspension."[^dailymed] The brand-name "Nizoral" identity on this strength is largely historical; the Rx 2% product available today is dispensed as generic ketoconazole shampoo, 2%, while the Nizoral brand itself currently markets only the OTC 1% strength.

The FDA-approved indication is narrower than most people expect from an "anti-dandruff shampoo" name: "Ketoconazole shampoo, 2% is indicated for the treatment of tinea (pityriasis) versicolor caused by or presumed to be caused by Pityrosporum orbiculare (also known as Malassezia furfur or M. orbiculare)."[^dailymed] Tinea versicolor is a distinct fungal skin condition, not the same thing as dandruff or [[seborrheic-dermatitis|scalp seborrheic dermatitis]], though all three involve the same Malassezia yeast family. This is a genuinely well-evidenced, single-use drug for its actual labeled condition, with a health grade of notable and established evidence resting on the manufacturer's own placebo-controlled trial data.

## The Evidence

**Tinea (pityriasis) versicolor, the labeled use.** The current label cites a three-arm, parallel, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in patients with moderately severe tinea (pityriasis) versicolor, all mycologically confirmed at baseline. Quoting the label verbatim: "Successful response rates in the primary efficacy population for each of both three-day and single-day regimens of ketoconazole shampoo, 2% were statistically significantly greater (73% and 69%, respectively) than a placebo regimen (5%)... Mycological clearing rates were 84% and 78%, respectively, for the three-day and one-day regimens of the 2% shampoo and 11% in the placebo regimen."[^dailymed] The label notes the difference between the two active regimens (one day versus three days) was not itself statistically significant, meaning a single application performed about as well as three days of use.[^dailymed]

**Scalp seborrheic dermatitis, off-label for this product.** This shampoo is not FDA-labeled for dandruff or seborrheic dermatitis at all, unlike the OTC 1% Nizoral, which does carry a dandruff Drug Facts label. The class evidence for ketoconazole in seborrheic dermatitis is nonetheless strong: a 2015 Cochrane review of topical antifungals pooled 12 ketoconazole trials (3,253 participants) and found ketoconazole gave a "31% lower risk of failed clearance of rashes compared with placebo (risk ratio (RR) 0.69, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.59 to 0.81, eight studies, low-quality evidence)" at four weeks, with remission similar to topical steroids and 44% fewer side effects.[^cochrane] That review's own head-to-head "Ketoconazole (2%) versus ketoconazole (1%)" section, built on the same randomized trial cited on the OTC 1% product's own page, found the 2% strength significantly outperformed 1% (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.80).[^cochrane] Anyone using this 2% product off-label for dandruff or seborrheic dermatitis is, on the evidence, using the more effective of the two Malassezia-active concentrations, just not for the condition the label covers.

> **Bottom line.** For its actual FDA-approved use, tinea versicolor, this is a well-evidenced, near-single-dose treatment. For dandruff or seborrheic dermatitis it is off-label, though backed by solid class evidence showing it beats the 1% OTC strength for that condition too.

## What's In It

Active ingredient: ketoconazole, 2%, in an aqueous suspension, described on the label as "a red-orange color liquid for topical application."[^dailymed]

Other ingredients, quoted verbatim from the ANI Pharmaceuticals 2% label: "coconut fatty acid diethanolamide, disodium laureth sulfosuccinate, FD&C Red No. 40, hydrochloric acid, imidurea, PEG-120 methyl glucose dioleate, purified water, sodium chloride, sodium hydroxide, and sodium lauryl ether sulfate."[^dailymed] Unlike the OTC 1% version, this label is a prescription structured product listing rather than an OTC Drug Facts panel, and it discloses no added fragrance in that ingredient list. It does contain FD&C Red No. 40 dye, which accounts for the described red-orange color.[^dailymed]

## Common Marketing Claims

There is no active brand marketing specific to this Rx 2% strength: the "Nizoral" consumer brand today promotes only the OTC 1% shampoo, and the 2% product circulating under prescription is generic. Two labeling points worth adjudicating on their own terms:

- **"One application of the shampoo should be sufficient."** This is the label's own directive, not a marketing claim, and it is backed by the trial data above showing single-day and three-day regimens performed similarly.[^dailymed] It is honest given the cited numbers, but it applies to tinea versicolor specifically, not to ongoing dandruff control.
- **Implicit "prescription strength equals better dandruff control."** Nothing on this drug's own label makes that claim; it is a reasonable inference from the separate Cochrane 2%-versus-1% comparison, not something this product's FDA labeling asserts.[^cochrane]
