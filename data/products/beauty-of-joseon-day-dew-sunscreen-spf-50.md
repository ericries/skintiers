The US-market sunscreen from a brand whose Korean sunscreens built its reputation. **Bottom line:** the sharpest gap between a label and a measurement anywhere on this site. Independent testing put its average SPF at 19 against a label of 50, while scoring its UVA protection at the top of the scale.

> **Tested SPF 19 against a label of 50.** Consumer Reports' lab testing records the US Day Dew formula with a UVA score of 5, a UVB score of 2, and an average tested SPF of 19.[^cr] The same table gives the brand's Korean Relief Sun formula a UVB score of 5 and an average tested SPF of 36 against its 50+ label.[^cr] Same brand, same marketing language, roughly half the measured sunburn protection.

## Summary

### What is actually in it

The actives are the familiar US organic filter set: "AVOBENZONE 3.0%", "HOMOSALATE 7.0%", "OCTISALATE 5.0%" and "OCTOCRYLENE 5.0%".[^brand] No modern UVA filter of the kind the brand's Korean formulas use, because the US has not approved any since the 1990s, which [[us-sunscreen-filters-by-what-fda-proposed-in-2019]] covers.

That filter set explains the shape of the result rather than excusing it. Avobenzone is a strong UVA filter and the UVA score of 5 reflects that. The UVB load is carried by homosalate, octisalate and octocrylene, and it is the UVB side that the measurement found wanting.

## The Rubric

**Sunburn protection: the label and the measurement disagree, badly.** SPF is a UVB measure, so the tested 19 against a labelled 50 is the number that matters for burning. At SPF 19 you are not getting nothing; you are getting roughly a third of what the bottle advertises. One independent lab, one test, and this page does not claim the result has been replicated. It is also not a trivial discrepancy, and it comes from a tester with no commercial stake.

**UVA protection: genuinely good.** A 5 out of 5, equal to the Korean formula that this site rates well.[^cr] Anyone dismissing this product outright should notice that half of what a sunscreen does, it does properly.

**The cosmetic extras are decoration.** Niacinamide, panthenol, sodium hyaluronate and rice extract appear in the inactive list without concentrations.[^brand] Nothing on the page supports a benefit from them.

**Price.** $12.60 for 50 mL at the brand's own store, which is inexpensive for a facial sunscreen.[^brand] A second variant is listed at $21.60 and the structured data does not say what size it is, so this page does not guess.

## The Evidence

One independent measurement, which is one more than almost any sunscreen on this site has. Consumer Reports tested Korean formulas against their US counterparts and found the same pattern across three brands: the US versions scored 2 for UVB where the Korean versions scored 5.[^cr] Day Dew is not an isolated failure so much as the brand's entry in a category-wide finding.

What this page does not have: any test the manufacturer commissioned or published. The brand states an SPF 50 on the tube and does not publish a protocol, which puts it on the bottom rung of [[sunscreens-by-what-the-maker-discloses-about-spf-testing]] along with forty-three others.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined, not endorsed. The brand's product imagery carries a Skin Cancer Foundation "Seal of Recommendation" badge reading "EFFECTIVE SUN PROTECTION", alongside a "GLOBAL NO.1 K-Beauty SUN CARE BRAND" sales claim sourced to Euromonitor and a Marie Claire US readers' choice award.[^imagery] A sales ranking is a statement about units sold. A seal programme is a statement about an application meeting a programme's criteria. Neither is a measurement of this product's SPF, and one independent measurement of that SPF came in at 19 against a label of 50.

## Who it is for

Someone who wants cheap, cosmetically pleasant UVA cover and treats the SPF number with suspicion. If you are relying on SPF 50 to avoid burning, the brand's Korean Relief Sun measured nearly twice as high, and the honest move is to use more product or pick something else.
