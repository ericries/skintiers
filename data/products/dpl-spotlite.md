The dpl SpotLite is a small blue-and-red LED acne device from LED Technologies, the firm behind both the dpl and reVive lines. **Bottom line:** it delivers the one home acne modality with a decent evidence grade, and its own FDA file contains no evidence that it works. The modality case is on [[home-led-light-therapy]], and everything below is specific to this clearance.

> **The only human study in the file is about whether people can switch it on.** The Summary reports that "A Usability Study was conducted with 16 participants", of whom "100% of the participants were able to use the device successfully", and then concludes that "The conclusions drawn from nonclinical tests demonstrate that the device is safe, as effective, and performs as well as the legally marketed devices".[^k183118] Sixteen people operating a device correctly is a usability result. Nothing in the file measures acne.

## Summary

Cleared 2019-02-07 as K183118, product code OLP, class II.[^openfda] The indication is one sentence: "The dpl® SpotLite is an Over-the-Counter (OTC) device intended for use in treatment of mild to moderate inflammatory acne."[^k183118] Note **inflammatory** acne, which means the red and inflamed kind rather than the clogged pores covered at [[blackheads-clogged-pores]]. "The device uses two types of LEDs, 630nm red and 415nm blue", is applied directly to the skin, and runs three minutes per treatment, with "no user settings or adjustments required".[^k183118] No price is quoted on this page; the brand's own site is behind a Cloudflare bot block and no metered lookup was spent to get past it.

## The Rubric

**Mild to moderate inflammatory acne**
- **Effect size: modest.** The 415nm blue plus 630nm red pairing is exactly the combination that earned the best grade in the 2026 review of home devices: "blue light-emitting diodes (LED) and combination blue-red LED devices demonstrated moderate quality evidence (Level B) with favorable safety profiles and Grade I recommendations".[^grade] That is a real grade and this device really does emit those two wavelengths. It is also a grade for a modality, assigned by a review that "identified clinical studies evaluating home-use devices" and names no manufacturer or model.[^grade]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Not because the modality is poorly studied, but because this unit contributes nothing to it. The file's own summary sentence calls its conclusions nonclinical, and the testing it lists is bench and safety work: IEC 60601-1 and 60601-1-2 for electrical safety and electromagnetic compatibility, ISO 10993-5 cytotoxicity, ISO 10993-10 intracutaneous reactivity and skin sensitisation, plus software validation.[^k183118] All of that speaks to whether the device is safe to hold against skin. None of it speaks to blemishes.

**Dose, which the file does not disclose**
- **Effect size: not applicable; a gap in the record.** Light dose is the variable that decides whether an LED device does anything, and the comparison table names the unit without ever giving a number. The submission claims the device "Has the similar output (mW/cm²) as predicate device", while the irradiance row of its own predicate chart reads only "LED" for both devices.[^k183118]
- **Evidence quality: not applicable.** A reader cannot work out from this file how much light the device delivers, so cannot check it against the doses used in the published literature.

## The Evidence

**The predicate is the maker's own earlier device.** The Summary names a single predicate, the "reVive Light Therapy® LED Cleansing System" under K180447, cleared eight months earlier, also under OLP, also by LED Technologies.[^k183118][^openfda] That device has a page here, and its clearance was nonclinical too. So the equivalence argument runs from one of the firm's products to another of the firm's products, and no clinical evidence enters at either step. The comparison itself is thin by design: matching wavelengths of 415nm and 630nm, three minutes per treatment, and OTC status.[^k183118] The conclusion is also carefully attributed in the document, which says "the manufacturer asserts that no significant differences exist between the subject device and predicate".[^k183118] That is the submitter's assertion, written by the submitter, and the clearance accepts it.

**An LED device cleared under a laser regulation.** FDA's clearance letter files this under "Regulation Number: 21 CFR 878.4810" with the regulation name "Laser Surgical Instrument For Use In General And Plastic Surgery And In Dermatology".[^k183118] The device contains no laser. This is a quirk of how the agency classifies light-emitting devices, and it is worth knowing before a marketing page describes a product as laser-grade on the strength of its paperwork. What clearance does and does not mean is covered at [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

**Fifteen clearances, three for acne.** A query of every LED Technologies 510(k) returns 15 records, all filed as Summaries rather than Statements, which is better transparency than several firms covered here manage.[^openfda] Only three sit under OLP, the acne code: the predicate K180447, this device, and K210968. The other twelve are wrinkle or general codes. The firm publishes its files; what the files contain is a different question.

**Where this sits among the acne devices here.** The full-face masks [[neutrogena-light-therapy-acne-mask-plus]] and [[pulsaderm-acne-mask-28]] use the same modality over the whole face for longer sessions, which is a different exposure from three minutes in one place, and the published studies behind the Level B grade were not run on a device of this format. [[revive-light-therapy-lux-spot]] is the closest comparison in format, and is in a weaker position still: no clearance of any kind names it.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. The cleared indication is mild to moderate inflammatory acne; moderate to severe or scarring acne is a reason to see a dermatologist rather than buy a light, and the treatment ladder is on [[acne]].
