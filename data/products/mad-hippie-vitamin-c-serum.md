Vitamin C Serum is Mad Hippie's best-selling 30 ml facial serum, a [[vitamin-c]] product built not on L-ascorbic acid but on the derivative [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]] (SAP), paired with vitamin E and [[ferulic-acid]] in an echo of the studied C E Ferulic combination.[^madhippie]

> The C plus E plus ferulic trio here borrows the framing of the best-studied vitamin C formula, 15% L-ascorbic acid with 1% alpha-tocopherol and 0.5% ferulic acid (see [[best-vitamin-c-serums]]), but every component differs: the vitamin C is the derivative SAP rather than L-ascorbic acid, the vitamin E is declared as tocotrienol rather than the studied tocopherol, and none of the three concentrations is disclosed.[^madhippie] SAP is genuinely more stable and gentler, but it must be converted to ascorbic acid by skin enzymes before it can act, and no vehicle-controlled human trial of SAP for brightening or wrinkles was located; its best human evidence is for [[acne]], a use this serum is not marketed for (see [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]]).[^woolery][^geesin]

## Summary

Mad Hippie positions Vitamin C Serum as "a stable vitamin C serum that visibly brightens, smooths, and helps improve the look of uneven tone," aimed at "aging, sun damaged and acne-prone skin."[^madhippie] The stability claim is the honest core of the product: SAP stays stable near neutral pH, where plain ascorbic acid degrades in solution, which is why derivative serums avoid the low-pH sting and short shelf life of L-ascorbic acid formulas.[^geesin] The tradeoff, covered in full on [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]], is that SAP is inactive as applied and depends on skin phosphatase enzymes to release free ascorbic acid, a conversion demonstrated in a mouse-skin model but never measured directly in human skin.

The formula around the SAP is humectant-forward: [[hyaluronic-acid]], glycerin, konjac root powder, and aloe, plus chamomile, clary sage, and grapefruit seed botanical extracts, with vitamin E (as tocotrienol) and [[ferulic-acid]] as companion antioxidants ([full list below](#whats-in-it)).[^madhippie] No concentration is declared for any active, including the SAP itself; the acne trials that anchor SAP's evidence used 5%.[^madhippie][^woolery]

The honest read: a well-tolerated derivative serum, priced similarly to several 15% L-ascorbic acid serums on [[best-vitamin-c-serums]], that is gentler and more shelf-stable than an L-ascorbic acid formula, but whose brightening and anti-aging claims rest on markedly weaker evidence than those L-ascorbic acid products. Buyers choosing between forms should read [[vitamin-c]] for the field map; the evidence hierarchy of the forms is laid out on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]] and [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]].

It costs $33.99 for 30 ml (1.02 fl oz) on the brand's own site ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-09-13).[^madhippie]

## The Evidence

No published trial tests this product. The brand's product page cites a "Clinical study" of 34 subjects in which "94% Saw Faded discoloration and brighter skin In 8 weeks," but no publication, protocol, or control arm is identified, so it reads as an unpublished brand-run study with a subjective endpoint.[^madhippie]

The ingredient-level evidence is set out on [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]]; in brief, SAP's strongest human data are for acne: a randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled 12-week trial of 5% SAP lotion in 50 subjects found "statistically significant improvement when compared to vehicle in all of the parameters measured," with tolerability similar to vehicle, and a separate randomized comparison found 5% SAP roughly matched 0.2% retinol on inflammatory lesion counts at 8 weeks.[^woolery][^ruamrak] For the brightening and anti-aging uses this serum is actually marketed on, no SAP-specific vehicle-controlled human trial was located; those claims are extrapolated from L-ascorbic acid's evidence base (see [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]) plus animal conversion data. The stability advantage is real and measured, but it is a solution-chemistry finding, not a demonstrated skin benefit.[^geesin]

Whether SAP's acne evidence transfers to this bottle is unknowable from the label: the trials used 5%, and Mad Hippie does not disclose its concentration.[^madhippie][^woolery]

> **Bottom line.** A stable, gentle derivative serum with a pleasant humectant base; the vitamin C form inside has real randomized evidence for acne at 5% (a use and concentration this product neither claims nor discloses) and no controlled human evidence for the brightening and antiwrinkle benefits on the label. If the goal is the photoprotection and collagen evidence that made vitamin C serums famous, that case belongs to L-ascorbic acid formulas (see [[best-vitamin-c-serums]]); if the goal is a no-sting daily antioxidant serum for sensitive or blemish-prone skin, the tolerability and stability data above (not the brightening or antiwrinkle claims) are what support it.[^madhippie][^woolery][^geesin]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by independent, product-specific evidence:

- **"Visibly brightens" with "C + E + ferulic acid" and "Reduces wrinkles" with "SAP vitamin C."** The C E Ferulic framing borrows the credibility of studies run on 15% L-ascorbic acid with 1% alpha-tocopherol and 0.5% ferulic acid; this formula substitutes SAP for the L-ascorbic acid, tocotrienol for the tocopherol, and discloses no concentrations. No SAP-specific controlled human trial for brightening or wrinkles was located.[^madhippie]
- **"94% Saw Faded discoloration and brighter skin In 8 weeks. Clinical study, N = 34."** Unpublished, no control arm or protocol identified, subjective endpoint.[^madhippie]
- **SAP "absorbs efficiently and is more stable than L-ascorbic acid," staying "potent through the bottle."** The stability half is measured chemistry: SAP is stable near neutral pH where ascorbic acid is not.[^geesin] But stability in the bottle is not efficacy on skin; SAP must still be enzymatically converted to ascorbic acid, and that conversion has only been quantified in an animal model (see [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]]).
- **"Kind to acne-prone skin."** Ironically the best-supported SAP claim, though the supporting trials used a declared 5% concentration this product does not state, and tolerability in the vehicle-controlled trial was similar to placebo.[^woolery]
