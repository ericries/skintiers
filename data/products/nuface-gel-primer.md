The NuFACE Gel Primer is the conductive gel you are told to spread on your face before switching the device on, and it holds an FDA clearance of its own, K161654, decided 2016-12-30.[^openfda] **Bottom line:** that clearance is real, and it is about electricity. FDA reviewed this gel as an electroconductive medium, the filing's own conclusion claims equivalence in safety alone, and no test in the file measured anything about how skin looks. The brand's current gel page calls the product "FDA-approved" in one paragraph and "FDA CLEARED" a few lines above it.[^brand] Which word applies, and why the difference matters, is argued at [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

> **The cleared indication is one sentence, and it is about conduction.** "The NuFACE Gel Primer is intended to be used with NuFACE microcurrent devices to improve skin conductivity."[^k510k] The device description adds that the gel "is to be applied to the area under an electrode to reduce the impedance of the contact interface between the electrode surface and the skin."[^k510k] A conductor between an electrode and skin is the entire claim FDA cleared.

## Summary

K161654 sits under product code GYB and 21 CFR 882.1275, whose regulation name is "Electroconductive Media", in Class II.[^k510k] openFDA records the classification name as "Media, Electroconductive" with a medical specialty of Neurology, and the clearance letter is signed out of FDA's "Division of Neurological and Physical Medicine Devices".[^classification][^k510k] It was filed as a Traditional 510(k) with a published 510(k) Summary rather than a Statement, which is why the basis is readable at all.[^openfda] Of the 14 clearances Carol Cole Company holds, this is the only one in the electroconductive-media code; the other 13 are the devices.[^openfda] The gel NuFACE sells in this role today is the Aqua Gel Activator, $35.00 for 1.69 oz, introduced by the brand with the words "Designed to help conduct microcurrent down to the muscle".[^brand]

## The Rubric

**Improving skin conductivity, the cleared use**
Graded modest on a preliminary base. The grade reflects a conductivity specification that was set and met, not a measured skin outcome, and it is deliberately no higher because no value was published. Conductivity is one of five characteristics listed as tested, alongside physical and chemical properties, biocompatibility, packaging compatibility and stability, and the reported result is that "The test results demonstrate that the NuFACE Gel Primer meets the established specifications."[^k510k] A specification met is a pass or fail, not a magnitude. No conductivity number, impedance reduction or comparison against any other gel appears in the Summary.

**Hydrating and plumping, the marketed benefit**
Graded minimal on anecdote. The current product is sold on hyaluronic acid and the company's IonPlex electrolyte blend, and the brand says the gel "doubles as a hydrating gel mask".[^brand] Nothing consulted here measured hydration from this gel. [[hyaluronic-acid]] and [[glycerin]] carry their own grades, earned in their own trials, and neither transfers to a specific product by being on its label.

**Driving current into muscle, the marketed benefit**
Graded minimal on anecdote. The brand's own FAQ says the gel "is FDA-Cleared to ensure your device glides smoothly while helping the microcurrent reach deep into the facial muscles."[^brand] The first half of that sentence has a basis in the file, which says the viscosity "permits the microcurrent device to easily glide across the skin for optimum results."[^k510k] The second half does not. Depth is not in the indication, not in the device description, and not in anything tested.

## The Evidence

**The conclusion claims safety, and only safety.** The Summary's predicate comparison section opens by saying the gel "is substantially equivalent to the predicate device in safety and effectiveness", then the Conclusion two pages later says it "is substantially equivalent to the predicate device in safety."[^k510k] The word effectiveness is dropped. For a product whose job is to conduct, that is a narrower finish than the setup promised, and it is the company's own wording in the document FDA cleared.

**The predicate is an electrotherapy cream, not a cosmetic.** Equivalence is claimed to the Prizm Medical Thera-Cream, K032239, described in the comparison table as "a conductive cream, which increases skin conductivity for electrotherapy treatment and was developed for use with the Prizm brand electrotherapy units and the Intelligent Textiles brand of garment electrodes."[^k510k] The lineage of a facial primer therefore runs back through garment electrodes. That is the same pattern as the devices it pairs with, which are cleared under a nerve-stimulator-for-pain regulation, documented at [[nuface-mini-microcurrent-device]].

**The conductive ingredient is a salt, and the filing names it.** The gel is "a clear, viscous and chloride-free formulation" whose conductive material is given as "Salt (Magnesium Sulfate)", against the predicate's "Conductive Copper Salts".[^k510k] The common or usual name the company supplied is "Electroconductive gel".[^k510k] The file lists packaging as "2 fl. oz. and 5 fl. oz. tube" and the intended user as "Adults 18 years of age or older".[^k510k]

**The cleared name is not the name on sale, and the published ingredient lists differ.** Nothing NuFACE currently sells is called Gel Primer; the brand's FAQ asks "Can I use this as gel primer or a moisturizer?" about the Aqua Gel Activator, and the store files it under the product type Primer.[^brand] INCIDecoder publishes magnesium sulfate, the salt the clearance names, in the list for the discontinued NuFACE Hydrating Leave-On Gel Primer, and does not publish it in the list for the Hydrating Aqua Gel, which instead shows sodium citrate, disodium phosphate, sodium phosphate and sodium nitrate. Those are ingredient lists compiled by a third party, not regulatory filings, and a cleared device's formula can change without a new submission, so this is not evidence that any clearance is void. It does mean the gel in the tube and the gel in the file cannot be assumed identical, and this page does not assume it. Sizes differ too: the file says 2 and 5 fl. oz., the store sells 1.69, 3.3 and 10 oz.[^k510k][^brand]

**What a conductive gel is actually for.** Microcurrent devices need a conductive medium, which is why the 2019 Mini filing states that the device requires the use of a conductive gel or medium during treatment, quoted at [[nuface-mini-microcurrent-device]]. Buying the gel is therefore sensible if you own the device. What the clearance does not do is add evidence to the device, and the device's own case, graded minimal, is at [[nuface-trinity-microcurrent-device]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. The gel was tested for cytotoxicity, skin irritation and skin sensitization under ISO 10993 and is described as biocompatible for surface contact with intact skin.[^k510k] It is meant for intact skin only, and anyone with a pacemaker or other implanted electrical device should read the device manufacturer's contraindications rather than a general page.

## Common Marketing Claims

**FDA-approved.** Not accurate for this product. It is 510(k) cleared, which is a substantial-equivalence finding against an earlier device, and the brand's own page says "FDA CLEARED" a few lines above the paragraph that says approved.[^brand] [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]] explains the gap.

**Helping the microcurrent reach deep into the facial muscles.** The brand's wording is "helping the microcurrent reach deep into the facial muscles". No depth claim is cleared and none was tested. The cleared wording stops at skin conductivity.[^k510k][^brand]

**Powered by IonPlex.** The page says the gel is "powered by IonPlex", a brand name for the gel's electrolyte content.[^brand] The clearance names magnesium sulfate as the conductive salt; nothing consulted graded an electrolyte blend against a plainer conductive gel on any skin outcome.
