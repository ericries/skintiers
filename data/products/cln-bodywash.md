CLn BodyWash is a dermatologist-developed medicated body wash built on sodium hypochlorite, the antibacterial active in dilute-bleach bath therapy, marketed for skin prone to eczema, folliculitis, body acne, and hidradenitis suppurativa, sold at about $36.00 for 8 fl oz.[^cln]

> This is dilute-bleach bath therapy repackaged as a convenient daily wash, and the concept has real but contested evidence. A 2022 GRADE meta-analysis concluded bleach baths "probably improve" atopic-dermatitis severity, but a 2017 meta-analysis found they were no better than plain water baths, raising the question of how much is the bleach versus the bathing.[^bakaa][^chopra] It is also a rinse-off wash rather than a soak, and no independent trial of this exact product was found, so the brand's "clinically proven" numbers are its own. Judge it as a reasonable, physician-designed way to apply a mild antibacterial to problem skin, not a proven upgrade over gentle washing.[^cln]

## Summary

CLn (pronounced "clean") built this wash around the same idea as a dilute-bleach bath: the brand says it "harnesses the most important ingredient of medicated baths" in a daily cleanser that "won't ruin clothes and is gentle on the skin."[^cln] The active is sodium hypochlorite, which the brand calls "the active cleansing ingredient," and the product is positioned for eczema and atopic dermatitis, folliculitis, body acne, itch, redness, and hidradenitis suppurativa.[^cln]

The general evidence for dilute sodium hypochlorite in eczema lives on [[atopic-dermatitis]]; what matters here is that it is genuinely mixed. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis with GRADE assessment found that in moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis, "bleach baths probably improve clinician-reported severity by a relative 22%," with about one in ten improving severity by 50%.[^bakaa] But an earlier 2017 meta-analysis that specifically compared bleach against water baths found "no significant differences" between them on the Eczema Area and Severity Index or body surface area, meaning much of the benefit may come from bathing itself rather than the bleach.[^chopra] This is a rinse-off wash used for two minutes rather than a soak, a further step removed from the bath studies.

## The Evidence

No independent, controlled trial of CLn BodyWash itself was located; its evidence is inferred from the sodium-hypochlorite class plus the brand's own testing. Two health uses are graded above.

**Eczema / atopic dermatitis.** Sodium hypochlorite reduces skin bacterial load, and reducing *Staphylococcus aureus* colonization is a plausible mechanism in atopic dermatitis. The class evidence is real but does not cleanly separate the bleach from the bath: the 2022 meta-analysis supports a modest severity benefit versus no bleach, while the 2017 meta-analysis found bleach baths no better than water baths.[^bakaa][^chopra] The brand states the wash "is clinically proven to reduce the appearance of skin redness, dryness, and flakiness seen with eczema when used for 2 minutes a day," with "a visible difference in as few as 3 uses"; no independent methodology, control arm, or publication is given, so those figures are held in Common Marketing Claims.[^cln]

**Folliculitis, body acne, and Staph-colonized skin.** The antibacterial rationale is strongest here: lowering surface bacterial load is a sensible approach to recurrent folliculitis, body acne, and the flares of hidradenitis suppurativa the brand targets. But this rests on mechanism and clinical practice rather than controlled trials of this wash.[^cln]

> **Bottom line.** A physician-designed, convenient way to apply a mild antibacterial to eczema-, folliculitis-, or HS-prone skin, standing on genuine but contested bleach-bath evidence.[^bakaa][^chopra] Worth trying if bacterial overgrowth drives your flares, but do not expect it to clearly beat plain gentle bathing, and note it is a sulfate-based wash despite the "gentle, non-drying" positioning.[^cln]

## What's In It

Full ingredient list, verbatim from the brand: "Water (Aqua), Sodium Laureth Sulfate, Cocamide MEA, Cocamidopropyl Betaine, Sodium Hypochlorite, Disodium EDTA."[^cln] Two honest notes: sodium hypochlorite (the bleach active) is distinct from the hypochlorous acid in sprays like Tower 28's SOS (see [[hypochlorous-acid]]); and despite the "gentle, non-drying" claim, the primary surfactant is sodium laureth sulfate, a standard sulfate cleanser, not a sulfate-free base.[^cln]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Clinically proven to reduce the appearance of skin redness, dryness, and flakiness seen with eczema when used for 2 minutes a day," "a visible difference in as few as 3 uses."** The brand's own claim; no independent methodology, control arm, or publication is disclosed on the product page.[^cln]
- **"Dermatologist-developed," "developed by physicians," "hypoallergenic," "gentle, non-drying."** Positioning and tolerability claims, not efficacy; the formula is sulfate-based.[^cln]
- **"Free of steroids, antibiotics, parabens, and triclosan," "safe for ages 6 months and older."** Formulation and safety statements, not evidence the wash outperforms gentle bathing.[^cln]
