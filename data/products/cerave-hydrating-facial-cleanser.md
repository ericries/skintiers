> A cheap, fragrance-free, non-foaming cleanser for normal to dry skin. A cleanser's health-relevant job is to remove dirt, oil, and makeup without stripping the skin barrier, and this is a low-surfactant, non-stripping wash that also leaves ceramides, hyaluronic acid, and glycerin behind. Dermatologists quoted in the press recommend it for dry and sensitive skin, and it holds the National Eczema Association seal. ## Summary

CeraVe Hydrating Facial Cleanser is a gentle, fragrance-free, non-foaming cleanser made for normal to dry skin. Its job is a health one: removing the dirt, oil, and makeup of the day without stripping the skin barrier that keeps moisture in and irritants out. It is a low-surfactant wash built on mild nonionic cleansers, with [no sulfate detergents and no added fragrance](#whats-in-it), the ingredients most often blamed for the tight, stripped feeling a harsher cleanser can leave.[^1] It also leaves behind a little [ceramide, hyaluronic acid, and glycerin](#whats-in-it), the same barrier and humectant ingredients that CeraVe builds its moisturizers around.

Two separate questions decide how much that is worth. The first is a category question: does a gentle, ceramide-carrying cleanser do more for the skin than any mild cleanser would? That is answered at the category level rather than by this product. Keeping a dry or barrier-impaired complexion moisturized is one of dermatology's better-evidenced routines, but that case rests on leave-on moisturizers, not rinse-off washes; the general evidence for it is on [[moisturizing]], and for the barrier lipids on [[ceramides]]. Whether a cleanser that mostly rinses down the drain deposits enough ceramide to matter is not something a trial has [tested for this product](#the-evidence).

The second question is whether this is a good example of a gentle cleanser, and here the answer is affirmative. It is a well-rounded, inexpensive, fragrance-free wash that skin doctors recommend by name. Quoted in NewBeauty, board-certified dermatologist Valerie Callender says, "I recommend this for my patients with normal or dry sensitive skin on their face."[^6] It also holds the National Eczema Association's Seal of Acceptance, a voluntary program for products shown suitable for eczema and sensitive skin ([see Tolerability](#tolerability)).[^4]

It is inexpensive. The manufacturer lists it at $15.99 ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28), in sizes from 8 to 19 fl oz.[^1][^3] It is not a treatment: it delivers no active drug, and the manufacturer's "cleanses, hydrates and helps restore the protective skin barrier" positioning is a [label claim](#common-marketing-claims) the product-specific evidence does not establish.

## The Evidence

Keeping dry, barrier-impaired, and eczema-prone skin moisturized reduces flares and spares topical corticosteroids, one of dermatology's better-evidenced routines; that general case, including the role of barrier lipids like [[ceramides]], is set out on [[moisturizing]] and in the [[van-zuuren-2017-emollients-moisturisers-eczema|2017 Cochrane review]], which found most moisturizers gave some benefit for eczema and that no single one was shown to beat another. That evidence is for leave-on moisturizers rather than rinse-off cleansers, and the closest test of a ceramide wash comes from a regimen study of a different brand.

Spada 2021 was a randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial (n=100, 28 days) in adults with moderate eczema. It tested a regimen of a ceramide-dominant moisturizing cream used together with a ceramide hydrating body wash, both from Ego Pharmaceuticals, which funded the study. The active group "had significantly greater improvements in TEWL at all-time points compared to placebo" (P=.0342). For eczema severity, the between-group "change was not significantly different between groups" (EASI, P=.7804).[^2]

> **Bottom line.** A ceramide wash used together with a ceramide cream improved a skin-barrier measurement, but it reduced eczema no more than a plain vehicle. It tested a two-product regimen from one manufacturer, not this cleanser, and was funded by the products' maker.[^2]

Because it paired a body wash with a cream, the study cannot separate the wash from the cream, or stand in for a facial cleanser used on its own.

## What's In It

The ingredient list (INCI) as declared by the manufacturer.[^1] The ingredients with a functional role are linked to their own pages; the rest are the cleansing surfactants, texture, and preservative system.

Barrier and hydration ingredients:

- [[glycerin]], a humectant and the second ingredient
- three [[ceramides]] (ceramide NP, ceramide AP, ceramide EOP), lipids that match the skin's own barrier lipids
- sodium hyaluronate ([[hyaluronic-acid]]), a humectant
- [[cholesterol]] and [[phytosphingosine]], additional barrier lipids
- [[tocopherol]] (vitamin E), an antioxidant

Cleansing, texture, and preservative system: water, cetearyl alcohol, stearyl alcohol and cetyl alcohol (emollient fatty alcohols and emulsifiers), PEG-40 stearate, glyceryl stearate and polysorbate 20 (nonionic surfactants), sodium lauroyl lactylate and behentrimonium methosulfate (a mild surfactant and a conditioning emulsifier), carbomer and xanthan gum (thickeners), potassium phosphate and dipotassium phosphate (pH buffers), disodium EDTA (chelator), and phenoxyethanol with ethylhexylglycerin (preservatives).[^1]

Not in the formula: no [[niacinamide]] (which the separate CeraVe Foaming Facial Cleanser contains), no added fragrance, and no sulfate detergents such as sodium lauryl or laureth sulfate.[^1]

## Tolerability

CeraVe Hydrating Facial Cleanser holds the National Eczema Association Seal of Acceptance.[^4] The NEA describes the Seal as a voluntary program: the manufacturer applies and submits ingredient details and clinical safety-test reports for sensitivity, irritation, and toxicity, and an accepted product must be fragrance-free and free of the ingredients on the NEA "Ecz-clusion List." The NEA states the Seal reflects suitability for eczema and sensitive skin, and that "a product that works for one may not work for another."[^5]

CeraVe labels the product non-comedogenic and developed with dermatologists.[^1] These are manufacturer label claims, not independently tested outcomes.
