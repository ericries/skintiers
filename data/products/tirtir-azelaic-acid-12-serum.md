TIRTIR's Azelaic Acid 12% Serum is a 30 ml over-the-counter serum built on azelaic acid at a stated 12%, with zinc PCA, panthenol and a group of humectants and algae extracts.[^brand] It is the lowest-strength azelaic serum profiled here, below [[cos-de-baha-az15-azelaic-acid-15-serum|AZ15]], [[cos-de-baha-az20-azelaic-acid-20-serum|AZ20]] and the prescription gel on [[finacea-azelaic-acid-15-gel]].

> **Bottom line.** A reasonable, gentle azelaic serum at a fair price, and the one in this group with the widest gap between its number and the trial evidence. Azelaic acid is genuinely well evidenced for acne and post-acne marks (see [[azelaic-acid]]), and 12% declared second on the list is a real dose.[^brand] But the controlled trials behind azelaic acid used 15% and 20%, so 12% is below the studied range rather than inside it, and no trial tests this product. Discount the brand's "Clinically proven" banner hardest: the numbers under it, including a **201.79% increase in outer skin hydration**, arrive with no study, no sample size and no comparator.[^brand]

## Summary

The declared ingredient list, as reproduced by incidecoder from the manufacturer's label, runs water, **azelaic acid**, propylene glycol, sodium hydroxide, pentylene glycol, panthenol, sorbitol, chondrus crispus (carrageenan) extract, hydroxyethylcellulose, chlorella vulgaris extract, glucose, sugarcane extract, 1,2-hexanediol, butylene glycol, fructooligosaccharides, fructose, ethylhexylglycerin, biosaccharide gum-1, tromethamine, xanthan gum, tocopherol, betaine, **zinc PCA**, dipropylene glycol, a hyaluronic acid copolymer, caprylyl/capryl glucoside, hydrogenated lecithin and ceramide NP.

Two placements are worth naming. **Azelaic acid is second**, immediately after water, which is consistent with the stated 12% and makes this a genuine azelaic treatment rather than a token inclusion.[^brand] **Zinc PCA is 23rd of 28**, which puts it in fractions of a percent, and the brand gives it a sebum-regulating role it cannot support at that position; the ingredient case is on [[zinc-pca]].[^brand]

Sodium hydroxide sits fourth, as it does in the sibling serums and in the studied prescription formulation. It is a pH adjuster, not evidence the acid has been neutralised; no final pH is published, so the formula's pH is simply unknown. The general case for azelaic acid, with its numbers and limits, is on [[azelaic-acid]] and is not repeated here.

## The Evidence

No published trial tests this serum, so the grade rests on the evidence for azelaic acid plus what the label supports.

The transfer gap is the whole argument, and it is wider here than for the 15% serums. The controlled azelaic evidence set out on [[azelaic-acid]] and [[finacea-azelaic-acid-15-gel]] comes from prescription formulations at 15% and 20%, in specific vehicles. This is a cosmetic serum at a stated **12%**, which is below that range,[^brand] in a different vehicle, with no trial of its own. Nothing in the cited evidence establishes a dose-response curve for azelaic acid in cosmetic vehicles, so neither the claim that 12% nearly matches 15% nor the claim that 12% is too low to work can be stated from the record here. What can be said is that the strength sits outside the tested range, and the burden of showing it performs is on the brand, which has not met it.

The supporting cast is a sensible comfort package for an acid serum: panthenol, ceramide NP, a hyaluronic acid copolymer, betaine and sorbitol are all humectants or barrier-supporting ingredients. Their amounts are undisclosed and most sit in the back half of the list, so ingredient order cannot tell you how much barrier support any of them delivers.

## Common Marketing Claims

Claims quoted from the brand's own product page; treat each as unproven unless it also appears under The Evidence.

- **"Clinically proven to hydrate, calm irritation, and visibly reduce redness"**[^brand] Not supported here. No study, protocol, duration, sample size or comparator is named anywhere on the page, so the phrase is doing the work of a citation without being one.
- **"201.79% increase in outer skin hydration"**[^brand] This is the number to distrust most, for two reasons. The false precision to two decimal places implies a measurement rigour that an uncited figure cannot carry, and a percentage increase in a corneometer reading is a surface-moisture instrument output, not a visible result a user would recognise. Any humectant raises that reading; see [[hyaluronic-acid]] for what such readings do and do not mean.
- **"73.40% reduction in redness after use"**[^brand] Same problem, and "after use" specifies no timepoint. Azelaic acid does have real evidence for redness in [[rosacea]], but that evidence belongs to the ingredient at prescription strength, not to this serum.
- **"12% Azelaic Acid works to unclog pores, smooth texture, and fade post-acne marks"**[^brand] The direction is supported at the ingredient level for acne and post-acne pigment (see [[azelaic-acid]] and [[hyperpigmentation]]), but no trial of this serum measures it, and pigment responses take months.
- **"Zinc PCA regulates excess sebum"**[^brand] Discount this. Zinc PCA is 23rd of 28 on the declared list, and no trial of this product or of zinc PCA at that kind of level for sebum is cited here (see [[zinc-pca]]).
- **"Heal-zip™ Technology"**[^brand] A brand-coined name, not an ingredient or a mechanism. Nothing on the page defines what it refers to.

## Who It Is For

Someone who wants to try azelaic acid for mild acne or post-acne marks, prefers a lightweight serum, and treats the 12% as a starting strength rather than a proven dose. If azelaic acid has already failed at this kind of strength, the prescription route on [[finacea-azelaic-acid-15-gel]] is the better comparison. The practical ranking against its siblings is on [[best-azelaic-acid-products]].
