A manual microneedling stamp with replaceable 0.5 mm titanium heads, sold as a kit from $117 on subscription or $130 outright. **Bottom line:** the design decisions are sensible and the safety instructions are better than most of this category, but no trial of this device is offered, no FDA record exists for it, and the running cost is higher than the headline figure. What microneedling does in general is at [[microneedling]].

> **The seller supplies no evidence for the device itself.** Its description is "a dermatologist-recommended at-home microneedling kit for reducing the look of acne scars minimizing the look of pores smoothing out texture collagen support dark spots".[^feed] That is four cosmetic claims, and nothing on the pages read cites a study of this stamp. The recommendation is from dermatologists rather than from data, and those are different things.

## Summary

The kit contains "Banisher 3.0 Microneedling Stamp", two replaceable 0.5 mm heads and a vitamin C serum.[^feed] Needles are "0.5mm gold-plated titanium bristles that are thinner than a human hair", used with "a precision stamping design" rather than a roller or motor.[^feed] Heads also come in 0.25 mm and 1.0 mm, which the seller assigns to different jobs: 0.25 mm for "Crow's feet & under-eye lines", 0.5 mm for "Reducing Look Of Acne scars & dark spots", and 1.0 mm for "Body stretch marks".[^feed]

Current pricing from the brand's own catalogue on 2026-11-15: the kit at $117.00 on either subscription option, $130.00 as a one-time purchase with the serum, or $175.00 with retinol added; replacement heads at $75.00 for three 0.5 mm heads, rising to $360.00 for twenty-four.[^feed]

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: not established for this product.** No study of the Banisher is cited anywhere on the pages read. The procedure's general evidence is graded at [[microneedling]] and is not restated here; the relevant caution is that most of it comes from in-office treatment, which is a different depth and a different operator.
- **Record finding: no FDA record, and the one microneedling code would not cover it anyway.** Searching openFDA's 510(k) database on 2026-11-15 for the company name and for the device name returned no match in either case.[^fda] The only FDA classification whose definition names microneedles is product code QAI, "Powered Microneedle Device", defined as using needles "to mechanically puncture and injure skin tissue for aesthetic use".[^qai] The Banisher is a manual stamp, so QAI is not its category. This page states what the databases return and draws no conclusion about what any product is required to do.
- **Record finding: the cost per session is roughly double the stated figure for full-face use.** The replacement-head listing says "Cost per session: under $5.00 on avereage", with the typo as published, and on the same listing says each head is good for "Up to 8 partial face or 4 full face sessions".[^feed] Three 0.5 mm heads cost $75.00, so a head costs $25.00. Four full-face sessions from a $25 head is $6.25 each; eight partial-face sessions is $3.13. The under-five claim is true for partial-face use and not for full-face, and neither figure includes the kit.
- **What the seller gets right.** The instructions are specific and cautious in ways much of this category is not: sanitise with "70-90% isopropyl alcohol" before each use, "Do not share your Banisher or heads with others - personal use only!", "Do not use if prone to keloids", replace the head "after maximum 8 sessions", and "AVOID any acne or rashes" when stamping.[^feed] Naming keloid-prone skin as a contraindication is the kind of thing a seller has no commercial reason to volunteer.
- **The hygiene ceiling is worth understanding.** Isopropyl alcohol sanitises; it does not sterilise, and these needles are reused up to eight times. That is the structural difference between a home stamp and a clinic, where needle cartridges are single-use.

## The Evidence

None specific to this device. [[microneedling]] carries the procedure's evidence and [[rf-microneedling-acne-scars]] the energy-based version; neither is repeated here.

> **Bottom line.** A thoughtfully designed tool with honest safety copy, sold on four cosmetic claims it does not evidence, at a running cost its own arithmetic understates for full-face use.

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted from the seller's own listings and **not** endorsed here.

- "dermatologist-recommended at-home microneedling kit"[^feed] No dermatologist is named in the text read, and a recommendation is not a trial.
- "thinner than a human hair" and "Thinner Bristles are Better!"[^feed] The thinness is plausible and the inference is the seller's. No measurement or comparison test is cited.
- "Patented design."[^feed] No patent number appears on the page read, so the claim cannot be checked against a filing.
- "With consistent use, most people begin to notice smoother texture and more even skin tone within 4-12 weeks."[^feed] "Most people" is a prevalence claim with no study behind it.
