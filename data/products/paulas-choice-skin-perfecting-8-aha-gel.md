Paula's Choice Skin Perfecting 8% AHA Gel Exfoliant is a water-based, leave-on gel exfoliant built around a labeled 8% glycolic acid, at a manufacturer-stated pH of 3.5 to 3.9, sold in a 100 ml (3.3 fl. oz.) bottle listed at $34.00 by at least one US retailer.[^1][^2]

> Glycolic acid is a well-studied alpha-hydroxy acid with modest, mostly appearance-level evidence for smoothing texture and photodamage, set out on [[glycolic-acid]]. This gel's labeled 8% matches the concentration used in the controlled trial behind that evidence exactly, closer than most other OTC glycolic products, at a manufacturer-stated pH (3.5-3.9) low enough to be exfoliation-active. The trade-off is the one well-documented downside of glycolic acid, increased sun sensitivity during use, and the manufacturer's own directions carry a Sun Protection Advice warning that the product "may increase your skin's sensitivity to the sun and the possibility of sunburn."[^1]

## Summary

Paula's Choice Skin Perfecting 8% AHA Gel Exfoliant is a daily leave-on exfoliant built around [[glycolic-acid|glycolic acid]], the smallest and most-used alpha-hydroxy acid (AHA) in skincare, at a labeled 8%.[^1] Glycolic acid has modest, mostly appearance-level evidence for smoothing skin texture and softening the signs of photodamage, and its best-documented health effect is a downside (increased UV sensitivity during use); that case, by use, is set out on [[glycolic-acid]]. The controlled trial behind the texture/photodamage evidence (set out on [[glycolic-acid]]) tested an 8% glycolic acid cream, the same labeled strength as this gel, which puts this formula directly at the studied concentration rather than reading across from a nearby one.

What distinguishes this product from a plainer acid is less the concentration itself, since 8% is a common ceiling for OTC leave-on glycolic acid, and more the format and supporting ingredients: a weightless, water-based gel (rather than a cream or toner) held at a manufacturer-stated pH of 3.5 to 3.9, low enough to stay exfoliation-active, formulated with chamomile flower extract, aloe vera leaf juice, and green tea leaf extract, which the manufacturer describes as "skin-soothing plant extracts and antioxidants," plus sodium hyaluronate and panthenol as humectants.[^1][^2] It is sold at a premium relative to a plain 7% acid toner: one US retailer lists it at a recommended retail price of $34.00 for the 100 ml size, discounted to $17.00 at the time of access though the listing was marked out of stock ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-07).[^2]

The other side of that acid strength is the documented downside of glycolic acid: increased skin sensitivity to sunlight while it is being used (see [[glycolic-acid]]). The manufacturer's own directions carry a "Sun Protection Advice" warning that the product's 8% glycolic acid "may increase your skin's sensitivity to the sun and the possibility of sunburn," and recommend a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher sunscreen every day while using it.[^1] Its marketing goes further than the trial evidence for texture and tone in places, including wrinkle-appearance and radiance claims; those are separated out under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Glycolic acid is a well-studied AHA exfoliant with modest, largely cosmetic (appearance-level) evidence for smoothing texture and softening the signs of photodamage; the full case, by use, including the higher-strength peel evidence for acne and melasma and the documented UV-sensitivity downside, is set out on [[glycolic-acid]]. No study of this specific gel formula has been published; the product-specific question is how its labeled concentration and pH sit against that ingredient-level evidence. The controlled trial behind the texture/photodamage grade on that page used an 8% glycolic acid cream. This gel is labeled 8%,[^1] matching that studied strength directly, though the trial tested a cream rather than a gel, so the vehicle itself was not tested. The manufacturer states the product's pH range as 3.5 to 3.9 in its own product description.[^1] A pH in that range keeps glycolic acid largely in its active, un-ionized (free-acid) form, the state that drives exfoliation (the mechanism is covered on [[glycolic-acid]]); the manufacturer does not publish a separate free-acid-value figure. Both the 8% concentration and the pH figure are the manufacturer's own stated specifications, not an independently assayed result.[^1]

> **Bottom line.** This gel's labeled 8% and stated pH of 3.5-3.9 match the concentration used in the controlled trial behind glycolic acid's modest texture/photodamage evidence (an 8% cream), though no trial has tested this specific gel formula. The evidence for what glycolic acid does, and does not do, is set out on [[glycolic-acid]].[^1]

## What's In It

The full declared ingredient list (INCI), matching between the manufacturer's own product page and a US retailer listing.[^1][^2]

Active and supporting ingredients:

- [[glycolic-acid|glycolic acid]], listed second on the INCI (after water), labeled 8%, described by the manufacturer as an "alpha hydroxy acid/exfoliant"[^1]
- Sodium Hydroxide, described by the manufacturer as a "pH adjuster"[^1]
- Chamomilla Recutita (Matricaria) Flower Extract (chamomile), described by the manufacturer as "skin-soothing"[^1]
- Aloe Barbadensis Leaf Juice (aloe vera), described by the manufacturer for "hydration"[^1]
- Camellia Oleifera Leaf Extract (green tea), described by the manufacturer as "antioxidant/skin-soothing"[^1]
- Sodium Hyaluronate and Sodium PCA, described by the manufacturer as "skin replenishing"[^1]
- Panthenol, described by the manufacturer for "hydration"[^1]

Base, texture, and preservative system: propylene glycol and butylene glycol (hydration), hydroxyethylcellulose and polyquaternium-10 (texture-enhancing), and phenoxyethanol and sodium benzoate (preservatives).[^1][^2]

The formula is water-based, and a US retailer lists it as fragrance-free, oil-free, paraben-free, sulfate-free, vegan, gluten-free, and cruelty-free.[^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by a trial of this product:

- **"Smooths the appearance of wrinkles."** The manufacturer's own product description makes this claim.[^1] The controlled trial behind glycolic acid's photoaging evidence graded overall photodamage severity and sallowness, not a specific wrinkle-appearance endpoint (see [[glycolic-acid]]).
- **"Revitalizes uneven skin tone" and "creates noticeable radiance."** Cosmetic (appearance) claims in the manufacturer's own copy, distinct from the texture/photodamage endpoints the trial behind glycolic acid actually measured.[^1]
- **"Improves signs of sun damage."** A key-benefit claim on a US retailer's listing of the manufacturer's copy; the controlled trial behind glycolic acid's evidence measured overall photodamage severity and sallowness, not a sun-damage-specific endpoint.[^2]
- **"With ongoing use, it can help promote firmer-looking skin."** A manufacturer claim about appearance, not a measured firmness or skin-thickness endpoint from a trial of this product.[^1]
