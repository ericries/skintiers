The Ordinary Azelaic Acid Suspension 10% is a silicone-based leave-on cream that lists azelaic acid as its fourth ingredient at a labeled 10%, sold inexpensively in a 30 ml or 100 ml tube.[^1]

> Azelaic acid has genuine health uses at prescription strength, where it treats papulopustular rosacea (a 15% gel) and inflammatory acne (a 20% cream) and competes with hydroquinone for melasma; that evidence is set out on [[azelaic-acid]]. This product carries a labeled 10%, below those studied strengths, so its own benefit is inferred from the ingredient rather than shown at this concentration. What it clearly is: one of the cheapest and most widely sold ways to try azelaic acid, about $12 for 30 ml. Allure names it a budget pick, and aesthetic doctor Rachel Ho calls it "a nice starter" below the studied 15-20% strengths.[^1][^4][^5]

## Summary

The Ordinary Azelaic Acid Suspension 10% is a leave-on cream built around [[azelaic-acid|azelaic acid]], a dicarboxylic acid with real health uses in dermatology. At prescription strength azelaic acid matches or beats metronidazole for papulopustular rosacea, treats mild inflammatory acne, and competes with hydroquinone for melasma and post-acne dark spots; that case, and its strengths and limits, is set out on [[azelaic-acid]]. Those uses were established with a 15% prescription gel (FINACEA) and a 20% prescription cream (AZELEX).[^2][^3] This product carries a labeled 10%, below those studied strengths, and the same strength as [[anua-azelaic-acid-serum|Anua's]] and [[paulas-choice-boost-10-azelaic-acid-booster|Paula's Choice's]] 10% azelaic products.[^1]

Two things follow, and both matter. The evidence that a 10% cosmetic does as much as the 15-20% prescription strengths [is thin](#the-evidence), so its product-specific effect is inferred from the ingredient rather than demonstrated at this concentration. But if the goal is to try azelaic acid, this is an unusually cheap and accessible way to get it, and it is well tolerated by the reviewers who have written it up. Aesthetic doctor Rachel Ho rates it 3.5 out of 5, says it "suffices as a nice multi-tasking moisturiser" that "dries quickly to leave a smooth and matt texture which I really liked," and frames the concentration honestly: for lower strengths like this one "there's not much research available yet," so it is "a nice starter for anyone who wishes to try out azelaic acid before moving onto a higher concentration."[^4] Allure lists it among its best budget picks from the brand, in a round-up vouched for by board-certified dermatologists.[^5]

It is inexpensive: the manufacturer's page lists the 30 ml tube at about $12 ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-07-28), which is part of why it is a common first azelaic acid. The manufacturer's "brightening formulation" and "high concentration" language is separated out under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Azelaic acid is a well-evidenced topical: at prescription strength it is at least as effective as metronidazole 0.75% for papulopustular rosacea, competes with and can beat hydroquinone for melasma, and helps mild-to-moderate acne with good tolerability; that evidence, by use, is set out on [[azelaic-acid]]. In the United States it is a prescription drug as a 20% cream (AZELEX) for inflammatory acne and as a 15% gel (FINACEA) for papulopustular rosacea.[^2][^3] The product-specific question is how this 10% cosmetic cream sits against azelaic acid's studied strengths and its other over-the-counter versions.

Most azelaic acid sold without a prescription is formulated at about 10%, and The Ordinary describes this as a "10%" suspension with azelaic acid listed fourth on the ingredient declaration, after water and two emollients.[^1] That is half the concentration of the 20% prescription cream AZELEX and below the 15% of the prescription rosacea gel FINACEA.[^1][^2][^3] It is the same labeled strength as the [[anua-azelaic-acid-serum|Anua]] and [[paulas-choice-boost-10-azelaic-acid-booster|Paula's Choice]] 10% azelaic products. Aesthetic doctor Rachel Ho makes the concentration point directly: "we do know that azelaic acid at concentrations of 15-20% are effective, but for lower concentrations such as The Ordinary's Azelaic Acid Suspension 10%; there's not much research available yet."[^4] The 10% figure is a cosmetic label claim, not an independently assayed or FDA-reviewed concentration.[^1]

> **Bottom line.** At a labeled 10%, this cream carries azelaic acid below the 15% gel and 20% cream strengths behind azelaic acid's controlled evidence, and at the same strength as its main OTC rivals. Its expected benefit is inferred from the ingredient at that concentration rather than shown in a trial of this product (see [[azelaic-acid]]).[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## What's In It

The full ingredient list (INCI) as declared by the manufacturer.[^1] The ingredients with a functional role are linked or named; the rest are the silicone base, texture, and preservative system.

Active and supporting ingredients:

- [[azelaic-acid|azelaic acid]], labeled 10% and listed fourth on the INCI (after water, isodecyl neopentanoate, and dimethicone)
- [[tocopherol]] (vitamin E), an antioxidant

Base, texture, and preservative system: water, isodecyl neopentanoate and isohexadecane (emollients), dimethicone, dimethicone/bis-isobutyl PPG-20 crosspolymer and polysilicone-11 (silicones that carry the azelaic acid and set the finish), dimethyl isosorbide and ethoxydiglycol (solvents), hydroxyethyl acrylate/sodium acryloyldimethyl taurate copolymer (a gelling emulsifier), isoceteth-20 and polysorbate 60 (surfactants), triethanolamine (pH adjuster), trisodium ethylenediamine disuccinate (a chelator), and phenoxyethanol and chlorphenesin (preservatives).[^1]

The formula is silicone-based, which is what gives it its finish: Rachel Ho attributes the "smooth and matt texture" she liked to the silicones, noting dimethicone is the third ingredient.[^4] The declared list carries no added fragrance.[^1]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by a trial of this product:

- **"High concentration of azelaic acid."** The manufacturer calls the 10% level a "high concentration."[^1] It is in fact below azelaic acid's studied strengths: the acne and melasma trials used a 20% cream and the rosacea approval a 15% gel (see [[azelaic-acid]]).[^2][^3]
- **"a cream-like brightening formulation" and "visibly improves skin texture."** These are cosmetic appearance claims. Azelaic acid's demonstrated pigment effect is specifically for melasma and post-acne (post-inflammatory) hyperpigmentation, evidenced at higher strengths on [[azelaic-acid]], not a generic glow for otherwise-even skin.[^1]
- **"Non-comedogenic and suitable for all skin types."** A manufacturer label claim, not an independently published outcome.[^1]
