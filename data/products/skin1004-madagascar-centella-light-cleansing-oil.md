## Summary

A cheap, competent first-step oil cleanser with a name that oversells one ingredient. **Bottom line:** at $5.50 for 30ml or $18.90 for 200ml it is among the least expensive ways to do the oil step, and the formula is built for that job.[^brand] But the centella the product is named after sits sixth on the brand's own ingredient list, below bergamot fruit oil, and the list ends with Limonene and Linalool on a page that elsewhere calls the product suited to sensitive skin.[^brand]

> **Read the order, not the name.** The brand's full ingredient list runs: Ethylhexyl Stearate, Cetyl Ethylhexanoate, Sorbeth-30 Tetraoleate, Caprylic/Capric Triglyceride, Citrus Aurantium Bergamia (Bergamot) Fruit Oil, Centella Asiatica Extract, then the other botanicals, Ethylhexylglycerin, and finally Limonene and Linalool.[^brand] Three synthetic esters and an emulsifier do the cleansing. The fragrant oils come next. The headline ingredient is sixth.

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: modest, for the job it is actually for.** Oil cleansers earn their place on waterproof sunscreen, and the one controlled test that isolates the oil step is set out on [[double-cleansing]]. No trial of this product was found, so the rest of this grade rests on composition.
- **The composition supports the cleansing claim.** The first three ingredients are Ethylhexyl Stearate and Cetyl Ethylhexanoate, both lightweight synthetic esters, and Sorbeth-30 Tetraoleate, the emulsifier that lets an oil cleanser turn milky and rinse off rather than smear.[^brand] That ordering is what you want in a first-step cleanser and it explains the water-like light texture the brand describes.[^brand]
- **Effect size: minimal, for the centella.** [[centella-asiatica]] is graded **weak** on this site on its own evidence, and [[madecassoside]], its best-studied constituent, is also weak. The brand's list puts Centella Asiatica Extract sixth, below bergamot fruit oil.[^brand] A rinse-off product is also the least favourable format for any active, since it is on the skin for under a minute.
- **Record finding: the brand declares the fragrance allergens itself.** The list ends with **Limonene** and **Linalool**, declared separately as EU labelling requires for a set of fragrance allergens, alongside bergamot, Pelargonium graveolens (geranium) and Rosa damascena (rose) oils.[^brand] None of that makes the product harmful, and it is a rinse-off. It does mean anyone avoiding fragrance materials should know they are present and declared; [[contact-dermatitis]] covers how fragrance allergy presents.
- **Record finding: the page disagrees with itself about who it is for.** One block gives "Skin Type: Normal, Dry, Combination". Another block on the same page reads "Suited for Normal Dry Sensitive".[^brand] Sensitive appears in one and not the other, which is the single claim a fragranced product most needs to get right.
- **"Non-comedogenic formula" is the brand's word.** The claim appears in the product details.[^brand] There is no regulatory definition or required test behind the term.

## What the two published ingredient lists disagree about

This matters enough to record, because the two lists a shopper is likely to meet are not the same. The brand's own page gives fourteen ingredients, including Caprylic/Capric Triglyceride fourth and Limonene and Linalool last.[^brand] The entry for this product on incidecoder, an ingredient aggregator rather than a primary source and cited here only as the thing it is, lists eleven, omits Caprylic/Capric Triglyceride, Limonene and Linalool entirely, and places Centella Asiatica Extract fourth with a declared 10,000 ppm, which is one per cent.

This page follows the brand, because a company is authoritative about its own formula and the aggregator is not. The practical consequence is worth stating plainly: the version of the list most often reproduced elsewhere is the one that leaves the fragrance allergens out and moves centella up.

## The Evidence

None specific to this product. The category evidence, including the residue figures for waterproof and non-waterproof sunscreen, belongs to [[double-cleansing]] and is not repeated here. What the composition shows is a formula arranged for cleansing rather than for treatment, which is the right arrangement for a first step.

> **Bottom line.** Good at the cheap, unglamorous job of dissolving sunscreen and makeup. The centella in the name is sixth on the label and graded weak anyway, and the brand's own list declares two fragrance allergens on a page that also calls the product suited to sensitive skin.

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted from the brand's own product page and **not** endorsed here.

- "Centella and 6 plant-derived oils* gently dissolve makeup, sunscreen and excess sebum on the skin".[^brand] Accurate as a description of the list, and the asterisk names bergamot first among the six. The dissolving is done mostly by the esters that are not in that count.
- "Non-comedogenic formula that dissolves sebum and helps keep pores clear".[^brand] An unregulated term with no required test behind it.
- "Micellar cleansing oil removes excess oil and makeup without leaving the skin feeling stripped or dry".[^brand] A texture claim rather than a measured outcome.
