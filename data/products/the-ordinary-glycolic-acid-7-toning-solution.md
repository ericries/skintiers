The Ordinary Glycolic Acid 7% Toning Solution is a water-based, leave-on toner built around a labeled 7% glycolic acid, with a manufacturer-stated pH of 3.5 to 3.9, sold for about $9 for a 100 ml bottle (also sold in 240 ml).[^1]

> Glycolic acid is a well-studied alpha-hydroxy acid with modest, mostly appearance-level evidence for smoothing texture and photodamage, set out on [[glycolic-acid]]. This toner's labeled 7% sits close to the 8% cream used in the controlled trial behind that evidence, close enough that the studied concentration is a reasonable proxy, at a manufacturer-stated pH (3.5-3.9) low enough to be exfoliation-active, in a widely available bottle priced at about $9 for 100 ml. The trade-off is the one well-documented downside of glycolic acid, increased sun sensitivity during use, and the brand's own directions say the formula "should not be used on sensitive, peeling, or compromised skin."[^1]

## Summary

The Ordinary Glycolic Acid 7% Toning Solution is a daily leave-on toner built around [[glycolic-acid|glycolic acid]], the smallest and most-used alpha-hydroxy acid (AHA) in skincare, at a labeled 7%.[^1] Glycolic acid has modest, mostly appearance-level evidence for smoothing skin texture and softening the signs of photodamage, and its best-documented health effect is a downside (increased UV sensitivity during use); that case, by use, is set out on [[glycolic-acid]]. The controlled trial behind the texture/photodamage evidence (set out on [[glycolic-acid]]) tested an 8% glycolic acid cream, one point above this product's labeled 7%, which puts this formula closer to that studied strength than most lower-percent OTC glycolic toners.

What distinguishes this toner from a plainer, cheaper acid product is less the concentration itself and more the combination: a 7% acid held at a manufacturer-stated pH of 3.5 to 3.9, low enough to stay exfoliation-active, in a water-based, oil-free, silicone-free formula that also carries a Tasmanian pepperberry extract, which The Ordinary describes as helping "reduce irritation commonly associated with exfoliation," plus aloe vera water, ginseng root extract, and an amino-acid/PCA humectant complex.[^1][^2] It is sold cheaply relative to other 7% glycolic products: The Ordinary's own page lists it at $9.00 USD, and it is sold internationally, including for €9.30 (100 ml) on Cult Beauty ([see Where to Buy](#where-to-buy), accessed 2026-08-05).[^1][^2]

The other side of that acid strength is the documented downside of glycolic acid: increased skin sensitivity to sunlight while it is being used (see [[glycolic-acid]]). The Ordinary's own directions carry a "Sunburn Alert," say the product "should not be used on sensitive, peeling, or compromised skin," and advise a patch test before use.[^1] Its marketing goes further than the trial evidence for texture and tone in places, including a wrinkle-reduction claim and a scalp-rebalancing use; those are separated out under [Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims).

## The Evidence

Glycolic acid is a well-studied AHA exfoliant with modest, largely cosmetic (appearance-level) evidence for smoothing texture and softening the signs of photodamage; the full case, by use, including the higher-strength peel evidence for acne and melasma and the documented UV-sensitivity downside, is set out on [[glycolic-acid]]. No study of this specific toner formula has been published; the product-specific question is how its labeled concentration and pH sit against that ingredient-level evidence. The controlled trial behind the texture/photodamage grade on that page used an 8% glycolic acid cream. This toner is labeled 7%,[^1] one point below that studied strength and, unlike products formulated at 2% to 5% glycolic acid, close enough that the trial's concentration is a reasonable read across.[^1] The Ordinary states the finished product's pH as 3.5 to 3.9 on its specifications table, and separately describes it in its product copy as having "a carefully calibrated pH of approximately 3.6."[^1] A pH in that range keeps glycolic acid largely in its active, un-ionized (free-acid) form, the state that drives exfoliation (the mechanism is covered on [[glycolic-acid]]); The Ordinary does not publish a separate free-acid-value figure.[^1] Both the 7% concentration and the pH figures are the manufacturer's own stated specifications, not an independently assayed result.[^1]

> **Bottom line.** This toner's labeled 7% and stated pH of 3.5-3.9 place it close to the concentration used in the controlled trial behind glycolic acid's modest texture/photodamage evidence (an 8% cream), closer than most lower-strength OTC glycolic toners, though no trial has tested this specific formula. The evidence for what glycolic acid does, and does not do, is set out on [[glycolic-acid]].[^1]

## What's In It

The full declared ingredient list (INCI), as listed by an authorized retailer; The Ordinary's own page notes its "formulations are updated from time to time," so the list on the box may vary by region and date of purchase.[^1][^2]

Active and supporting ingredients:

- [[glycolic-acid|glycolic acid]], listed second on the INCI (after water), labeled 7%
- Aloe Barbadensis Leaf Water (aloe vera water)
- Panax Ginseng Root Extract, which The Ordinary describes as helping "target the appearance of uneven skin tone"[^1]
- Tasmannia Lanceolata Fruit/Leaf Extract (Tasmanian pepperberry), which The Ordinary describes as helping "reduce irritation commonly associated with exfoliation"[^1]
- an amino-acid and PCA humectant complex (aspartic acid, alanine, glycine, serine, valine, isoleucine, proline, threonine, histidine, phenylalanine, glutamic acid, arginine, PCA, sodium PCA, sodium lactate, urea), which The Ordinary describes as mimicking "the function of natural amino acids present within the skin to support skin hydration"[^1][^2]
- Rosa Damascena (rose) flower water and Centaurea Cyanus (cornflower) flower water[^2]

Base, texture, and preservative system: water, propanediol and glycerin (humectants), triethanolamine and aminomethyl propanol (pH adjusters), sodium chloride, hexylene glycol, fructose, glucose, sucrose, dextrin, citric acid, hexyl nicotinate, polysorbate 20 (a surfactant), gellan gum (a thickener), trisodium ethylenediamine disuccinate (a chelator), and potassium sorbate, sodium benzoate, 1,2-hexanediol, and caprylyl glycol (preservatives).[^2]

The formula is water-based rather than water-free, and The Ordinary lists it as alcohol-free, oil-free, silicone-free, vegan, gluten-free, and cruelty-free.[^1]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by a trial of this product:

- **"Reducing the visibility of fine lines, wrinkles, and other signs of aging."** The manufacturer's own product description makes this claim.[^1] The controlled trial behind glycolic acid's photoaging evidence graded overall photodamage severity and sallowness, not a specific wrinkle-reduction endpoint (see [[glycolic-acid]]).
- **"Can also be applied to the scalp to help rebalance, hydrate, and reduce dryness."** A manufacturer usage suggestion, not a scalp-specific clinical claim.[^1]
- **"Promotes the appearance of more luminous and radiant skin."** A cosmetic (appearance) claim in the manufacturer's own copy, distinct from the texture/photodamage endpoints the trial behind glycolic acid actually measured.[^1]
