Caudalie Vinoperfect Radiance Serum, sold today under the storefront name Vinoperfect Brightening Dark Spot Serum Vitamin C Alternative, is a French grape-derived brightening serum built around viniferine, Caudalie's patented tyrosinase-inhibiting active, in a base of water, glycerin, squalane, and bisabolol, sold in a 50 mL (1.7 fl oz) bottle for $82.[^1]

> Caudalie states viniferine is "62X more effective than vitamin C" at inhibiting tyrosinase in an in vitro test, and cites its own clinical studies reporting a 63% reduction in dark-spot coloration after one bottle (81% on deeper skin tones) and an 81% reduction after 56 days of twice-daily use; these are the brand's own unpublished, non-peer-reviewed studies, not independently verified findings.[^1] A separate, older study co-authored by Caudalie R&D researchers found ε-viniferin, the compound the brand calls viniferine, was the strongest tyrosinase inhibitor among several grape-derived stilbenes tested in vitro, supporting the mechanism but not the specific clinical percentages above.[^morel]

## Summary

The declared ingredient list, in INCI order, is water, butylene glycol, glycerin, cococaprylate/caprate, [[squalane]], Palmitoyl Grapevine Shoot Extract, polyglyceryl-3 distearate, bisabolol, glyceryl stearate, xanthan gum, caprylyl glycol, microcrystalline cellulose, polyacrylate crosspolymer-6, potassium sorbate, glyceryl stearate citrate, citric acid, sodium phytate, sodium hydroxide, and parfum (fragrance).[^1] Viniferine itself does not appear by that name in the declared INCI; Caudalie's own materials describe it as derived from Palmitoyl Grapevine Shoot Extract, the sixth-listed ingredient, a proprietary complex without an independent published assay of the finished product's viniferine content or concentration.[^1]

Caudalie's mechanism claim for viniferine is a "triple action": inhibiting the enzyme that starts melanin production (tyrosinase), reducing melanin production, and limiting melanin transfer to the skin's surface, citing an in vitro tyrosinase-inhibition test and an ex vivo epidermal-melanin test as its basis.[^1] A 2014 study, co-authored by two Caudalie R&D scientists alongside academic pharmacognosy researchers and published in a peer-reviewed analytical-chemistry journal, purified ε-viniferin (the compound Caudalie markets as viniferine) from grapevine stalks and found it the most potent tyrosinase inhibitor among the compounds tested, including resveratrol.[^morel] That finding supports viniferine's tyrosinase-inhibiting mechanism in a laboratory assay; it is not a clinical trial of skin lightening in humans, and the paper's authorship overlaps with Caudalie's own R&D lab, so it is not fully independent of the brand.

No independently published, controlled human trial of viniferine, or of this specific formula, was located. The dark-spot-reduction figures Caudalie publishes on its product page, "63% after 1 bottle," "81% after 56 days," "95% saw a visible reduction," come from the brand's own clinical studies, described on the page only as "dermatological scoring" of small volunteer panels (33 to 65 people) over 56 days, without a linked, peer-reviewed publication, disclosed control group, or independent replication ([see Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims)).[^1] Against the broader hyperpigmentation evidence base, [[niacinamide]], [[tranexamic-acid]], and [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c|vitamin C]] each have independent, product-agnostic human-trial evidence of their own, graded on their own pages; viniferine has no comparable independent human evidence, only the in vitro mechanism study above and the brand's own unpublished clinical data.

## The Evidence

The tyrosinase-inhibition mechanism behind viniferine's brightening claim rests on one peer-reviewed in vitro paper, co-authored by Caudalie R&D scientists, that purified ε-viniferin from grapevine stalks and compared it to resveratrol and other stilbenes for anti-tyrosinase activity, finding ε-viniferin the most potent inhibitor of the set.[^morel] That is a cell-free enzyme assay, not a test on human skin or a clinical trial, and because two of the four authors are Caudalie employees, it is company-affiliated rather than fully independent evidence. A broader PubMed search for "viniferin" and "tyrosinase" returned a small number of results, mostly plant-chemistry and stilbene-biosynthesis papers rather than clinical dermatology trials; no randomized, controlled human trial of topical viniferine for hyperpigmentation was found.

Caudalie's own clinical claims for this product, a 63% reduction in dark-spot coloration "after 1 bottle," an 81% reduction on deeper skin tones, an 81% reduction after 56 days of twice-daily use, and 95% of users seeing "a visible reduction," are attributed on the product page to "clinical study, dermatological scoring" of 33 to 65 volunteers over 56 days.[^1] No sample demographics, control arm, statistical methodology, or peer-reviewed publication are linked from the product page, so these figures are reported here as the brand's own claims, not as independently verified findings ([Common Marketing Claims](#common-marketing-claims)).

Against comparator brightening serums built on actives with independent human evidence: [[anua-niacinamide-10-tranexamic-acid-4-serum|Anua Niacinamide 10% + TXA 4% Serum]] combines labeled 10% niacinamide and 4% tranexamic acid, both of which have their own small, independent human trials for hyperpigmentation (modest for niacinamide, modest and stronger orally for tranexamic acid), graded on [[niacinamide]] and [[tranexamic-acid]]. This Caudalie serum's central active, viniferine, has no comparable independent human trial; its evidentiary case is a company-affiliated in vitro mechanism study plus unpublished brand clinical testing.

> **Bottom line.** Viniferine's tyrosinase-inhibiting mechanism has support from one company-affiliated in vitro study; the dark-spot-reduction percentages Caudalie advertises come from its own unpublished clinical studies. No independent, published, controlled human trial of viniferine or of this product was found.[^1][^morel]

## Common Marketing Claims

Label and marketing claims not established by independent, product-specific evidence:

- **"Viniferine is 62X more effective than vitamin C" to brighten.** Attributed on the product page to "an in vitro test on the inhibitory action of Viniferine on tyrosinase," with no linked methodology, concentrations tested, or peer-reviewed publication.[^1]
- **"Dark spots visibly reduced by 63% after 1 bottle" and "81% on deeper skin tones."** Attributed to a Caudalie "clinical study, dermatological scoring," 65 volunteers for the 63% figure and 33 volunteers for the 81% figure, both over 56 days, without a linked publication, disclosed control group, or independent replication.[^1]
- **"95% saw a visible reduction in the appearance of their dark spots."** A self-reported satisfaction figure from the same unpublished clinical study, methodology not disclosed on the product page.[^1]
- **"1 Vinoperfect Serum is sold every 15 seconds globally."** A sales-volume claim, not an efficacy claim, sourced to the brand itself with no independent sales data linked.[^1]
- **"Non-photosensitising."** Attributed on the product page to the brand's own formulation testing; no independently published phototoxicity study is linked.[^1]
