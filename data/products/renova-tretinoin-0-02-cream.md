Renova is [[bausch-health|Bausch Health]]'s brand of tretinoin 0.02% emollient cream, a prescription-only topical retinoid FDA-approved "as an adjunctive agent for use in the mitigation (palliation) of fine facial wrinkles in patients who use comprehensive skin care and sunlight avoidance programs."[^dailymed]

> Renova is the low-strength, cosmetic-labeled version of [[tretinoin]], approved specifically for fine facial wrinkles rather than acne. Its own five-trial approval program found a modest edge over an inert vehicle cream, and the label states the trials did not adequately demonstrate benefit for coarse wrinkling, mottled pigmentation, roughness, or sagging ([the evidence](#the-evidence)).[^dailymed] Tretinoin's broader, better-evidenced photoaging record, built at higher strengths, is set out on [[tretinoin]] and [[retinoids]].

## Summary

Renova is Bausch Health's brand-name tretinoin 0.02% cream, formulated in an emollient base and dispensed only by prescription.[^dailymed] Unlike [[tretinoin-0-025-cream]] (sold as Retin-A or Avita and FDA-approved for acne), Renova is labeled specifically for cosmetic use: mitigating fine facial wrinkles alongside a skin-care and sun-avoidance routine.[^dailymed] Whether tretinoin as a molecule improves photoaged skin is covered in full on [[tretinoin]] and [[retinoids]]: it is the reference prescription topical for photoaging, with replicated trial evidence at the 0.025% and 0.1% strengths.

What is distinct about Renova is its own approval evidence, gathered at the lower 0.02% concentration and scored only against the FDA's fine-wrinkle endpoint: five trials totaling 324 subjects on Renova and 332 on vehicle cream, over 24 weeks, found 60% of Renova users reached at least minimal improvement in fine facial wrinkles versus 39% on vehicle ([the evidence](#the-evidence)).[^dailymed] That is a real but modest edge, most of it concentrated in the "minimal improvement" band rather than moderate change, and the label states plainly that none of the trials adequately showed a benefit for coarse wrinkling, mottled hyperpigmentation, tactile roughness, or skin laxity.[^dailymed] A separate 107-subject trial in darkly pigmented skin found fewer subjects improved on Renova (29%) than on vehicle cream used with the same skin-care and sun-avoidance program (43%), a finding specific to that cohort.[^dailymed]

The label is also explicit about what the product does not do: it "DOES NOT ELIMINATE WRINKLES, REPAIR SUN-DAMAGED SKIN, REVERSE PHOTOAGING, or RESTORE MORE YOUTHFUL or YOUNGER SKIN."[^dailymed] As a prescription-only drug, Renova has no retail listing or public price; it is dispensed by pharmacies against a prescription ([where to buy](#where-to-buy)).[^dailymed]

## The Evidence

Whether tretinoin improves photoaged skin generally, at the higher 0.025% and 0.1% strengths studied in independently replicated trials, is covered on [[tretinoin]]. The trial program below is specific to Renova's own 0.02% concentration and is the basis for its FDA indication.

The FDA-cited approval program combined five trials (four multicenter, adequate and well-controlled, plus one single-center randomized controlled trial), enrolling 324 subjects treated with Renova and 332 treated with a matching vehicle cream, all using a comprehensive skin-care and sun-avoidance program, over 24 weeks.[^dailymed] In lightly pigmented subjects, the graded outcome for fine facial wrinkling was: worsened, 1% (Renova) vs 3% (vehicle); no change, 40% vs 58%; minimal improvement, 35% vs 27%; mild improvement, 15% vs 9%; moderate improvement, 10% vs 3%.[^dailymed] Summed, 60% of Renova subjects showed at least minimal improvement versus 39% of vehicle subjects, an edge weighted toward the minimal-improvement category rather than moderate change. The label states that "no two of the five trials adequately demonstrated efficacy for mitigation of coarse wrinkling, mottled hyperpigmentation, tactile skin roughness, and laxity," meaning the approved claim is narrowly for fine wrinkling.[^dailymed] A separate trial in darkly pigmented subjects (N=107) found the label "demonstrated minimal or mild improvement in fine facial wrinkling in 43% of patients using Vehicle + CSP compared to 29% of subjects using RENOVA (tretinoin cream) 0.02% + CSP," an outcome running the opposite direction from the lightly pigmented result.[^dailymed]

> Bottom line: against an inert vehicle cream, Renova's own approval trials found a modest, mostly-minimal improvement in fine facial wrinkles (60% of users improved at least minimally versus 39% on vehicle), no adequately demonstrated benefit for coarse wrinkles, blotchy pigmentation, roughness, or sagging, and in one darkly pigmented cohort, fewer subjects improved on Renova than on vehicle.

## What's In It

- **Active: tretinoin 0.02%.** All-trans retinoic acid, the receptor-active form of vitamin A. Mechanism, cross-strength comparisons, and the broader photoaging and acne evidence for the molecule are on [[tretinoin]].[^dailymed]
- **Inactive ingredients:** benzyl alcohol, butylated hydroxytoluene, caprylic/capric triglyceride, cetyl alcohol, edetate disodium, methylparaben, propylparaben, purified water, stearic acid, stearyl alcohol, steareth-2, steareth-20, and xanthan gum, as declared on the FDA label.[^dailymed]

The label directs application in the evening only, after gentle washing and waiting 20 to 30 minutes for the skin to dry, using a pearl-sized amount (about 1/4 inch, 5 mm, diameter) for the entire face, avoiding the eyes, ears, nostrils, and mouth, and waiting about an hour before applying any other product; it also directs daily use of a sunscreen of at least SPF 15 and states that sunlight, including sunlamps, should be avoided or minimized during use.[^dailymed] The label states Renova "should not be used during pregnancy."[^dailymed]

## Where to Buy

Renova is dispensed only by prescription; the FDA label lists it "Rx only," with no over-the-counter or public retail listing.[^dailymed] It is supplied in 20 g, 40 g, and 60 g tubes and a 44 g pump, per the FDA label (accessed 2026-08-07).[^dailymed]
