An oral Janus kinase inhibitor, sold as Olumiant, whose one dermatology indication is severe alopecia areata. It is a prescription tablet with a boxed warning, not anything applied to skin, and the realistic read is that it regrows a meaningful amount of scalp hair in roughly a third of people at the higher dose while two thirds do not reach that bar.

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: notable, at the 4 mg dose.** The label's primary endpoint was at least 80% scalp hair coverage, a SALT score of 20 or below, at week 36. In Trial AA-1 that was reached by 35% on 4 mg against 5% on placebo, a difference of 30 percentage points (95% CI 23 to 36); in Trial AA-2, 32% against 3%, again a 30 point difference (95% CI 23 to 36).[^label] The 2 mg dose did roughly half as well: 22% and 17%, differences of 16 and 15 points.
- **Evidence quality: solid.** Two randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trials enrolling 1,200 adults between them, with consistent results across both, supporting an FDA indication.[^label] That is as good as the evidence base gets for a hair-loss drug.
- **What the headline number leaves out.** Roughly two thirds of patients on 4 mg did not reach the endpoint. On the stricter measure of at least 90% coverage (SALT 10 or below) the figures fall to 26% and 24% on 4 mg, and the 2 mg result in Trial AA-2 carries the label's own footnote that it was "Not statistically significant under the multiplicity control plan".[^label] Report the 35% with the 65% beside it.
- **Safety: this is the part that decides it.** The label carries a boxed warning headed "SERIOUS INFECTIONS, MORTALITY, MALIGNANCY, MAJOR ADVERSE CARDIOVASCULAR EVENTS (MACE), and THROMBOSIS", including increased risk of "serious bacterial, fungal, viral and opportunistic infections leading to hospitalization or death, including tuberculosis", with latent TB testing required before and during therapy.[^label] Nothing on this site balances a cosmetic benefit against that; severe alopecia areata is a disease with real psychological burden, and the trade is a clinician's to make with a patient.

## What We Actually Know

It works for a minority of people with severe disease, by a lot when it works, and the trials ran to week 36 so the label figures describe nine months rather than a lifetime. Whether hair is kept after stopping is not something the indications section answers.

## How It Works

As a Janus kinase inhibitor, the same class as [[upadacitinib]] and [[abrocitinib]], both of which are graded here for atopic dermatitis rather than hair. The mechanism argument belongs to those pages and is not repeated.

## The Evidence

The two trials were AA-1 (NCT03570749) and AA-2 (NCT03899259), enrolling adults with at least 50% scalp hair loss by the Severity of Alopecia Tool for more than six months, males 18 to 60 and females 18 to 70.[^label] The enrolled population was 61% female, 2% aged 65 or older, and 52% White, 36% Asian and 8% Black.[^label] It was a severely affected group: 53% had at least 95% scalp hair loss at baseline, 34% had been in the current episode for at least four years, 69% had significant gaps in eyebrow hair or none, and 58% the same for eyelashes.[^label]

That baseline severity is the main thing limiting how far these numbers travel. They describe people with extensive, established, long-running hair loss, which is who the drug is indicated for, and they say nothing about patchy or recent alopecia areata and nothing at all about pattern hair loss, which is a different disease covered on [[hair-loss-thinning]].

## Uses

Severe alopecia areata in adults and in patients aged 12 and over, by prescription. The originator approval is NDA207924 to Eli Lilly, covering 1 mg, 2 mg and 4 mg tablets, and generic baricitinib is now listed under ANDA217585.[^fda] The same tablet is separately indicated for rheumatoid arthritis and for COVID-19 in hospitalised adults, neither of which is a skin use.[^label]

## Common Marketing Claims

There are none to quarantine. Baricitinib is a prescription drug promoted to clinicians rather than a cosmetic ingredient with a consumer product page, and nothing read for this profile makes a direct-to-consumer claim. What a reader is likely to encounter instead is a summary stating the 35% figure without the 65% that sits beside it, or describing a JAK inhibitor as a hair-growth treatment without the boxed warning.
