Polyglyceryl-10 diisostearate is a polyglycerol fatty acid ester: a ten-unit polyglycerin backbone carrying two isostearic acid chains. In the surfactant literature the same molecule is called decaglycerol diisostearate, abbreviated DG2IS.[^dg2is] It is one of the non-ionic emulsifiers that make a cleansing oil rinse away with water instead of needing a second wash.

> **This is formulation chemistry, not an active, and the evidence base is thinner than for the alkyl glucosides.** Nothing found for this page tests what it does to skin, and PubMed holds no contact-allergy report for this ester at all. The nearest dermatology record is a single 2008 case report about polyglyceryl laurate, a different ester in the same family, published without an abstract, so nothing beyond its existence can be read here.[^laurate] Graded `weak` for the same reason as [[caprylyl-capryl-glucoside]] and [[decyl-glucoside]]: a formulation function is not a skin benefit.

## The Rubric

**Non-ionic emulsifier and vesicle former (functional, not a skin-health active)**
- **Effect size: none on skin itself.** Its job is to hold oil and water together. The one primary study found here that names the molecule is a formulation-chemistry paper, not a skin study: using supercritical CO2, "decaglycerol distearate (DG2S) and decaglycerol diisostearate (DG2IS) formed niosomes", the non-ionic cousins of liposomes, and the diisostearate's vesicles "had higher trapping efficiencies and dispersion stabilities than those of DG2S because the membrane fluidity of the DG2IS niosomes was greater".[^dg2is] That is a result about carrier particles in a lab, not about anybody's face.
- **Evidence quality: not applicable to benefit.** No trial, case series or instrumental measurement of a skin outcome for this ingredient was found. Three PubMed searches on 2026-11-09 returned three records for the molecule by name, none of them a clinical or instrumental study of an effect on skin.[^searches]

**How many chains it carries decides what it does**
- **Effect size: a clean structure-function result, still entirely in the formulation domain.** The same paper varied the degree of esterification on the identical decaglycerol backbone: the monostearate, "which has a high hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) value, yielded a solution of spherical micelles", while the tristearate, "which has a low HLB value, yielded a gel-like solution".[^dg2is] Only the di-esters made vesicles. This is why an ingredient list naming polyglyceryl-10 diisostearate is telling you something different from one naming a mono- or tri-ester, even though all three share a name stem.
- **Evidence quality: solid for the chemistry, silent on skin.**

## The Evidence

**Tolerability, and the claim this page will not repeat.** The 2016 paper describes this class of surfactants as "made from naturally derived materials and are neither harmful to the human body nor to the environment".[^dg2is] That sentence is the authors' framing in a paper about vesicle preparation, and it is reported here as their assertion rather than as a finding, because the study presents no toxicity, irritation or sensitisation data of any kind. A formulation-chemistry paper is not a safety assessment, and "naturally derived" is not a safety argument.

What can be said is narrower and more useful: searching PubMed for contact allergy across this family on 2026-11-09 returned one case report, and it concerns polyglyceryl laurate rather than this ester.[^searches][^laurate] That is an absence of evidence rather than evidence of safety, and it contrasts with the alkyl glucosides, where a 5775-patient patch-test series produced a real family-level signal, set out on [[caprylyl-capryl-glucoside]].

## Common Marketing Claims

No brand copy is quoted here, because brands do not market this ingredient. It is an emulsifier that appears mid-list on a cleansing oil, and the marketing attached to a product containing it belongs to that product rather than to this molecule, which is where [[biodance-collagen-cleansing-oil]] handles its own collagen claim. The one promotional-sounding sentence found anywhere in the sources for this page, that these surfactants are neither harmful to people nor to the environment, comes from the study authors rather than from a brand, and is quarantined as their assertion in The Evidence above.
