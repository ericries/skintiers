Luliconazole is a prescription topical azole antifungal, sold as Luzu Cream 1% under NDA 204153, held by Bausch and currently marketed.[^fda] Its FDA-approved use is "the topical treatment of interdigital tinea pedis, tinea cruris, and tinea corporis caused by the organisms Trichophyton rubrum and Epidermophyton floccosum".[^label] It is applied once daily, and the course is short: two weeks for [[athletes-foot|interdigital tinea pedis]], one week for [[tinea-cruris-jock-itch|tinea cruris]] or [[tinea-corporis-ringworm|tinea corporis]].[^label]

> **The short course is the selling point, and the cure rate is the catch.** One week of once-daily cream for jock itch or ringworm is a genuinely convenient regimen next to the two-to-four-week courses on most [[otc-antifungal-creams-by-labelled-scope|over-the-counter antifungal labels]]. But read what the registration trials actually counted. In the two athlete's foot trials, complete clearance four weeks after treatment reached 26% and 14%, against 2% and 3% on vehicle. The drug clearly beats its vehicle. It also leaves most treated people short of complete clearance.

## The Rubric

**Interdigital tinea pedis, tinea cruris and tinea corporis (topical, prescription)**

- **Effect size: modest on the strictest endpoint, larger on the laboratory one.** Across the registration trials, complete clearance ran 26% and 14% (athlete's foot), 21% (tinea cruris) and 71% (tinea corporis), against vehicle rates of 2%, 3%, 4% and 36%.[^label] Mycological cure, meaning a negative KOH exam and a negative fungal culture, ran much higher: 62% and 56% in athlete's foot against 18% and 27%.[^label] The gap between those two numbers is the honest story. The fungus is usually killed; the skin often still looks and feels affected at the follow-up visit.
- **Evidence quality: good.** Four randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled multicentre trials behind an FDA approval, with culture-confirmed diagnosis at entry and assessment weeks after treatment stopped rather than on the last day of it.[^label] That is a better evidence base than most ingredients on this site have.

## How It Works

An azole, the same broad class as [[clotrimazole]], [[ketoconazole]] and miconazole, all of which interfere with fungal ergosterol synthesis. The class mechanism is argued on those pages and is not repeated here. What distinguishes luliconazole in practice is potency against dermatophytes at low concentration, which is what makes a one-week course plausible where older azoles are labelled for two to four.

## The Evidence

**Athlete's foot, two trials, 423 subjects.** Once-daily application to the forefoot and web spaces for 14 days, assessed four weeks after treatment ended. Complete clearance, defined as both clinical and mycological cure, was 28 of 106 (26%) versus 2 of 103 (2%) in the first trial and 15 of 107 (14%) versus 3 of 107 (3%) in the second.[^label] Mycological cure alone was 62% versus 18% and 56% versus 27%.[^label]

**Tinea cruris, one trial, 256 subjects.** Seven days of once-daily treatment, assessed three weeks later. Complete clearance 35 of 165 (21%) versus 4 of 91 (4%); effective treatment 43% versus 19%.[^label]

**Tinea corporis, one trial, and the number to be careful with.** Complete clearance was 36 of 51 (71%) on luliconazole versus 5 of 14 (36%) on vehicle.[^label] Both figures are out of line with every other arm in the programme, and the reason to distrust the comparison is the comparator: fourteen people. A vehicle arm that small gives a very imprecise estimate, and a 36% spontaneous-clearance rate against 2% to 4% in the other trials suggests these were easier infections, not that the vehicle worked. The 71% is real but it is not evidence that luliconazole works better on ringworm than on athlete's foot.

**Tolerability.** The label reports application site reactions in under 1% of subjects as the most common adverse reaction in trials.[^label]
