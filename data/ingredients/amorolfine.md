Amorolfine is a topical antifungal painted on nails weekly, sold in Europe and elsewhere as a 5% lacquer and not available in the United States. **Bottom line:** a 2007 Cochrane review flagged it as the one nail topical that "might be substantially more effective" than the alternatives, and when an independent trial finally tested that in 2024, nobody taking it was cured. The condition is at [[onychomycosis]].

> **In the trial that tested it against placebo, the complete-cure count for amorolfine was zero.** The Onycho Trial randomised primary-care patients with mild to moderately severe onychomycosis to once-weekly amorolfine 5% lacquer, once-daily miconazole, or placebo, and reported that "none of the participants receiving miconazole or amorolfine reached complete cure compared with two in the placebo group".[^onycho] On two secondary outcomes amorolfine did measurably worse than placebo: mycological cure "OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.92, p=0.029" and clinical improvement "OR 0.25, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.85, p=0.021".[^onycho]

## The Rubric

**Curing toenail onychomycosis**
- **Effect size: none, on the best available test.** Zero complete cures in the amorolfine arm against two in placebo, and no significant difference on clinical cure.[^onycho] The authors' conclusion is unambiguous: these treatments "should, therefore, not be advised as monotherapy to treat onychomycosis".[^onycho]
- **Evidence quality: gold-standard, and that is what makes the result count.** "Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, with computer-generated treatment allocation at a 1:1:1 ratio", conducted in primary care from February 2020 to August 2022, with a declared absence of competing interests.[^onycho] An independent trial with no commercial sponsor reporting a null result is the strongest kind of evidence this site encounters, and it points the wrong way for this ingredient.

**The 2007 hint that put it on the map**
- **Effect size: not applicable; a superseded signal.** The Cochrane review of topical treatments for fungal foot infections said of toenails that "ciclopiroxolamine and butenafine are both effective but they both need to be applied daily for prolonged periods (at least 1 year)" and that "topical ciclopiroxolamine has poor cure rates and that amorolfine might be substantially more effective but more research is required".[^cochrane]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, and explicitly provisional.** The reviewers asked for more research and attached the hedge themselves. The 2024 trial is that research, and it did not confirm the hint. Where the two conflict, the placebo-controlled trial wins; the comparison across topical antifungals is on [[otc-antifungals-by-evidence]].

**Availability in the United States**
- **Effect size: not applicable; a practical finding.** Three separate United States drug databases return nothing for amorolfine: no DailyMed Structured Product Label, no openFDA drug-label record, and no Drugs@FDA record.[^us] Anyone reading about it from a US perspective is reading about a product they cannot obtain through normal channels.
- **Evidence quality: not applicable.** Absence from these three databases is strong evidence it is not approved or marketed in the United States, not proof it never was.

## The Evidence

**One real defence of the ingredient, stated fairly.** The Onycho Trial measured cure at six months, while the Cochrane review's own observation about nail topicals is that they need daily application "for prolonged periods (at least 1 year)".[^onycho][^cochrane] Amorolfine is applied weekly rather than daily, so a six-month endpoint may simply be too early for this drug. That is a genuine limitation of the trial rather than an excuse, and it does not rescue the complete-cure count, which was zero rather than small.

**How to read "less effective than placebo".** The two significant results favouring placebo rest on small numbers and wide confidence intervals, both reaching to 0.92 at the upper bound.[^onycho] The honest reading is the authors' own, that the drugs were not effective, rather than that painting amorolfine on a nail actively makes fungus worse. Nothing here supports that stronger claim.

**Why this matters beyond one ingredient.** Amorolfine earned attention here specifically because a respected systematic review said it might be substantially better. It turned out the hint was seventeen years old and the follow-up was negative. A provisional sentence in a good review poses a research question rather than settling one, and what happened when this one was answered is the whole substance of this page.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Thickened or discoloured nails are not always fungal, confirming the organism matters before treating, and oral antifungals remain the better-evidenced route for genuine onychomycosis; see [[onychomycosis]] and [[terbinafine]]. Nothing here is a reason to stop a treatment a clinician has prescribed.

## Common Marketing Claims

No brand or retailer copy is quoted on this page, because amorolfine is not marketed in the United States and no brand page was fetched. The claim that put it on this site's radar came not from a brand but from a 2007 Cochrane review, which hedged it at the time and has now been overtaken.[^cochrane][^onycho]
