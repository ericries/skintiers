Microplastics in cosmetics covers two claims that are usually run together: a narrow one about solid plastic scrub particles, which lawmakers in the United States, England, and the European Union have banned or restricted under written definitions, and a much broader one that counts hundreds of everyday cosmetic polymers, including soluble thickeners and liquid polymers, as microplastic ingredients.[^us][^uk][^eu][^btmb]

> The regulated problem is solid plastic: water-insoluble particles 5 mm or smaller, which the EU restriction's recitals call "extremely persistent, practically impossible to remove from the environment once emitted".[^eu] Rinse-off cosmetics with plastic microbeads were phased out in the US between 2017 and 2019, England's ban took effect in 2018, and the EU is phasing out solid polymer microparticles across all cosmetics on a schedule running to 2035.[^us][^uk][^eu] The broader lists that flag polymers such as carbomer, PVP, and dimethicone reach past those legal definitions: the EU excluded water-soluble and degradable polymers from its restriction because they "do not possess the same long-term persistence and, therefore, do not contribute to the identified risk".[^eu][^btmb]

## The Rubric

The term carries one environmental claim and one definitional claim, and they grade differently.

**Solid plastic microbeads as persistent water pollution**
- **Claim status: established in law across three jurisdictions.** The EU restriction's recitals state that "microplastic pollution is extremely persistent, practically impossible to remove from the environment once emitted" and that "it accumulates progressively in the environment."[^eu] The US barred rinse-off cosmetics containing "intentionally-added plastic microbeads",[^us] England barred microbeads, defined as "any water-insoluble solid plastic particle of less than or equal to 5mm in any dimension", from rinse-off personal care products,[^uk] and the EU restricted solid synthetic polymer microparticles across cosmetics generally.[^eu]
- **Evidence quality: solid (a consistent regulatory record).** Three instruments, enacted in 2015, 2017 and 2023, define the restricted particle in materially the same way: solid, plastic, 5 mm or smaller.[^us][^uk][^eu]

**Everyday cosmetic polymers as microplastic pollution (the broad claim)**
- **Claim status: broader than every definition in force.** The Beat the Microbead campaign counts "more than 500 microplastic ingredients" and its red-flag markers include acrylates copolymer, carbomer, nylon, polyurethane, PVP, and [[dimethicone]].[^btmb] The legal definitions all require a solid, water-insoluble particle, and the EU restriction excludes water-soluble polymers (solubility above 2 g/L) and degradable polymers outright, with the recitals recording that these "do not possess the same long-term persistence and, therefore, do not contribute to the identified risk."[^eu][^us][^uk]
- **Evidence quality: precautionary, by the list authors' own description.** The campaign's orange category is defined as "skeptical microplastic" ingredients, meaning "synthetic polymers for which there is not enough information available", and also covers "carbon-free, soluble, and liquid polymers".[^btmb]

**Health harm from polymer ingredients in cosmetics**
- **Claim status: not what the bans were based on.** The stated concern behind the EU restriction is environmental persistence and accumulation,[^eu] and the US and English bans reach only rinse-off products, the class whose use ends with removal "by washing or rinsing with water".[^us][^uk] Cosmetic chemist Michelle Wong writes that "there are concerns around the health impacts of microplastics" while cautioning that "overblowing the concerns, and switching to 'eco' alternatives without looking at evidence, actually goes against the underlying goals of better human and environmental health".[^lm]

## The Evidence

The evidence here is the regulatory record: what each instrument defines, prohibits, and leaves alone.

**United States.** The Microbead-Free Waters Act of 2015 amended the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act to prohibit "the manufacture or the introduction or delivery for introduction into interstate commerce of a rinse-off cosmetic that contains intentionally-added plastic microbeads". It defines a plastic microbead as "any solid plastic particle that is less than five millimeters in size and is intended to be used to exfoliate or cleanse the human body or any part thereof", and specifies that the term rinse-off cosmetic "includes toothpaste". The ban applied to manufacturing from July 1, 2017 and to introduction into commerce from July 1, 2018, with both dates one year later for rinse-off cosmetics that are nonprescription drugs.[^us] The statute reaches only rinse-off cosmetics; leave-on products are outside it.[^us]

**England.** The Environmental Protection (Microbeads) (England) Regulations 2017, made on 19 December 2017, prohibit using microbeads as an ingredient in the manufacture of rinse-off personal care products and supplying such products. A microbead is "any water-insoluble solid plastic particle of less than or equal to 5mm in any dimension", and plastic is defined as a synthetic polymeric substance that "retains its final manufactured shape during use in its intended applications". A rinse-off product is one whose application ends with "the prompt and specific removal of the product (or any residue of the product) by washing or rinsing with water". The manufacturing prohibition came into force 21 days after the regulations were made, the supply prohibition six months after.[^uk]

**European Union.** Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2055 added entry 78 to Annex XVII of REACH, restricting synthetic polymer microparticles: solid polymer particles 5 mm or smaller, restricted once present at 0.01% by weight or more. The definition excludes natural polymers that have not been chemically modified, degradable polymers, water-soluble polymers (solubility above 2 g/L), and polymers without carbon atoms; the recitals record that the excluded classes "do not possess the same long-term persistence and, therefore, do not contribute to the identified risk". For microbeads used as abrasives, "no transitional period was proposed, as industry was expected to have voluntarily phased out their use by 2020". Other cosmetics received transition periods: the restriction applies to rinse-off cosmetics from 17 October 2027, to leave-on cosmetics from 17 October 2029, and to make-up, lip and nail products from 17 October 2035.[^eu]

**The broad lists.** Beat the Microbead, a Plastic Soup Foundation campaign, maintains "a list of more than 500 microplastic ingredients that are widely used in cosmetics & personal care products", drawing on the ECHA restriction proposal and UNEP reports, and sorts products into red, orange, green, and zero categories.[^btmb] Its red list covers "synthetic polymers which are commonly considered to be microplastic ingredients", and its red-flag markers include acrylates copolymer, carbomer, nylon, polyurethane, PVP, and [[dimethicone]], a silicone occlusive that a peer-reviewed dermatology review of moisturizers describes as "hypoallergenic, noncomedogenic and nonacnegenic".[^btmb][^kang] The orange category is defined as "skeptical microplastic" ingredients, "synthetic polymers for which there is not enough information available", and also covers "carbon-free, soluble, and liquid polymers".[^btmb] Those groupings are wider than the definitions in force: a dissolved or liquid polymer is not a solid, water-insoluble particle, and the EU restriction excludes water-soluble and degradable polymers by name.[^us][^uk][^eu]

**On the studies behind microplastic headlines.** Michelle Wong, a chemistry PhD who writes on cosmetic science, reports that "a lot of microplastic studies have issues in the way they're conducted that make their results pretty meaningless", and that "a lot of the common issues cause false positives or exaggerated numbers, which leads to exciting, headline-grabbing results".[^lm]

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below carries more than The Evidence above supports.

- **"This product contains microplastics" (scanner-app red flags on leave-on products).** The flag often rests on list membership rather than on a legal definition: the campaign's own orange category covers "carbon-free, soluble, and liquid polymers" and polymers "for which there is not enough information available",[^btmb] while the EU restriction excludes water-soluble and degradable polymers because they "do not possess the same long-term persistence and, therefore, do not contribute to the identified risk."[^eu]
- **"Plastic-free" as a safety upgrade.** The microplastic restrictions are environmental measures aimed at persistent solid particles.[^eu] Wong, who states plainly that "there are concerns around the health impacts of microplastics", argues that switching to alternatives without looking at evidence "goes against the underlying goals of better human and environmental health".[^lm]
- **"Microbeads are still hiding in your face wash."** US manufacture of rinse-off cosmetics with intentionally-added plastic microbeads ended July 1, 2017 and their introduction into commerce July 1, 2018, a year later for those that are nonprescription drugs;[^us] England's prohibitions took effect in 2018;[^uk] and the EU recitals record that industry "was expected to have voluntarily phased out their use by 2020".[^eu]
- **"Your serum is liquid plastic."** The polymers this points at are the ones the regulatory definitions leave out: every instrument in force restricts solid, water-insoluble particles, England's regulations define plastic by its ability to hold "its final manufactured shape during use in its intended applications", and the EU excludes soluble and degradable polymers from its definition.[^us][^uk][^eu]
