Benzoyl peroxide (BPO) is a topical oxidizing agent that releases reactive oxygen radicals, killing *Cutibacterium acnes* (formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*) on contact.[^3][^4] It is sold both over-the-counter and by prescription, and it is one of the oldest and most universally recommended first-line acne treatments. It has a distinctive property: no bacterial resistance to it has been documented, and it suppresses the emergence of antibiotic resistance when co-formulated with a topical antibiotic.[^4][^5] By GRADE, the certainty of its efficacy evidence is low, and its clinical effect is modest.[^1]

## The Rubric

Grades are per use. Framing below is relative and names comparators.

**Acne (mild-to-moderate; and as the base of a combination product)**
- **Effect size: modest.** BPO reliably beats vehicle, but only by a modest margin, and in head-to-head pooling it is statistically indistinguishable from the alternatives. The Cochrane review found BPO improved acne versus placebo (risk ratio 1.27; 95% CI 1.12-1.45) yet showed "little to no difference" versus adapalene (RR 0.99; 95% CI 0.90-1.10) or topical clindamycin (RR 0.95; 95% CI 0.68-1.34).[^1] In a 179-RCT network meta-analysis, BPO monotherapy landed mid-pack (15.60% lesion reduction vs placebo, 95% CrI 6.02-25.11; rank ~25.5 of 49 treatment classes), well behind BPO-plus-retinoid combinations.[^2] "Modest. On par with adapalene and topical clindamycin, another mid-tier topical alongside [[azelaic-acid]]; not a standout, and clearly bettered by combination regimens."
- **Evidence quality: mixed.** Decades of trials and near-universal first-line status, yet the Cochrane review rated the certainty of the key participant-reported improvement outcome **low**, and the withdrawal outcomes **very low**; the network meta-analysis rated its trial pool "moderate to very low" quality with high overall uncertainty.[^1][^2] "Mixed. A large body of evidence that GRADE rates low-certainty. Better-graded than a single small RCT, worse than the high-quality rating [[azelaic-acid]] earned for rosacea."

## What We Actually Know

BPO reliably outperforms vehicle for mild-to-moderate acne, but by a modest margin, and in direct comparison it is statistically no better (and no worse) than adapalene or topical clindamycin.[^1] Combination products (BPO plus a retinoid, or BPO plus an antibiotic) clearly outperform BPO alone.[^2] Its distinctive property is its resistance profile: as a non-specific oxidizer it kills *C. acnes* by a mechanism antibiotics cannot select against, no resistance to BPO has been documented, and co-formulating it with clindamycin prevents the resistance that clindamycin alone would drive.[^4][^5] The main cost is irritation: withdrawals for adverse effects run at roughly twice the placebo rate.[^1] By GRADE, that large body of evidence is rated low-certainty.[^1]

## How It Works

The FDA prescribing information describes the mechanism tersely: "Benzoyl peroxide is an oxidizing agent with bactericidal and keratolytic effects."[^3] Mechanistically, BPO decomposes in skin to release reactive oxygen radicals that non-specifically oxidize bacterial proteins and membranes. An in vitro study documented this directly, reporting a "significant decrease of viable counts of *C. acnes*" at 0.5, 1, and 2 mmol/L BPO within 15 to 60 minutes of exposure, alongside comparable killing of *S. aureus* and *S. epidermidis*.[^4] Because the mechanism is blunt oxidative damage rather than a specific molecular target, bacteria do not readily evolve around it: a 2025 in vitro study stated flatly that "to date, there is no evidence of *C. acnes* resistance to BPO," and showed that "bacterial cultures repeatedly exposed to the combination of clindamycin and BPO did not develop antibiotic resistance, which occurred with exposure to clindamycin alone."[^5] The keratolytic (drying, desquamative) action contributes to comedone treatment.[^3]

## The Evidence

Primary clinical evidence:

**Efficacy vs placebo.** The Cochrane systematic review (Yang et al., 2020) found that BPO as monotherapy or add-on "may be more effective than placebo or no treatment," pooling a risk ratio of 1.27 (95% CI 1.12-1.45) across 3 trials and 2,234 participants, and rated this **low-certainty** evidence.[^1] A 12-week multicenter, placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blind Japanese RCT (Kawashima et al., 2017) reported median inflammatory-lesion reductions of 72.7% (2.5% BPO) and 75.0% (5% BPO), "significantly higher than that in the placebo group (41.7%)."[^6]

**Head-to-head vs other topicals.** The same Cochrane review found "little to no difference" between BPO and either adapalene or clindamycin: RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.90-1.10; 5 trials, 1,472 participants) versus adapalene, and RR 0.95 (95% CI 0.68-1.34; 1 trial, 240 participants) versus clindamycin, both low-certainty.[^1] The Mavranezouli network meta-analysis (2022, 179 RCTs, ~35,000 observations, 49 treatment classes) placed BPO monotherapy mid-table for mild-to-moderate acne (15.60% reduction vs placebo; 95% CrI 6.02-25.11), with combinations ranking substantially higher (BPO + retinoid 26.16%, 95% CrI 16.75-35.36), and noted BPO "and topical retinoids were less well tolerated than placebo."[^2]

**Tolerability / withdrawals.** Cochrane found participant withdrawal due to adverse effects more than doubled versus placebo (RR 2.13; 95% CI 1.55-2.93; 24 trials, 13,744 participants; low-certainty), with the common causes being "erythema, pruritus, and skin burning."[^1] Kawashima reported adverse-event incidences of 56.4% (2.5%) and 58.8% (5%) versus placebo, and stated that "adverse events were mild or moderate in severity."[^6]

**Antibacterial / anti-resistance.** In vitro, BPO is rapidly bactericidal against *C. acnes* across strains,[^4] no resistance to it has been documented, and fixed BPO-antibiotic combinations prevented the resistance that antibiotic monotherapy induced.[^5]

**Regulatory status (noted separately from trial data).** BPO is FDA-labeled for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris, including in fixed combinations such as adapalene/benzoyl peroxide gel (initial U.S. approval 2008), and only for acne.[^3]

## Uses

Well-supported: mild-to-moderate acne vulgaris, where BPO is an inexpensive, resistance-proof first-line topical whose effect is modest and no greater than adapalene or topical clindamycin, on low-certainty evidence.[^1] It is also used as the base of a combination, pairing BPO with a topical antibiotic to suppress resistance, or with a retinoid for greater efficacy than either alone.[^2][^5]

## Common Marketing Claims

Treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Higher strength = better / stronger clears faster."** In a head-to-head RCT, 5% BPO reduced inflammatory lesions 75.0% versus 72.7% for 2.5%, a 2.3-point difference, while both far exceeded placebo (41.7%).[^6] Discounted.
- **"Kills acne-causing bacteria as a headline cure."** BPO is bactericidal against *C. acnes* in vitro,[^4] but the clinical effect is only modest (RR 1.27 vs placebo) and no better than adapalene or clindamycin, one of which is not antibacterial.[^1] Discounted.
- **"Spot treatment clears blemishes overnight."** The trials that establish BPO's effect run 10 to 12 weeks,[^1][^6] and the label states that improvement is most likely during the first four weeks of use.[^3] Discounted.
