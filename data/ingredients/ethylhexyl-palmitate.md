Ethylhexyl palmitate is a synthetic emollient ester, the 2-ethylhexyl ester of palmitic acid, used as a light skin-feel agent and as the oil phase of cleansing balms. It is a texture ingredient with no demonstrated active benefit, which is why it is graded weak here and why it is nonetheless worth knowing: it is the first or second ingredient in several of the cleansing balms on this site.

> **Its reputation is older than its evidence.** Under its former name octyl palmitate it appears on the best-known list of comedogenic cosmetic ingredients, Fulton's 1984 rabbit-ear survey.[^fulton] That list is still quoted as settled fact. Fulton's own sentence about the method is "The data presented were gleaned from the rabbit ear assay, which is not an ideal animal model but is the best we have".[^fulton] No human comedogenicity trial of this ester turned up in the search behind this page.

## The Rubric

**Light, dry-touch emollient feel**
Graded **notable on anecdotal evidence**. This is a formulation property rather than a trial result. The molecule is a branched C8 alcohol esterified to a C16 fatty acid, formula C24H48O2 at 368.6 g/mol, which is what makes it a thin, fast-spreading oil rather than a heavy one.[^pubchem] Nothing cited here measures skin-feel, so the grade reflects what the chemistry predicts and what formulators use it for, not a published outcome.

**Dissolving makeup and sunscreen as a cleansing-oil base**
Graded **notable on anecdotal evidence**. Dissolving oily soil is solvency rather than pharmacology, it is not in dispute, and no trial measures it. This is its main job on this site: it leads the formula on [[clinique-take-the-day-off-cleansing-balm]], [[banila-co-clean-it-zero-cleansing-balm-original]], [[heimish-all-clean-balm]] and [[anua-heartleaf-pore-control-cleansing-oil]].

**Any active skin benefit**
Graded **none**. No controlled trial attributes barrier repair, anti-ageing, brightening or soothing to this ester. It is in formulas to carry other things and to make them feel good. For emollients with an actual evidence base, see [[glycerin]] and [[petrolatum]].

**Avoiding clogged pores on acne-prone skin**
Graded **unknown on anecdotal evidence**, and this is the row people actually want answered. The honest position is below: the comedogenic label rests on a rabbit assay whose own author hedged it, and the follow-up rabbit study that retested a dozen esters did not include this one.[^fulton][^nguyen]

## The Evidence

**Where the comedogenic reputation comes from.** Fulton's 1984 survey in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology lists the ingredient offenders as "isopropyl myristate and its analogs, such as isopropyl palmitate, isopropyl isostearate, butyl stearate, isostearyl neopentanoate, myristyl myristate, decyl oleate, octyl stearate, octyl palmitate or isocetyl stearate".[^fulton] Octyl palmitate is this compound: PubChem records "Octyl palmitate" as a synonym of 2-ethylhexyl palmitate, CID 62851.[^pubchem] So the entry in every comedogenicity chart traces to that one paper.

**Two reasons to hold the label loosely.** The first is the paper's own caveat about the rabbit ear being "not an ideal animal model".[^fulton] The second is what the method does: it applies raw ingredients to the rabbit external ear canal, which Fulton's successors describe as a test of whether chemicals "caused comedone formation on topical application" and of "follicular hyperkeratosis in the rabbit ear assay".[^nguyen] A raw ingredient applied to a rabbit's ear canal is not a finished formula on a human face, and neither abstract states the application schedule, so the exposure cannot be compared directly.

**The retest skipped it.** A 2007 rabbit study from Maibach's group quantified the comedogenic potential of isopropyl palmitate, isopropyl myristate, butyl stearate, isopropyl isostearate, decyl oleate, isostearyl neopentanoate, isocetyl stearate, myristyl myristate, cocoa butter, cetyl alcohol, paraffin, stearyl alcohol, sodium lauryl sulfate and petrolatum, reporting that "The first nine were deemed positive".[^nguyen] Checked against that list, ethylhexyl palmitate and octyl palmitate are absent, so the 2007 work neither confirms nor clears it.

**Contact time cuts the question in half.** In a cleansing balm the ester is emulsified and rinsed within a minute, which is a reason to expect less exposure than a leave-on, and not a published finding. In a leave-on it stays. Of the nine products here that declare it, four are the rinse-off cleansers named above and five are leave-ons: [[aestura-atobarrier-365-cream]], [[torriden-dive-in-low-molecular-hyaluronic-acid-cream]], [[neutrogena-rapid-wrinkle-repair-retinol-serum]], the stick sunscreen [[beauty-of-joseon-matte-sun-stick]] and the body oil [[mederma-quick-dry-oil]]. If you are acne-prone and want to be cautious, that is the distinction to apply, rather than avoiding the ingredient everywhere.

**One formula worth a second look.** [[anua-heartleaf-pore-control-cleansing-oil]] is sold on pore control and leads with the ester whose reputation is pore-clogging. On the evidence above that is not a contradiction so much as a reminder that the reputation is weakly founded. It is still the kind of thing a reader deserves to notice themselves.

**No safety assessment is cited here.** The Cosmetic Ingredient Review's own report on alkyl esters was not accessible when this page was written, returning HTTP 403, and a bounded PubMed search found no indexed CIR assessment covering this ester. So this page makes no safety claim beyond the identity and comedogenicity evidence above.

## Common Marketing Claims

No brand copy is quoted in this section. Ethylhexyl palmitate is a formulation ingredient rather than a marketed hero active, so no single brand page was fetched for it, and the claim patterns below are described rather than quoted.

**Lightweight and non-greasy, as texture descriptions.** Fair accounts of how the ester behaves and the reason formulators choose it over heavier oils. Neither is a skin outcome.

**Non-comedogenic, printed on a product that contains it.** There is no agreed test behind that phrase and no regulatory definition of it. Given that the only evidence either way for this ester is a rabbit assay its own author hedged, a product can carry the phrase without contradicting anything, and without it meaning much.

**Any claim that it hydrates or repairs.** An occlusive film reduces water loss, which is true of most oils and esters. No trial attributes hydration or repair to this ingredient specifically.
