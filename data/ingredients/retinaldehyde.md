Retinaldehyde (retinal) is the aldehyde form of vitamin A, an over-the-counter cosmetic [[retinoids|retinoid]] that sits one oxidation step below the receptor-active acid on the vitamin A pathway. That pathway runs retinyl ester to [[retinol]] to retinaldehyde to retinoic acid ([[tretinoin]]), each step an oxidation; because retinaldehyde is only one step from the active molecule rather than two, Saurat and colleagues found it induced the retinoic-acid marker CRABP-2 more strongly than retinol did, ranking "retinoic acid > retinaldehyde > 9 cis retinoic acid > retinol > beta carotene."[^1] It is the closest-to-active retinoid available without a prescription, and it anchors a growing premium-serum category (examples include Avene RetrinAL, Medik8 Crystal Retinal, and The INKEY List Retinal). Its clinical evidence is thinner than prescription tretinoin's but better than that of the retinyl esters, and this profile grades it against both.

## The Rubric

Grades below are relative and name comparators. They cover retinaldehyde's cosmetic uses, not proof that any finished serum performs.

**Photoaging (fine wrinkles, roughness, texture)**
- **Effect size: modest.** Vehicle-controlled and concentration-comparison studies show real improvement in wrinkles, roughness, and texture over several months. In the one trial with a vehicle arm, 0.05% retinaldehyde and 0.05% retinoic acid both significantly reduced wrinkle and roughness features at week 18 while the vehicle did not.[^2] This is the strongest over-the-counter retinoid evidence, ahead of the thin vehicle-controlled data behind retinol and the retinyl esters, but the effect is gradual and has not been shown to match prescription [[tretinoin]]. "Modest: measurable wrinkle and texture improvement, the best-evidenced OTC retinoid, slower and unproven against prescription retinoic acid."
- **Evidence quality: solid (for an OTC retinoid).** One vehicle-controlled trial (n=125) plus two independent concentration-comparison trials in different countries (n=40 Korean, n=56 Polish) converge on benefit.[^2][^3][^4] Short of gold-standard: the cohorts are small, several endpoints are instrument-based (profilometry, ultrasound, Cutometer), the two newer trials lack a vehicle arm, and the foundational work was conducted by the manufacturer's development group.[^1][^2]

**Mottled pigmentation**
- **Effect size: minimal to modest, and concentration-dependent.** In the Kwon trial only the 0.1% cream significantly improved the melanin index (by 6.5%); the 0.05% cream did not.[^3] "Minimal to modest: a small pigment effect seen only at the higher concentration in one cohort."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** A single 40-person trial without a vehicle arm.[^3]

**Tolerability**
- **Effect size: notable (better tolerated than retinoic acid).** Across cohorts retinaldehyde caused less irritation than retinoic acid and did not show phototoxic or photoallergic effects. Creidi and colleagues reported retinoic acid "caused more local irritation, and affected compliance of the patients," whereas retinaldehyde "was well tolerated during the entire study."[^2][^5]
- **Evidence quality: solid.** Tolerance data span a 229-patient dose-ranging series, a 135-patient safety population, and a 45-patient long-term follow-up (12 to 89 months).[^1][^2][^5]

## How It Works

Retinaldehyde is a natural metabolite of vitamin A that the skin oxidizes to retinoic acid, the form that binds nuclear retinoic acid receptors.[^1] Because it is one enzymatic step from that active molecule rather than the two steps retinol requires, it delivers retinoid activity without a prescription: Saurat and colleagues showed topical retinaldehyde induced CRABP-2, a retinoic-acid-responsive protein, and produced "a dose-dependent and significant increase in epidermal thickness, keratin 14 immunoreactivity, and Ki67-positive cells" in volunteers treated for one to three months.[^1] The general retinoid mechanism, and how the family compares, lives on [[retinoids]] and [[tretinoin]]; the point specific to retinaldehyde is its position one step from the active acid.

## The Evidence

The three uses have different amounts of support.

**Photoaging.** The controlled evidence comes from Creidi and colleagues, who compared 0.05% retinaldehyde cream, 0.05% retinoic acid cream, and the retinaldehyde vehicle in patients with facial photodamage, using silicone replicas of the crow's-feet area analyzed by optical profilometry.[^2] Among 125 patients studied (40 retinoic acid, 40 retinaldehyde, 45 vehicle), "at week 18, a significant reduction of the wrinkle and roughness features was observed with both retinaldehyde and retinoic acid," while "no statistically significant changes were observed with the retinaldehyde vehicle at any assessment point"; the authors concluded retinaldehyde "was efficacious and well tolerated in patients with photodamage."[^2] Two later concentration-comparison trials add independent cohorts. Kwon and colleagues applied retinaldehyde 0.1% or 0.05% twice daily for three months in 40 Korean women and reported textural improvement (13.7% and 12.6%), reduced transepidermal water loss (14.5% and 17.9%), and increased hydration (10.2% and 6.0%), with no statistical difference between the two concentrations.[^3] Deda and colleagues treated 56 women for 24 weeks with stabilized retinaldehyde and found, by high-frequency ultrasound, that "statistically significant increases in dermal density were observed bilaterally on the forehead (p < 0.001), cheeks (p < 0.001), and chin (p < 0.001)," with the larger wrinkle reductions at 0.1% (forehead 71.1% median decrease, p=0.018; chin 28.5%, p=0.040).[^4] The two newer trials compared strengths rather than testing against a vehicle, so the vehicle-controlled result rests on Creidi.[^2][^3][^4]

**Pigmentation.** Only one cited trial measured pigment. In Kwon and colleagues, "only RAL 0.1% significantly improved the melanin index (by 6.5%)," and the 0.05% cream did not, making the pigment effect small and concentration-dependent in a single 40-person cohort.[^3]

**Tolerance.** Saurat and colleagues gave topical retinaldehyde at several concentrations to 229 patients and found "both 0.1 and 0.05% preparations applied on facial skin were well tolerated and allowed prolonged use (up to 3 years) in patients with inflammatory dermatoses."[^1] Creidi and colleagues, whose 135-patient safety population let them compare head to head, reported retinoic acid "caused more local irritation, and affected compliance," while retinaldehyde "was well tolerated during the entire study."[^2] A long-term review by Sachsenberg-Studer, following 45 patients who used retinaldehyde for 12 to 89 months, concluded the data "indicate a good topical tolerance of RAL in humans," that it "was much better tolerated than retinoic acid," and that "it does not have phototoxic or photo-allergic properties."[^5]

**Independence and limits.** Kwon (Korea, 2018) and Deda (Poland, 2026) are separate cohorts run by groups unconnected to the earlier work.[^3][^4] The foundational Geneva and Creidi studies were part of the manufacturer's retinaldehyde development program, so they carry an industry origin, and the newer efficacy trials omit a vehicle arm.[^1][^2][^4] No cited trial tests retinaldehyde head to head against prescription tretinoin for photoaging.

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted. Treat everything here as unproven unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Retinal is about 10 to 11 times more potent (or faster) than retinol."** This specific multiple comes from brand and retailer marketing, not from the peer-reviewed studies cited here, and no citation is given for the figure. The controlled data support only a ranked, not a quantified, difference: Saurat and colleagues found retinaldehyde induced the retinoic-acid marker CRABP-2 more than retinol did (rank order "retinoic acid > retinaldehyde > ... > retinol"), which establishes that retinaldehyde is closer to the active form, not that it is any particular number of times stronger.[^1] Discount the "10x" figure; the direction of the difference is supported, the multiplier is not.
- **"As effective as prescription tretinoin."** Not established. The one vehicle-controlled trial measured retinaldehyde and retinoic acid in parallel and found both reduced wrinkle and roughness features at week 18, but it was not designed or powered as an equivalence test, and no cited study compares the two head to head for photoaging.[^2] What the evidence does show is a tolerability gap in retinaldehyde's favor: it caused less irritation than retinoic acid.[^2][^5]
- **"Reverses aging."** Not supported. The trials report gradual, partial improvement in wrinkles, texture, and dermal density over three to six months, not reversal.[^2][^3][^4]
