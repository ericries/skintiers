Retinoids are the family of vitamin A derivatives used on skin. The family runs from prescription retinoic acid, the receptor-active form, out through the over-the-counter esters, [[retinol]], and [[retinaldehyde]], to a set of newer cosmetic derivatives. They are not interchangeable: the forms differ in how many enzymatic steps separate them from the active molecule, and in how much clinical evidence stands behind each. This hub maps the family by conversion pathway, by form, and by the strength of evidence for each form, and links to the full profile for every member.

> The retinoid family shares one active endpoint, retinoic acid, but the evidence behind each member is very different: decades of randomized trials for the prescription drugs, solid over-the-counter data for [[retinaldehyde]], thinner and mostly single-group data for the newest cosmetic esters. [[bakuchiol]] is marketed alongside them as a "retinol alternative" but is not a vitamin A derivative at all.

## The Conversion Pathway

The skin's retinoid pathway is a chain of enzymatic conversions. A retinyl ester is hydrolyzed to [[retinol]], which is then oxidized in stages to the active acid. Zasada and Budzisz describe the esters as compounds that "are very stable but first they need to be converted to retinol by cleavage of the ester bond, and in the subsequent stage into retinoic acid."[^zasada] The full chain runs:

- retinyl ester ([[retinyl-esters]]) -> [[retinol]] -> [[retinaldehyde]] -> retinoic acid ([[tretinoin]])

Each arrow is an enzymatic conversion, and each conversion is a place where activity can be lost. As a rule, the more steps a form sits from retinoic acid, the milder and weaker it is: an ester (multiple steps away) is weaker than retinol, retinol is weaker than retinaldehyde, and all of them are weaker than the acid itself. Saurat and colleagues measured this ordering in skin, ranking the forms by how strongly each induced the retinoic-acid marker CRABP-2: "the rank order for CRABP-2 increase was retinoic acid > retinaldehyde > 9 cis retinoic acid > retinol > beta carotene."[^saurat] Zasada and Budzisz report the functional consequence for the esters, which show "decreased effectiveness of anti-wrinkle properties (smaller increase in the epidermal thickness) as compared to retinol and retinoic acid."[^zasada]

Some retinoids sit off this chain. [[tretinoin]] is retinoic acid, the endpoint, so it needs no conversion. The synthetic prescription retinoids [[adapalene]], [[tazarotene]] (a prodrug hydrolyzed in skin to its active acid), and [[trifarotene]] act on the retinoic acid receptors directly rather than being converted along the ester-to-acid chain. The cosmetic ester [[hydroxypinacolone-retinoate]] (HPR) is presented by its manufacturer as binding retinoid receptors directly without the retinol-to-retinaldehyde-to-acid conversion, a claim that rests on manufacturer and in-vitro material rather than controlled human evidence (see its profile).

## The Forms

Grouped by regulatory status, with generation and pathway position. The generation labels (first through fourth) follow the classification in Cosio and colleagues' 2021 review.[^cosio]

Prescription:

- [[tretinoin]]: first-generation; retinoic acid itself, the active endpoint of the pathway and the trial-validated benchmark against which milder retinoids are measured.
- [[adapalene]]: third-generation synthetic, receptor-selective (RAR-beta and RAR-gamma). It is also the one retinoid sold over the counter as a drug: adapalene gel 0.1% carries an over-the-counter Drug Facts label for acne treatment.[^adapalene-otc]
- [[tazarotene]]: third-generation synthetic prodrug, hydrolyzed in skin to tazarotenic acid; the only topical retinoid also indicated for plaque psoriasis.
- [[trifarotene]]: fourth-generation synthetic and the newest, a near-exclusive RAR-gamma agonist, FDA-approved for acne in 2019.

Over-the-counter and cosmetic (positioned on the conversion pathway):

- [[retinaldehyde]]: one oxidation step from retinoic acid, the closest-to-active retinoid available without a prescription.
- [[retinol]]: two steps from the acid, the most-studied cosmetic retinoid.
- [[retinyl-esters]]: the far left of the pathway (retinyl palmitate, acetate, propionate, linoleate); must be cleaved to retinol before doing anything, the mildest and weakest class.
- [[hydroxypinacolone-retinoate]] (HPR): a synthetic ester of retinoic acid, sold as "Granactive Retinoid," marketed as direct-acting.
- [[retinyl-retinoate]]: a synthetic retinol-plus-retinoic-acid hybrid ester developed in Korea.

Not a retinoid:

- [[bakuchiol]] is a plant-derived compound marketed as a "retinol alternative." It is not a vitamin A derivative and sits nowhere on the conversion pathway; it is grouped with retinoids by marketing, not chemistry.

## The Rubric

Grades below are relative, name comparators, and summarize each form's own profile. They rank evidence strength, not potency alone, and each is consistent with the grade on that form's page.

Prescription retinoids (strongest evidence):

- [[tretinoin]]: photoaging notable effect, solid evidence; acne modest effect, solid evidence. The benchmark topical, backed by replicated vehicle-controlled RCTs and meta-analysis.[^huang]
- [[adapalene]], [[tazarotene]], [[trifarotene]]: FDA-approved for acne with vehicle-controlled registration and network-meta-analysis evidence; the effect over vehicle runs modest to notable and is class-typical rather than one retinoid decisively beating another.[^mavranezouli][^tan]

Best-evidenced OTC retinoid:

- [[retinaldehyde]]: photoaging modest effect, solid evidence for an OTC retinoid; one vehicle-controlled trial plus concentration-comparison cohorts.[^creidi]

Growing but thinner OTC evidence:

- [[retinol]]: a body of clinical data that is growing but thinner than retinaldehyde's; graded on its own page.

Weak or negative:

- [[retinyl-esters]]: minimal effect, low-quality evidence; the one clinical trial of a retinyl propionate cream was negative.[^mukherjee]

Thin, manufacturer- or single-group-dominated:

- [[hydroxypinacolone-retinoate]]: effect unresolvable in isolation, evidence preliminary and limited to in-vitro and combination-product data.[^wang]
- [[retinyl-retinoate]]: modest wrinkle effect versus retinol in preliminary split-face trials, all from the patent-holder's group.[^kim]

## The Evidence

The honest differentiator across the family is not potency but how much controlled evidence stands behind each form.

The prescription retinoids carry the most. Tretinoin has replicated vehicle-controlled RCTs and a 2025 meta-analysis of 8 trials (1,361 patients) for photoaging.[^huang] For acne, a 179-RCT network meta-analysis covers the topical retinoid class, including adapalene and tretinoin;[^mavranezouli] tazarotene has vehicle-controlled registration trials and a pooled analysis; and trifarotene has two phase 3 trials (2,420 patients combined).[^tan] Across these, the effect over vehicle is real and, for acne, modest and class-typical rather than one retinoid decisively outperforming another.

Among the over-the-counter forms, retinaldehyde has the strongest data: in the one trial with a vehicle arm, 0.05% retinaldehyde reduced wrinkle and roughness features while its vehicle did not.[^creidi] Retinol has a body of clinical data that is growing but thinner than retinaldehyde's; its evidence is graded on its own profile.

The retinyl esters are anchored by a negative result. Mukherjee and colleagues report that "topical retinyl propionate cream (0.15%) did not demonstrate any statistically significant improvement over placebo in any of the evaluated histopathological or clinical symptoms of photoaging."[^mukherjee] The two newest cosmetic derivatives have the thinnest evidence of all. HPR's antiaging support is in-vitro and combination-product data with no isolated human trial,[^wang] and retinyl retinoate's superiority-over-retinol finding comes only from small split-face trials by the compound's patent-holding group.[^kim] [[bakuchiol]] appears in this discussion only because it is marketed as a retinol substitute; because it is not a vitamin A derivative, its evidence is assessed on its own page, not as a retinoid.

## Common Marketing Claims

Treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Contains a retinoid, so it works like retinol or tretinoin."** A form's position on the conversion pathway matters. An ester is inactive until the skin cleaves it to retinol and converts it onward, and even then it converts less efficiently; Zasada and Budzisz record the esters' "decreased effectiveness of anti-wrinkle properties (smaller increase in the epidermal thickness) as compared to retinol and retinoic acid."[^zasada] The word "retinoid" on a label is not evidence of retinol-level or prescription-level efficacy.
- **"Newer cosmetic derivatives match prescription strength."** The clinical evidence does not support equivalence claims for HPR or retinyl retinoate: their human data are in-vitro, combination-only, or single-group and small.[^wang][^kim] The trial-validated benchmark remains prescription tretinoin.[^huang]
- **"Retinol alternatives are retinoids."** [[bakuchiol]] and similar plant "alternatives" are not vitamin A derivatives and do not sit on the retinoid conversion pathway. They are marketed alongside retinoids, which is a positioning choice, not a shared chemistry.
