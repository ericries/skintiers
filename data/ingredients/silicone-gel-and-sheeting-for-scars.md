The most recommended non-invasive scar treatment, and the one whose evidence base is weakest relative to its reputation. **Bottom line:** across 20 randomised trials the direction is consistently favourable, the pooled effects are statistically significant, and the trials themselves are poor quality. Cochrane's own summary word is "weak".

> **The best synthesis available says both things at once.** Cochrane pooled 20 trials in 873 people and found, for treating established scars, "a statistically significant reduction in scar thickness (mean difference (MD) -2.00, 95% CI -2.14 to -1.85) and colour amelioration (RR 3.49, 95% CI 1.97 to 6.15)". The same sentence ends "but again these studies were highly susceptible to bias".[^cochrane]

## The Rubric

**Flattening and fading an established hypertrophic or keloid scar (health)**
Graded modest on mixed evidence. The pooled thickness and colour effects above are real numbers from randomised trials, not testimonial, which is more than most scar products have. What stops it going higher is the quality judgement attached to them by the people who did the pooling, and the fact that a 2026 systematic review of fluid silicone gels, restricted to randomised trials, still opens by calling the evidence of superiority "inconsistent".[^cochrane][^ejd]

**Preventing a hypertrophic scar forming on a newly healed wound (health)**
Graded modest on preliminary evidence. Against no treatment in people prone to scarring, silicone sheeting "reduced the incidence of hypertrophic scarring", at a risk ratio of 0.46 with a confidence interval of 0.21 to 0.98.[^cochrane] An interval whose upper bound is 0.98 is one that only just excludes no effect, and Cochrane flags these studies as highly susceptible to bias too.

**Improving a flat scar, an acne mark or a stretch mark (cosmetic)**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. Every trial pooled here studied hypertrophic or keloid scarring, which is an overgrowth of dermal tissue, not the flat discolouration people usually mean by a scar. Nothing in these reviews reaches that use. Post-inflammatory marks are a different problem, covered on [[post-inflammatory-erythema]] and [[hyperpigmentation]].

## The Evidence

**What the Cochrane review actually did.** Twenty randomised, quasi-randomised or controlled trials, 873 people, ages 1.5 to 81, comparing adhesive silicone gel sheeting against no treatment, non-silicone dressings, other silicone products, laser, triamcinolone injection, onion extract and pressure therapy.[^cochrane] Its conclusion is blunt: "There is weak evidence of a benefit of silicone gel sheeting as a prevention for abnormal scarring in high-risk individuals but the poor quality of research means a great deal of uncertainty prevails."[^cochrane]

**Thirteen years later the picture has not resolved.** A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in the European Journal of Dermatology, restricted to randomised trials of fluid silicone gels, included 35 papers and meta-analysed 20.[^ejd] Its abstract states the rationale that "although silicone gel fluid is widely recognised as a leading non-invasive treatment, there is inconsistent evidence of superiority", and then reports methods without any pooled result, so no effect size from it can be quoted here.[^ejd] The full text was not obtained; PubMed lists no PMC copy.

**What this does not tell you.** Which form to use. Sheets and fluid gels are pooled separately in these two reviews and compared directly in some included trials, but neither abstract reports a head-to-head estimate, so the common advice that a sheet beats a gel is not supported by anything quoted here.

**Why it is still the standard recommendation.** Not because the evidence is strong, but because it is non-invasive, cheap relative to the alternatives, and carries little downside; the comparators in these trials include triamcinolone injection and laser. That is a reasonable basis for trying it and a poor basis for expecting much.

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven unless it also appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Clinically proven to reduce scars."** Partly true and routinely overstated. There are randomised trials and pooled effects, and the body that pooled them calls the research poor quality and highly susceptible to bias.[^cochrane]
- **"Works on all scars."** Discount. The trials studied hypertrophic and keloid scars. No fetched trial covers flat scars, acne marks or stretch marks.[^cochrane][^ejd]
- **"Medical grade" or "the silicone doctors recommend."** Not an evidence claim. It is true that silicone is the usual first recommendation, and that is a statement about its low downside and the invasiveness of the alternatives rather than about effect size.
- **"Gel and sheet work the same" or "the sheet is stronger."** Unsupported either way here. Neither review's abstract reports a head-to-head estimate between forms.[^cochrane][^ejd]
