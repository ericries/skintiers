Coco-Betaine (INCI name) is an amphoteric co-surfactant made from coconut-derived fatty alkyl chains, used as the mild secondary detergent in gentle cleansers and body washes, including several [[la-roche-posay-toleriane-hydrating-gentle-cleanser|La Roche-Posay cleansers]] profiled here. It is a close commercial substitute for [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]] and is frequently confused with it, but the two are different molecules and their evidence does not transfer in either direction.[^iijima]

> Coco-betaine is cleansing-base chemistry, not an active: it helps a cleanser foam and buffers harsher detergents, and it does nothing visible to skin on its own. Judge it on mildness and tolerability, not on benefit. The important practical point is that it is a documented contact allergen in its own right, and patch testing it is not the same as patch testing [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]]: in two reported cases, coco betaine was clearly positive while cocamidopropyl betaine was only doubtfully so.[^iijima] Its own evidence base is two case reports, not the registry-scale data behind cocamidopropyl betaine, which means less is known, not that less risk exists.

## The Rubric

**Mild cleansing co-surfactant (functional, not a skin-health active)**
- **Effect size: none on skin itself; this is a formulation function.** Amphoteric betaines carry both a positive and a negative charge, which lets them co-micellize with harsher anionic detergents and buffer their interaction with skin, the accepted rationale for why gentle cleansers pair them with sulfates. That mechanism and its supporting irritancy data are set out on [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]]; they describe that molecule, and no comparative irritancy trial of coco-betaine itself is cited here. It has no anti-aging, barrier-repair, or disease-modifying claim of its own.
- **Evidence quality: thin for this specific molecule.** Coco-betaine is a formulation ingredient, so there is no therapeutic trial to weigh, and unlike [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]] it has not been through large comparative surfactant-irritancy panels in the literature cited here. Its grade reflects a functional role plus a real tolerability caveat, not a demonstrated skin benefit.

**Contact allergy risk (topical exposure via cleansers, shampoos, medicated washes)**
- **Effect size: established as a real sensitizer, magnitude unknown.** Allergic contact dermatitis to coco betaine has been reported in a beard cleanser and, in a separate report, in two patients using a clobetasol propionate shampoo, where "only coco betaine 1% aq. showed a clearly positive reaction in both cases."[^badaoui][^iijima] Those are case reports, so they establish that it can sensitize without supporting any prevalence figure.
- **Evidence quality: case reports only, which is a gap rather than a reassurance.** No large patch-test registry series for coco-betaine is cited here, in contrast to the tens of thousands of patch-tested patients behind the [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]] allergen data. The honest reading is that its sensitization rate is not established, not that it is low.

## How It Works

Coco-betaine is an alkyl betaine: a coconut-derived fatty alkyl chain attached directly to a quaternary nitrogen bearing a carboxylate group, giving it both charges at once (amphoteric, or zwitterionic). That dual charge is the functional point. It lets the molecule sit in mixed micelles with anionic surfactants, which dilutes the harsher detergent's contact with skin proteins and lipids while keeping foam, which is why it appears as the second or third surfactant in cleansers rather than the first.

The structural difference from [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]] is the part that matters clinically. Cocamidopropyl betaine is built by first reacting coconut fatty acids with dimethylaminopropylamine (DMAPA) to form an amidoamine intermediate, and its allergenicity is attributed primarily to residual amidoamine and DMAPA left over from that manufacture rather than to the finished betaine, as set out on [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]]. Coco-betaine has no amidopropyl linker and is not made by that route, so that specific impurity explanation does not apply to it. What follows from that is narrow: the *mechanism* behind cocamidopropyl betaine's allergen reputation does not carry over. It does not follow that coco-betaine is hypoallergenic, and the reported cases show it is not.[^badaoui][^iijima]

## The Evidence

**Allergic contact dermatitis from a medicated shampoo (two cases, patch-tested).** Iijima and colleagues reported "two cases of allergic contact dermatitis caused by the amphoteric surfactant coco betaine, a constituent of clobetasol propionate (CP) shampoo, despite the low allergenic potential of CP shampoo formulations." The patients were "Japanese males, aged 49 and 52 years, with severe atopic dermatitis, who had been treated with oral cyclosporine for several years." The discriminating result is in the patch tests: "only coco betaine 1% aq. showed a clearly positive reaction in both cases, whereas the patch test results of CP shampoo, cocamidopropyl betaine, and lauramidopropyl betaine were positive in one case and doubtful in the other."[^iijima] The authors' practical conclusion was that "in cases of recalcitrant scalp dermatitis, it is necessary to consider the possibility that it is being caused by the topical medication used to treat it, and that the 'active' ingredient may not be the culprit."[^iijima]

Two things to take from that and no more. First, coco betaine sensitized these patients where the related betaines did not reliably reproduce the reaction, which is direct evidence that testing or avoiding [[cocamidopropyl-betaine]] does not settle coco-betaine. Second, both patients had severe atopic dermatitis and long-term systemic immunosuppression, so they are not a general population, and two cases cannot estimate how often this happens.

**A second independent case.** A 2024 report in Contact Dermatitis is titled "Allergic contact dermatitis to coco betaine in a beard cleanser."[^badaoui] It is a short correspondence with no abstract available, so only its existence and subject are recorded here, not its case details.

Beyond these reports, the published literature indexed under this ingredient name is sparse and mostly unrelated to skin (surfactant physical chemistry and nanoemulsion formulation), so no prevalence, dose-response, or comparative-tolerability conclusion is drawn here.

## What's Overhyped

"Coconut-derived" and "gentle" describe where the raw material comes from and the formulation role, not a tested skin benefit, and neither term predicts whether a given person will tolerate it. "Sulfate-free" cleansers often lean on betaines like this one, so a sulfate-free label does not mean a cleanser is free of potential sensitizers. For anyone with recurring cleanser-related dermatitis, the useful step is patch testing with a dermatologist rather than ingredient-swapping by label, and the relevant condition page is [[contact-dermatitis]].
