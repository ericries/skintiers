Coco-Glucoside (INCI name) is a mild non-ionic alkyl polyglucoside surfactant made by reacting coconut-derived fatty alcohols with glucose, used as a gentle primary or co-cleansing and foaming agent in rinse-off face washes, body washes, shampoos, and baby products; PubChem's record for the INCI name uses n-dodecyl-beta-D-glucopyranoside (C18H36O6) as a representative single congener of what is, in practice, a mixture of C8-C16 alkyl chains from coconut oil.[^pubchem] Its selling point is mildness relative to harsher anionic detergents like sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), a real and instrumentally documented effect for the alkyl-polyglucoside class, but it is a cleansing-base ingredient with no active skin benefit, and it is also a genuine, increasingly documented contact allergen, with coco-glucoside specifically flagged as the most common alkyl glucoside found in cosmetics marketed as "hypoallergenic."[^loffler][^warshaw][^cirin]

> In patch testing at 0.125% to 2.0% with exposure times of 6, 12, and 24 hours, an alkyl polyglucoside (APG) produced a skin reaction that was "hard to detect" even at the highest concentration and showed "no significant reaction even at day 3," compared with a pronounced and still-elevated transepidermal water loss from sodium lauryl sulfate at day 10.[^loffler]

## The Rubric

Coco-glucoside is a functional cleansing agent, so it is graded on what it is actually asked to do, not on a skin-treatment claim.

**Gentle cleansing / low irritation (its role)**
- **Effect size: real and instrumentally documented, but for the surfactant class rather than coco-glucoside specifically.** A patch-test kinetics study comparing sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium laureth sulfate, and an alkyl polyglucoside (APG) found "a pronounced reaction to SLS" and "a far milder one to SLES," while "even at the highest concentration the skin reaction to APG was hard to detect," with transepidermal water loss and laser Doppler flowmetry showing no significant reaction on APG-tested skin even 3 days after patch removal, versus a still-elevated TEWL from SLS at day 10.[^loffler] This describes how kind the surfactant class is to skin during use, not a measurable improvement to the skin afterward, and the tested APG was not confirmed to be coco-glucoside's specific chain-length blend.
- **Evidence quality: solid for the class, indirect for the specific ingredient.** The Löffler and Happle trial is a controlled, quantitative (TEWL, laser Doppler) comparison, but it tested "alkyl polyglucoside" generically rather than coco-glucoside by INCI name, so the mildness data transfers by chemical-class inference rather than direct ingredient-level proof.[^loffler]

**Any active skin benefit (hydration, barrier repair, anti-aging, acne)**
- **Effect size: none demonstrated.** Coco-glucoside is a rinse-off surfactant with no cited evidence that it hydrates, repairs the barrier, or treats any skin condition; it is not formulated to stay on skin and deliver an active effect.
- **Evidence quality: none.** No controlled trial identified here tests coco-glucoside for a skin-improvement endpoint.

**Tolerability / contact allergy (the caveat)**
- **Effect size: a real, non-trivial allergen, and disproportionately present in products marketed as gentle.** In a retrospective review of 24,097 patients patch-tested by the North American Contact Dermatitis Group to decyl and/or lauryl glucoside (2009-2018), 2.0% had positive reactions, most (83.9%) currently relevant, most often traced to personal-care products, especially hair products and skin cleansers; glucoside-positive patients had significantly higher rates of atopic dermatitis (39.0% vs 28.6%, P<0.0001), hay fever, and asthma than glucoside-negative patients, and cross-reactivity between decyl and lauryl glucoside exceeded 40%.[^warshaw] A 2025 pediatric patch-test review found an even higher positivity rate to decyl glucoside (11.2% definite positive of 241 children tested, 19.5% including doubtful/irritant reactions), though the same authors caution that many of these positives may be false-positive or irritant reactions, particularly in young atopic children.[^kanokrungsee] In a survey of best-selling Amazon shampoos and body cleansers marketed as "hypoallergenic" or "for sensitive skin," coco-glucoside was "the most commonly present" alkyl glucoside, ahead of decyl and lauryl glucoside, and the authors concluded there is "a necessity to include coco-glucoside in the baseline series of patch testing allergens."[^cirin]
- **Evidence quality: solid.** A large multi-center registry study, an independent pediatric case series, and a market-survey paper converge on alkyl glucosides, and coco-glucoside specifically, as a real and likely under-recognized contact allergen, one that appears with above-average frequency in products explicitly marketed to sensitive or reactive skin.[^warshaw][^kanokrungsee][^cirin]

## How It Works

Coco-glucoside is produced by a Fischer glycosidation, reacting glucose (from corn or wheat starch) with fatty alcohols derived from coconut oil to form an alkyl glycoside bond; the resulting mixture spans the C8-C16 chain lengths naturally present in coconut-derived fatty alcohols, though PubChem's entry for the INCI name resolves to n-dodecyl-beta-D-glucopyranoside (C12 chain, C18H36O6) as a representative structure.[^pubchem] As a non-ionic surfactant, it lacks the charged head group of anionic detergents such as sodium lauryl sulfate; that charge is what drives anionic surfactants to bind and denature stratum corneum proteins and strip lipids more aggressively, so the non-ionic class as a whole tends to be milder in irritation testing, which is the basis for its use in sensitive-skin and baby cleansers.[^loffler] The allergen mechanism runs on a separate track from the mildness mechanism: alkyl glucosides act as classic contact allergens via a delayed-type hypersensitivity (type IV) reaction in susceptible individuals, unrelated to their generally low irritant potential, which is why a surfactant can be simultaneously "mild" on irritation testing in the general population and a documented allergen in a sensitized subset.[^warshaw][^kanokrungsee]

## The Evidence

**Alkyl polyglucoside versus SLS and SLES, patch-test kinetics (topical, class-level).** Löffler and Happle patch-tested SLS and either SLES or an alkyl polyglucoside (APG) at 0.125%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0% for 6, 12, or 24 hours, then measured transepidermal water loss and laser Doppler flowmetry at 24 hours and 7 and 10 days after patch removal. They found "a pronounced reaction to SLS, and a far milder one to SLES," while "even at the highest concentration the skin reaction to APG was hard to detect," and during the day 3 to 10 regeneration period "the APG-tested skin areas showed no significant reaction even at day 3," compared with SLS, which "showed even at day 10 an increased TEWL at all concentrations tested."[^loffler] *A controlled, instrumented comparison found the alkyl-polyglucoside surfactant class dramatically milder than SLS and gentler than even the already-milder SLES, though the study did not isolate coco-glucoside as a specific INCI ingredient.*

**Decyl and lauryl glucoside patch testing, North American Contact Dermatitis Group (retrospective registry).** Warshaw and colleagues retrospectively analyzed 24,097 patients patch-tested to decyl glucoside (2009-2018) and/or lauryl glucoside (2017-2018); 470 (2.0%) had a positive reaction, 83.9% of which were judged currently clinically relevant. Glucoside-positive patients had significantly higher rates of atopic dermatitis (39.0% vs 28.6%, P<0.0001), hay fever (38.5% vs 31.6%, P=0.0014), and asthma (21.8% vs 16.5%, P=0.0023) than glucoside-negative patients; the most common source of the allergen was personal care products (63.0%), especially hair products (16.5%) and skin cleansers (15.2%), and cross-reactivity between the two glucosides was over 40%.[^warshaw] *A large, multi-center clinical registry found that roughly 1 in 50 tested patients reacted to alkyl glucosides, that the reactions are usually clinically meaningful rather than incidental, and that atopic and allergy-prone people are disproportionately affected.*

**Alkyl glucoside prevalence in "hypoallergenic" and "sensitive skin" cosmetics (market survey).** Ćirin and colleagues surveyed the top 30 best-selling shampoos and body cleansers on Amazon.com marketed as "hypoallergenic" or "for sensitive skin" and checked ingredient lists for alkyl glucosides. As much as 56.7% of hypoallergenic shampoos contained an alkyl glucoside, and "the most commonly present AG in our analysis was coco-glucoside," ahead of decyl and lauryl glucoside, the two ingredients most often studied in patch-test panels; the authors concluded there is "a necessity to include coco-glucoside in the baseline series of patch testing allergens."[^cirin] *Coco-glucoside is not a marginal ingredient in the sensitive-skin category; it is the single most common alkyl glucoside in products explicitly sold on a mildness promise, which sharpens the practical stakes of its allergen profile.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Naturally derived, gentle, coconut-based cleanser."** The mildness half is genuinely supported: alkyl polyglucosides as a class produced far less irritation than SLS and less than SLES in controlled patch testing.[^loffler] But "gentle" describes irritation potential in the general population, not allergy risk in the sensitized minority, and coco-glucoside is documented as the alkyl glucoside most frequently found in products carrying exactly this marketing promise.[^cirin]
- **"Hypoallergenic / suitable for sensitive skin."** Not supported as an absolute claim. Alkyl glucosides, including coco-glucoside by name, are recommended candidates for standard contact-allergen patch-test panels, positivity rates run around 2% in a large adult registry and higher still in a pediatric cohort, and glucoside-positive patients skew toward atopic dermatitis, hay fever, and asthma, the very population "hypoallergenic" products are marketed to.[^warshaw][^kanokrungsee][^cirin]
