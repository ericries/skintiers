A rose-scented terpene alcohol used as a fragrance ingredient. It is there to make a product smell of roses, not to do anything to skin, which is why the grade here is `weak`. **Bottom line:** the thing worth knowing is that what sensitises people is usually the OXIDISED form, not the fresh molecule, and that distinction changes what avoiding it means.

> **Oxidised geraniol tests positive roughly twenty times as often as plain geraniol.** In a Swedish patch-test series, allergic reactions were recorded to "ox. geraniol 11% (n = 39, 5.8%), ox. geraniol 6% (n = 12, 1.8%), geraniol 6% (n = 2, 0.3%)".[^oxid] Same molecule, air-exposed versus not. The authors conclude that "Contact allergy to ox. geraniol, geranial and citral is common in routinely tested dermatitis patients".[^oxid]

## The Rubric

**Fragrance ingredient, not a skin-health active**

- **Effect size on skin: none claimed.** Geraniol is in a formula for its scent. No efficacy evidence was sought because no benefit is advertised.
- **Tolerability: a genuine allergen, and a common one in the right population.** A 23-centre Spanish study patch-tested "1013 patients" who had already reacted to a fragrance marker, then broke results down by demographic group. Geraniol was the most frequent allergen in two of those groups: women at 16.6% and retired people at 22.4%.[^spain]
- **Read that denominator carefully.** Those patients were selected for having already tested positive to fragrance markers in a baseline series. The percentages describe who reacts *within an already fragrance-allergic group*, not how often geraniol troubles the general population. Anyone citing the 16.6% figure as a share of all women is misreading it.
- **The oxidation point is the practical one.** Terpenes autoxidise on exposure to air, and the Swedish data above shows the oxidised form driving almost all the positives. An old, often-opened bottle is not chemically the same product as a fresh one.
- **It travels with its relatives.** The same study found overrepresentation of simultaneous contact allergy to combinations of oxidised geraniol, oxidised limonene and oxidised linalool.[^oxid] If one is a problem, the others are worth testing.

## The Evidence

Two papers, both read from their PubMed abstracts, both about allergy rather than benefit. Neither measures anything geraniol does for skin, because that is not what it is for.

A note on the second paper's framing: its primary question was whether oxidised geraniol allergy is overrepresented in people photoallergic to ketoprofen, and it reports that it is, on "those four patients who were diagnosed with photocontact allergy to ketoprofen during the test period".[^oxid] Four patients is a very small group and this page draws nothing from that comparison; the prevalence figures quoted above come from the much larger routinely-tested population in the same study.

## Common Marketing Claims

Nothing to quarantine. No brand markets geraniol itself, and no marketing copy was fetched for this page; it appears partway down an ingredient list as a scent component rather than as something a brand is selling. It is named individually on some labels rather than hidden inside the word fragrance, which is what makes it checkable at all, though no page here has verified which labelling rule requires that. The claims worth scrutinising around this ingredient are the ones on the FRONT of the bottle, and what fragrance-free and unscented are allowed to mean is at [[label-words-alcohol-free-fragrance-free-hypoallergenic]].

> **Bottom line.** Not worth avoiding on principle. Worth knowing the name if you have unexplained facial or hand dermatitis and use scented products, and worth knowing that [[patch-testing]] with the oxidised form is what finds it.
