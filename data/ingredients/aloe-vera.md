Aloe vera (Aloe Barbadensis Leaf Juice) is the gel-derived leaf extract used as a soothing humectant in after-sun gels, moisturizers, and sensitive-skin products, with genuine but modest evidence for hydrating skin and helping minor burns and wounds.[^dalbelo][^hekmatpou]

> Aloe is a real mild soother and humectant, not a hero active. A vehicle-controlled study found aloe-containing formulas increased the water content of the outer skin, though they did not improve the skin barrier (transepidermal water loss was unchanged).[^dalbelo] Its famous use on burns and wounds is supported by a body of trials, but they are small and mostly low-quality, so the effect is plausible and modest rather than proven.[^hekmatpou] Judge it as a pleasant, well-tolerated soothing ingredient, comparable to other humectants like [[glycerin]] but with weaker, mixed evidence, and note it is an occasional contact allergen.

## The Rubric

Aloe is graded on the two things it is actually used for: soothing/hydrating skin, and helping minor burns and wounds.

**Skin hydration and soothing (cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: modest.** In a vehicle-controlled study, cosmetic formulations with 0.25% and 0.50% aloe extract raised stratum corneum water content after a single application, and all tested concentrations (0.10 to 0.50%) did so after two weeks, versus the vehicle; transepidermal water loss was not changed, so this is a humectant, surface-hydration effect rather than a barrier-repair one.[^dalbelo] Its cooling, soothing feel on irritated or sunburned skin is real but is a comfort effect, not a demonstrated acceleration of healing.
- **Evidence quality: limited.** One small, vehicle-controlled instrumental study underpins the hydration claim; the soothing use rests largely on tradition and mild anti-inflammatory activity.[^dalbelo]

**Minor burns and wounds (health)**
- **Effect size: modest and uncertain.** A systematic review of 23 clinical trials found aloe has been used for skin ulcers, burns, postoperative wounds, cracked nipples, and psoriasis, and concluded its properties support wound use, but the underlying trials are small and heterogeneous in quality.[^hekmatpou] The honest read is a plausible, modest benefit for minor burns and wounds, not a reliably proven one.
- **Evidence quality: low to moderate.** A body of trials exists, but they are mostly small, varied, and at risk of bias, so the systematic-review conclusion is weaker than its trial count suggests.[^hekmatpou]

## The Evidence

Aloe vera's honest profile is a well-tolerated mild soother and humectant with modest, mixed evidence. The clearest controlled finding is cosmetic: aloe formulas increase the water content of the outer skin, but do not change transepidermal water loss, so it hydrates the surface without repairing the barrier.[^dalbelo] Its traditional and most-marketed use, calming and healing minor burns and wounds, is backed by a substantial number of trials but their small size and mixed quality keep the conclusion tentative.[^hekmatpou] For settings where aloe is often reached for, its role is supportive: psoriasis and seborrheic dermatitis have their own better-evidenced treatments on [[psoriasis]] and [[seborrheic-dermatitis]]. Aloe is generally very well tolerated, though allergic contact dermatitis to it is documented and not rare enough to ignore.
