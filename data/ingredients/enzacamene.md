Enzacamene (INCI 4-Methylbenzylidene Camphor, abbreviated 4-MBC, sold under the trade name Eusolex 6300) is a camphor-derivative organic UV filter classified as a "UV filter; UV absorber" in the EU's combined cosmetic-ingredient inventory.[^pubchem] It does not appear anywhere in the table of 16 active ingredients the FDA carried into the 1999 US sunscreen monograph, nor in the agency's 2019 proposed update of that monograph, so it is not a permitted active ingredient in US over-the-counter sunscreens.[^fda2019] A series of University of Zurich pharmacology studies found it estrogenic in cell and rat assays, active at doses in the range environmental and cosmetic exposure can reach, which is the main reason its safety, not its filtering performance, dominates the literature on it.[^schlumpf2001][^schlumpf2008]

> Enzacamene (4-MBC) is a camphor-based organic UV filter used in sunscreens outside the United States but absent from the FDA's US sunscreen monograph, so it is not sold in US OTC sunscreens.[^fda2019] University of Zurich researchers found it increased proliferation of estrogen-sensitive breast-cancer cells and dose-dependently increased uterine weight in immature rats, both orally and applied to the skin, and a later developmental study in rats found it delayed male puberty and altered thyroid-axis and reproductive-organ endpoints at doses as low as 7 mg/kg/day.[^schlumpf2001][^schlumpf2008] A 2021 dermatology review notes these findings alongside similar signals for other filters but states that "none of these human studies have yet to show any real world human biologic consequences."[^sabzevari2]

## The Rubric

Enzacamene's role as a working UV filter and its safety profile sit on different footings: the filtering claim rests on its EU cosmetic classification with no independently verified spectral or SPF-contribution data in the sources fetched for this page, while the safety question has real, repeated animal data behind it.

**UV-filtering use and US availability**
- **Effect size: not independently verified here, and irrelevant to a US reader regardless.** The EU's combined cosmetic-ingredient inventory lists enzacamene under "Uv filter; Uv absorber," confirming its functional category, but no primary spectral-characterization or SPF-contribution study for enzacamene was located and verified for this page, so no claim about how strong a filter it is can be made.[^pubchem] What is verified is that it is absent from the FDA's 1999 sunscreen monograph (16 active ingredients) and from the 2019 proposed rule that would update it, meaning it cannot legally be sold as a sunscreen active in the US regardless of how it performs elsewhere.[^fda2019] "Functions as a UV filter in markets where it is sold, but not sold in the US at all, and its filtering performance is not independently documented in the sources checked here."
- **Evidence quality: thin.** The only verified claim is a classification entry, not a controlled performance study.[^pubchem]

**Estrogenic and developmental-toxicity signal (a separate axis, not a filtering-performance grade)**
- In vitro, enzacamene was one of five UV screens (of six tested) that increased proliferation of estrogen-sensitive MCF-7 breast-cancer cells, and its proliferative effect was blocked by the estrogen antagonist ICI 182,780, direct mechanistic evidence of estrogen-receptor activity.[^schlumpf2001] In an immature-rat uterotrophic assay, oral enzacamene dose-dependently increased uterine weight (ED50 309 mg/kg/day), and dermal application to immature hairless rats at 5% and 7.5% concentrations in olive oil also increased uterine weight.[^schlumpf2001] A follow-up multigenerational study administered enzacamene to rats in chow from before mating through lactation and to the offspring into adulthood (0.7 to 47 mg/kg/day) and found enhanced neonatal prostate growth, delayed male puberty, altered reproductive-organ weights, and effects on the thyroid axis, with a lowest-observed-adverse-effect level of 7 mg/kg/day and no-observed-adverse-effect level of 0.7 mg/kg/day for classical endpoints; molecular (gene- and protein-expression) endpoints were affected at the lowest doses tested.[^schlumpf2008] A 2021 dermatology review summarizing this and related human-exposure literature notes associations between 4-MBC (along with oxybenzone and octinoxate) and "minor changes in testosterone, estradiol, and inhibin B in male patients, decreased sperm counts, and delayed puberty" but is explicit that "none of these human studies have yet to show any real world human biologic consequences."[^sabzevari2] The animal-model estrogenicity and developmental effects are well replicated across this group's own studies; whether they translate into harm at human cosmetic-use exposure is not established.

## What We Actually Know

Enzacamene is an organic UV filter marketed outside the United States and classified as such in the EU's cosmetic-ingredient inventory, but it was never part of the FDA's US sunscreen monograph and does not appear in the agency's 2019 review of sunscreen actives, so it is not a legal ingredient in US OTC sunscreens.[^pubchem][^fda2019] Independent of its filtering job, a University of Zurich research group repeatedly found it estrogenic: it increased proliferation of estrogen-sensitive breast-cancer cells through an estrogen-receptor-blockable mechanism, increased uterine weight in immature rats both orally and dermally, and in a multigenerational rat study delayed male puberty, altered reproductive-organ and thyroid-axis measures, and affected neonatal prostate growth, with adverse effects on classical endpoints starting at 7 mg/kg/day.[^schlumpf2001][^schlumpf2008] A later review folding in human biomonitoring data for enzacamene and related filters describes only minor hormone-level associations in men and states no human study has yet shown a real-world biological consequence.[^sabzevari2] No fetched source characterizes enzacamene's own UV-filtering performance (SPF contribution, UVA/UVB spectral range) independently of its EU functional classification.

## How It Works

Enzacamene is a bicyclic camphor ketone bearing a 4-methylbenzylidene substituent (molecular formula C18H22O), consistent with the general class of organic ("chemical") UV filters that absorb ultraviolet photons and dissipate the energy rather than scattering light the way mineral filters do.[^pubchem] No primary spectroscopic characterization of enzacamene's specific absorption range was located and verified for this page, so its UVA/UVB balance is not stated here.

## The Evidence

**In vitro and rat estrogenicity (2001).** In a study of six commonly used UV screens for estrogenic activity, University of Zurich researchers found that five of the six, including 4-MBC (enzacamene), "increased cell proliferation" of MCF-7 breast-cancer cells, with median effective concentrations (EC50) across the active compounds ranging 1.56 to 3.73 microM; the proliferative effect of 4-MBC specifically was blocked by the estrogen antagonist ICI 182,780, evidence it works through the estrogen receptor.[^schlumpf2001] In the uterotrophic assay in immature Long-Evans rats fed the chemicals for four days, uterine weight was "dose-dependently increased by 4-MBC (ED50 309 mg/kg/day)."[^schlumpf2001] Dermal application of 4-MBC to immature hairless rats "also increased uterine weight at concentrations of 5 and 7.5% in olive oil," showing the effect is not limited to the oral route.[^schlumpf2001] The authors concluded that "UV screens should be tested for endocrine activity, in view of possible long-term effects in humans and wildlife."[^schlumpf2001]

**Multigenerational developmental toxicity (2008 review of the group's own rat studies).** A follow-up review by the same University of Zurich group describes rat developmental-toxicity studies in which 4-MBC (0.7, 7, 24, 47 mg/kg/day) was administered in chow to the parent generation before mating, through pregnancy and lactation, and to the offspring into adulthood. "Neonates exhibited enhanced prostate growth after 4-MBC," and 4-MBC "delayed male puberty and affected reproductive organ weights of adult offspring," with "effects on the thyroid axis" also noted; oestrogen-regulated gene expression was altered in adult uterus, prostate, and gonad-control brain regions, and female sexual behavior was impaired.[^schlumpf2008] The study reports a "lowest observed adverse effect level" (LOAEL) of 7 mg/kg and a "no observed adverse effect level" (NOAEL) of 0.7 mg/kg for classical endpoints, with molecular endpoints "affected by the lowest doses studied."[^schlumpf2008]

**Human-relevance framing.** A 2021 two-part dermatology review of sunscreen UV filters, citing the Zurich group's work alongside a human biomonitoring study, states that "BP-3, 4-MBC, and OMC have also been associated with minor changes in testosterone, estradiol, and inhibin B in male patients, decreased sperm counts, and delayed puberty," but adds that "none of these human studies have yet to show any real world human biologic consequences."[^sabzevari2] The same review notes more broadly that "several jurisdictions have prohibited specific UV filters, but this does not adequately address the dichotomy of the benefits of photoprotection vs lack of eco-friendly, safe, and FDA-approved alternatives."[^sabzevari2]

**US regulatory status.** The FDA's stayed 1999 final sunscreen monograph (Table 1 of the agency's 2019 proposed rule) lists exactly 16 active ingredients ever carried into the US OTC sunscreen framework: aminobenzoic acid (PABA), avobenzone, cinoxate, dioxybenzone, ensulizole, homosalate, meradimate, octinoxate, octisalate, octocrylene, oxybenzone, padimate O, sulisobenzone, titanium dioxide, trolamine salicylate, and zinc oxide.[^fda2019] Enzacamene is not among them, and it does not appear in the 2019 proposed rule's Category I/II/III classification either; the only mention of "4-Methylbenzylidene Camphor" in that document is in a bibliography citation to an unrelated academic study, not in the list of ingredients under FDA review.[^fda2019] This is consistent with enzacamene never having been the subject of a US new-drug-application filing or monograph petition, rather than a specific FDA safety finding against it.

## Uses

Not established for US readers: enzacamene is not a legal active ingredient in US over-the-counter sunscreens, so no US-market product can contain it as a filter.[^fda2019] Where it is sold (outside the US, per the EU cosmetic-ingredient classification), no fetched source in this review independently verifies its filtering performance.[^pubchem] Its best-documented property in the sources checked here is estrogenic and developmental-toxicity activity in rat and cell-based assays at doses that overlap with plausible cosmetic and environmental exposure, an animal-model finding whose human clinical significance remains unresolved.[^schlumpf2001][^schlumpf2008][^sabzevari2]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"A well-established, widely used sunscreen filter."** It is classified as a UV filter in the EU cosmetic-ingredient inventory, but no fetched source in this review verifies a specific performance claim (SPF contribution, spectral coverage) for enzacamene, and it has never been part of the US sunscreen monograph.[^pubchem][^fda2019]
- **"No safety concerns; other jurisdictions allow it."** A 2021 review explicitly frames non-US jurisdictions' continued use of some UV filters against a documented endocrine-activity literature, and states that jurisdictional bans of certain filters exist without fully resolving the tradeoff against sun protection; it does not describe enzacamene as free of concern.[^sabzevari2] Independent University of Zurich rat and cell studies found dose-dependent estrogenic and developmental effects for enzacamene, with adverse classical endpoints starting at 7 mg/kg/day in the multigenerational study.[^schlumpf2001][^schlumpf2008]
- **"No proven human harm, so the concern is overstated."** This is technically accurate as far as verified: the human clinical significance of the animal findings has not been demonstrated, and the 2021 review states plainly that none of the related human studies "have yet to show any real world human biologic consequences."[^sabzevari2] That absence of demonstrated human harm is different from a demonstration of human safety; no fetched source makes the latter claim for enzacamene.
