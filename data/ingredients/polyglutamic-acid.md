Polyglutamic acid (usually the gamma isoform, written γ-PGA) is an anionic polymer made of repeating glutamic-acid units, produced by fermenting bacteria such as *Bacillus subtilis* (the organism used to make the Japanese fermented soybean food natto), and it is used in skincare as a humectant and film-forming agent, typically alongside [[hyaluronic-acid|hyaluronic acid]] in "moisture acid" serums and boosters.[^ko][^parati]

> No published human clinical trial testing a topical polyglutamic-acid product on skin turned up in a PubMed search covering hydration, wrinkle, and water-holding-capacity terms. The available primary evidence is a mix of cell-culture and reconstructed-skin-model experiments and non-skin water-binding comparisons; the strongest of these, a 2025 study of a novel high-molecular-weight γ-PGA strain, found it raised skin-barrier and moisture-related gene expression in keratinocytes and a lab-grown skin model, but its own authors flagged the "absence of in vivo data" and called for "well-designed clinical trials involving human subjects."[^ko]

## The Rubric

**Skin hydration / barrier support (topical, cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: minimal, unconfirmed in skin.** In keratinocytes and a reconstructed human-skin model, a high-molecular-weight γ-PGA dose-dependently raised mRNA and protein markers tied to barrier function and hydration, including filaggrin, involucrin, loricrin, hyaluronic-acid synthase (which increased the cells' own hyaluronic-acid output), and aquaporin-3.[^ko] Separately, a cream-formulation study found commercial γ-PGA gave a hydration effect "comparable" to, not exceeding, [[hyaluronic-acid|hyaluronic acid]] and glycerin in that testing.[^parati] Neither result is a measurement on living human skin.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** The gene-expression data come from isolated skin cells and a lab-grown skin-equivalent model, not a clinical trial, and the study's own authors state that in vivo human data are absent and needed.[^ko] The comparative hydration-capacity result comes from a cosmetic-formulation study, not a controlled skin trial.[^parati]

## What We Actually Know

Polyglutamic acid's case for skincare rests on chemistry and cell-culture data rather than a human trial. As a polymer built from repeating glutamic-acid units and rich in carboxylic-acid groups, γ-PGA is water-soluble and hygroscopic (moisture-absorbing) by structure, which is the basis for its humectant use.[^ko][^parati] In the most skin-relevant primary study located, a newly isolated high-molecular-weight γ-PGA strain (molecular weight reported up to roughly 7,000 kDa) increased dose-dependent expression of barrier and moisture-related genes and proteins, filaggrin, involucrin, loricrin, hyaluronic-acid synthase enzymes, and aquaporin-3, in cultured keratinocytes, and produced similar increases (plus thicker horny-layer structure) in a reconstructed human-skin model treated with 1% γ-PGA.[^ko] That is evidence of a plausible mechanism, not a demonstrated effect on real skin: the paper's authors explicitly note the "absence of in vivo data" and recommend "well-designed clinical trials involving human subjects" before drawing conclusions about actual skin benefit.[^ko] A separate formulation study comparing commercially available γ-PGA to established humectants in cream systems found it produced a hydration effect "comparable" to hyaluronic acid and glycerin, not a superior one, which cuts against marketing framing that positions polyglutamic acid as out-holding hyaluronic acid.[^parati] A broader search of PubMed for polyglutamic-acid trials in humans otherwise returns unrelated work, wound-microneedle patches tested in lab models, food-science water-holding-capacity studies in yogurt, dough, and surimi, and case reports of allergic reactions to orally ingested natto (which contains γ-PGA), none of which speak to a topical cosmetic serum's effect on human skin.[^zhang][^allergy]

## How It Works

γ-PGA is a polyamide in which the glutamic-acid units link through their gamma-carboxyl and alpha-amino groups rather than the standard peptide bond, producing a long, negatively charged, water-soluble chain.[^ko] That density of carboxylic-acid groups is what makes it hygroscopic, able to draw and hold water molecules, the same structural logic used to justify hyaluronic acid's humectant role, though the two are chemically distinct (a polypeptide versus a polysaccharide).[^ko][^parati] In cultured skin cells, γ-PGA also raised expression of enzymes that synthesize the skin's own hyaluronic acid and of aquaporin-3, a water-channel protein, suggesting a possible additional route to hydration beyond simple surface water-binding, but this has only been shown in cell culture and a lab-grown skin model, not confirmed in living human skin.[^ko]

## The Evidence

Primary evidence:

**Keratinocyte and reconstructed-skin-model gene expression.** A 2025 study isolated a novel *Bacillus subtilis* strain producing high-molecular-weight γ-PGA and tested it in human keratinocytes and a reconstructed skin model. γ-PGA treatment "dose-dependently increased the mRNA expression of skin barrier markers, including filaggrin, involucrin, loricrin, serine palmitoyl transferase, fatty acid synthase, and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase," and "enhanced hyaluronic acid synthesis by upregulating hyaluronic acid synthase-1, -2, and -3 mRNA levels and promoted aquaporin 3 expression." In the reconstructed skin model, topical application of 1% γ-PGA "elevated filaggrin, involucrin, CD44, and aquaporin 3 expression, compared to the control." The authors state that "further research is needed to explore the long-term effects of γ-PGA on skin health," and the study used cultured cells and a lab-grown skin model, with no human volunteers.[^ko]

**Hydration capacity versus established humectants (formulation study, not a skin trial).** A 2024 study producing γ-PGA from brown-algae substrate reported "comparable hydration effect of commercially available γ-PGA to conventional moisturising agents (HA and glycerol)" when tested in model cream systems, with greater hydration capacity from their algae-derived variant specifically; those algae-derived γ-PGA cream systems "did not display any negative effect on model HaCaT keratinocytes" in cytotoxicity testing.[^parati]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Holds more water than hyaluronic acid."** The one cosmetic-formulation study located that directly compared the two found γ-PGA's hydration effect "comparable" to hyaluronic acid and glycerin, not superior.[^parati] No cited source shows polyglutamic acid out-performing hyaluronic acid on human skin.
- **"Clinically proven to improve skin hydration and reduce wrinkles."** No human clinical trial of a topical polyglutamic-acid product turned up in a PubMed search. The closest primary evidence is gene-expression data from cultured skin cells and a reconstructed-skin model, which the study's own authors say needs confirmation in human trials.[^ko]
