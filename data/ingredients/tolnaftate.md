Tolnaftate is an over-the-counter topical antifungal, sold at 1% in Tinactin and many store brands. On the Bayer label its purpose is "Antifungal" and its stated uses are that it is "proven clinically effective in the treatment of most athlete's foot (tinea pedis) and ringworm (tinea corporis)".[^dailymed] Like the azoles it is an OTC Monograph Drug under citation M005 rather than an approved NDA, so the regulatory basis is the monograph for the active.[^dailymed]

> **It has the lowest risk ratio in the Cochrane table, and that is the most misleading number on this page.** A Cochrane review of 67 trials of topical treatments for fungal foot infections reported placebo-controlled pooled risk ratios of treatment failure including "tolnaftate RR 0.19 (95% CI 0.08 to 0.44)", which is numerically better than "azoles RR 0.30" or "allylamines RR 0.33".[^cochrane] **Those are separate placebo-controlled pools, not a head-to-head.** Comparing them directly is the indirect-comparison trap, and tolnaftate's interval is the widest of the group, which points to few or small trials. The one genuine head-to-head in that review pooled 11 trials and favoured allylamines over azoles; tolnaftate was not in it.[^cochrane] For a foot infection the evidence still points to [[terbinafine]].

## The Rubric

**Treating athlete's foot and body ringworm**
- **Effect size: notable.** The label uses the regulated phrase "proven clinically effective in the treatment of most athlete's foot (tinea pedis) and ringworm (tinea corporis)", and the hedge in "most" is the honest part.[^dailymed] The Cochrane placebo-controlled pooled estimate is a large reduction in treatment failure, with the caveat about its width above.[^cochrane] This is a resolvable infection, so the endpoint is clearance rather than improvement.
- **Evidence quality: solid at the class level, monograph-grade for the product.** The marketing category is OTC Monograph Drug M005, so no trials of this cream were required.[^dailymed] The randomised evidence behind the active is the Cochrane pool, which is foot-specific.[^cochrane]

**Preventing athlete's foot**
- **Effect size: not established by any source cited here.** This is what separates tolnaftate from the azoles. Its label carries a prevention claim, "helps prevent most athlete's foot with daily use", and a separate prevention dose, "to prevent athlete's foot, apply once or twice daily (morning and/or night)".[^dailymed] The Cochrane review covers treatment, not prophylaxis, so nothing cited here quantifies how well that works.
- **Evidence quality: monograph-permitted, unquantified.** A claim the FDA monograph allows is a real regulatory permission and not marketing invention, but it is not the same as a measured prevention rate, and no rate is asserted on this page.

**Anything on the scalp or nails**
- **Effect size: none, by the manufacturer's own statement.** The label says plainly that "this product is not effective on the scalp or nails".[^dailymed]
- **Evidence quality: not applicable; an explicit exclusion rather than an untested use.**

## The Evidence

**What the label directs.** Wash and dry the area, "apply a thin layer over affected area twice daily (morning and night)", and "use daily for 4 weeks; if condition persists longer, ask a doctor".[^dailymed] Four weeks is both the course and the decision point, and the Stop-use rule names "no improvement within 4 weeks" alongside irritation.[^dailymed] Prevention uses the lighter schedule quoted above.

**The safety section is short.** It is "For external use only", you should "avoid contact with the eyes", and it should not be used "on children under 2 years of age except under the advice and supervision of a doctor".[^dailymed]

**A buying trap worth knowing.** The same DailyMed index that carries this label also lists tolnaftate products marketed for nails, including a "NAIL FUNGUS CARE LIQUID".[^dailymed] The canonical Bayer label for the same active states it "is not effective on the scalp or nails".[^dailymed] An active does not change properties because a relabeller prints a different use on the carton, so read the Drug Facts rather than the brand promise. The reverse trap applies to the azoles, where one brand family spans several different actives, which is set out on [[clotrimazole]].

**Where it sits here.** [[terbinafine]] is the allylamine the head-to-head data favour for feet, [[clotrimazole]] is the comparable OTC azole, and [[ketoconazole]] is the azole with the deeper dermatological literature for Malassezia conditions. The conditions are on [[athletes-foot]] and [[seborrheic-dermatitis]]. Malassezia folliculitis is a different organism and nothing on this label covers it; see [[fungal-acne]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. A rash that is not actually tinea can look very like it, and treating the wrong thing with an antifungal wastes four weeks.

## Common Marketing Claims

These are the label's own Drug Facts claims, recorded with what supports each.

- **"proven clinically effective in the treatment of most athlete's foot (tinea pedis) and ringworm (tinea corporis)"**. Regulated OTC Drug Facts wording permitted under monograph M005, and supported at the class level by the Cochrane placebo-controlled pool.[^dailymed][^cochrane] The qualifier "most" is the label's own.
- **"helps prevent most athlete's foot with daily use"**. The distinguishing claim, permitted by the monograph. No source cited here measures a prevention rate, so treat it as a permitted indication rather than a quantified benefit.[^dailymed]
- **"for effective relief of itching, burning and cracking"**. A symptom claim, not a cure claim, and not separately quantified in anything cited here.[^dailymed]
- **Nail and scalp claims seen on other tolnaftate products**. Contradicted by this label's own exclusion.[^dailymed]
