Disodium laureth sulfosuccinate is a mild anionic surfactant used as a cleansing agent, usually as a co-surfactant in sulfate-free face and body washes and often alongside an isethionate or a betaine. Like every surfactant, its job is to remove oil and soil, so the only benefit it can offer skin is **not damaging it while doing so**. Gentle cleansers are ranked on [[best-gentle-cleansers-sensitive-skin]], and the barrier this has to avoid disturbing is on [[skin-barrier-repair]].

> **Judge this ingredient by what it avoids, not what it adds.** It does have real comparative human data, which is more than many "gentle" surfactants can claim: a study in 80 people with active skin disease grouped it with surfactants whose "chemical structure makes them quite safe in real-life conditions", explicitly contrasted with sodium lauryl sulfate.[^cd2003] But an older forearm study that tested it head to head with seven other surfactants concluded the damage mechanism is shared: "The mechanism of the damaging effect of the surfactants seems to be similar, although some minor differences were noted."[^barany] So it is a reasonable choice, not a skin treatment, and the strongest "mild" verdict in the literature comes from a manufacturer-authored paper.[^dick]

## The Rubric

**Cleansing with less irritation than a harsh sulfate**
- **Effect size: modest, and it is an avoided harm rather than a gain.** In 80 subjects with active atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, eczema, urticaria or generalised pruritus, patch-tested over 2 days, disodium laureth sulfosuccinate at 5% and 10% sat among the surfactants the authors said "have a chemical structure that makes them quite safe in real-life conditions", while "BAK and SLS have chemical properties that condition the number and intensity of the responses".[^cd2003] That is a meaningful separation from the reference irritant.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Two small human studies, the larger from 2003 and the comparative one from 1999 with 12 volunteers.[^barany][^cd2003] Neither reports a ranked irritation figure for this surfactant in its abstract, so the separation is qualitative. No trial measures a skin improvement, because there is none to measure.

**Being meaningfully milder than other mild surfactants**
- **Effect size: minimal to none established.** The 1999 forearm study applied 10% disodium laureth sulfosuccinate alongside 5% sodium cocoyl isethionate, 10% cocamidopropyl betaine and 10% lauryl glucoside and found that while "The surfactants induced different degrees of irritation", the underlying damage mechanism looked common across them.[^barany] Nothing here supports choosing it over the isethionate on [[sodium-cocoyl-isethionate]] or a glucoside such as [[caprylyl-capryl-glucoside]].
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, with a sponsorship caveat.** The one paper concluding that a blend containing it "was mild to the skin while also showing good cleansing performance" was authored by employees of Peter Greven Physioderm GmbH and Peter Greven GmbH & Co. KG and is copyright to the former.[^dick] It is also a study of **laureth-23** as the primary surfactant, with this ingredient one component of the winning combination, so it does not isolate it.

## The Evidence

**The comparison that matters is against SLS, and it goes this ingredient's way.** The 2003 study is the more demanding design because it tested people who already had skin disease, applying aqueous solutions including this surfactant at 5% and 10% to the upper back.[^cd2003] Its conclusion separates the gentle group from the two reference irritants, and it adds a finding worth carrying beyond this page: "there is no proof that the healthy skin of active atopic subjects is the most susceptible to the irritating effects of the tested substances."[^cd2003]

**The comparison against other mild surfactants is where the claim thins out.** Bárány and colleagues characterised the irritant reaction over two weeks after a single exposure to eight surfactants, using clinical assessment plus evaporimetry, laser Doppler flowmetry and corneometry.[^barany] The abstract reports that erythema, transepidermal water loss and blood flow moved together and were "inversely related to the delayed scaling and reduced skin capacitance", but publishes no per-surfactant ranking, so this page cannot tell you where it placed.[^barany]

**One concentration detail cuts both ways.** In that study the harsh reference was tested at 2% while this surfactant was tested at 10%, five times the concentration.[^barany] If the reactions were nonetheless comparable in kind, that is consistent with lower per-unit irritancy; it also means the trial was not an equal-concentration contest, and the abstract gives no numbers to settle it either way.

**Mechanistic work is on artificial skin, not people.** A 2025 study systematically characterised how solutions of this surfactant, sodium laureth sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate and others remove soil, but the substrate was artificial skin, so it speaks to cleansing performance rather than to tolerance on a human face.[^molecules]
