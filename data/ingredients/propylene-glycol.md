Propylene glycol (INCI name) is one of the most common ingredients in skincare, a small humectant and solvent used at low percentages to hold water in a formula and dissolve other actives. Its more interesting, less-known use is at high concentration: a 50% solution in water is a cheap, effective topical treatment for pityriasis (tinea) versicolor and other conditions driven by the *Malassezia* yeast.[^faergemann80][^faergemann93]

> Propylene glycol wears two hats. At the fraction of a percent it appears at in most products, it is an unremarkable humectant and penetration helper, comparable to but weaker than [[glycerin]]. At 50% in water it is a genuine, inexpensive anti-*Malassezia* treatment, but that evidence rests on a small, old, uncontrolled study, and 50% is a concentration you will almost never find in a finished cosmetic. It is also a recognized contact allergen and can sting at high strength. Judge it as a solid workhorse humectant, and as a budget anti-yeast option that a dermatologist might reach for, not as a hero active.

## The Rubric

Propylene glycol is graded on its two real uses: humectant hydration and, at high concentration, anti-*Malassezia* activity.

**Anti-*Malassezia* (pityriasis versicolor, and by extension fungal acne and seborrheic dermatitis)**
- **Effect size: notable, in the one small study that tested it.** In an open study, 20 patients with tinea versicolor used propylene glycol 50% in water twice daily for 2 weeks, and "all of these were cured when seen 2 weeks after the last day of treatment," with only 2 reporting slight burning.[^faergemann80] A later review confirmed that "propylene glycol 50% in water is effective and cheap" for pityriasis versicolor, while listing several other effective topicals and noting oral antifungals are very effective.[^faergemann93] Because pityriasis versicolor, [[fungal-acne]], and [[seborrheic-dermatitis]] are all driven by the same *Malassezia* yeast, this is the mechanism behind its use for those conditions.
- **Evidence quality: limited.** The clinical support is essentially one small (n=20), open-label, uncontrolled study from 1980 plus a narrative review; there is no modern randomized, vehicle-controlled trial, and pityriasis versicolor responds to many treatments, so the striking "cure" rate should be read cautiously.[^faergemann80][^faergemann93]

**Hydration (cosmetic humectant)**
- **Effect size: modest.** As a humectant, propylene glycol draws and holds water in the outer skin much as other small polyols do; it is effective but ordinary, and it also improves how well other actives dissolve and penetrate.
- **Evidence quality: adequate but unremarkable.** Its humectant role is well accepted and not controversial, but it is a supporting-cast ingredient rather than a studied hero active for hydration.

## The Evidence

Propylene glycol's honest profile is a ubiquitous, well-tolerated humectant with one striking but thin piece of anti-fungal evidence. The clinical data point is old and small: 20 tinea versicolor patients treated with the 50% solution were all clear at follow-up in an uncontrolled study, and a review called the same regimen "effective and cheap" while making clear it is one option among several (imidazoles, antidandruff shampoos, ciclopirox, terbinafine) and that short oral antifungal courses are the most effective.[^faergemann80][^faergemann93] For the *Malassezia*-driven conditions where this matters, the better-evidenced and better-tolerated topicals are ranked on [[fungal-acne]] and [[seborrheic-dermatitis]] (for example [[ketoconazole]], [[selenium-sulfide]], and [[piroctone-olamine]]); propylene glycol 50% is the cheap, generic alternative rather than the first choice. Its everyday cosmetic role as a low-percentage humectant and solvent is real but minor, and it is worth knowing that propylene glycol is a documented contact allergen and can irritate at high concentrations.

## How It Works

At low concentrations propylene glycol is a humectant and solvent: a small glycol that binds water in the stratum corneum and helps dissolve and carry other ingredients, including some actives, into the formula and the skin. At the much higher 50% concentration used against *Malassezia*, it is thought to act as an antifungal by dehydrating and disrupting the lipid-dependent yeast rather than by a specific drug target, which fits its broad, cheap, and non-resistance-prone behavior; the underlying yeast biology is covered on [[fungal-acne]] and [[seborrheic-dermatitis]].

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Hydrating / helps other ingredients absorb."** Fair for a humectant and solvent; propylene glycol does hold water and aid penetration, though at the low levels used it is a supporting ingredient, not the reason a product works.[^faergemann93]
- **"Fungal-acne safe."** Propylene glycol is not a *Malassezia* food source the way some fatty esters are, and at high strength it is actively anti-yeast, so it is a reasonable ingredient for fungal-acne-prone skin, but a low-percentage humectant dose is not a treatment; the treatments are on [[fungal-acne]].[^faergemann80]
