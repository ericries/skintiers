A US sunscreen filter that is legal, rare, and one of the ingredients FDA says it does not have enough data on. **In short:** it sits in the monograph at up to 5%, it appears in only nine products in FDA's current directory, and in 2019 the agency proposed it as Category III, meaning not shown to be generally recognised as safe and effective. The filter comparison is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

> **Approved is not the same as established, and this is the clearest case on the site.** The 2019 proposed rule says that "the public record does not currently contain sufficient data to support positive GRASE determinations for cinoxate, dioxybenzone, ensulizole, homosalate, meradimate, octinoxate, octisalate, octocrylene, padimate O, sulisobenzone, oxybenzone, or avobenzone", proposing all twelve as Category III.[^fr2019] Meradimate is then placed in the narrower group where "The most significant gaps in the administrative record exist", for which FDA expects data from every study type it describes.[^fr2019]

## The Rubric

**UV protection as one filter inside a finished sunscreen (health)**
Graded unknown on preliminary evidence, and the distinction matters. An SPF number and a broad-spectrum designation are earned by the finished formula in a standardised test, not by any single filter in it, so a product containing meradimate can be genuinely protective without that telling you what this ingredient contributed. Nothing read for this page isolates its contribution, and FDA's own position is that the record lacks the data to support a GRASE determination for it.[^fr2019] What the filters collectively do is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

**Covering the UVA I range specifically (health)**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. FDA defines "UVA I rays (340 to 400 nm) and UVA II rays (320 to 340 nm)", and in the same rule proposes to require that broad-spectrum products "meet a UVA I/UV ratio of 0.7 or higher", on the reasoning that UVA I makes up most of the UVA spectrum and carries skin-cancer risk.[^fr2019] No source read for this page characterises where meradimate absorbs or how it performs against that proposed ratio, so no claim is made here either way. A PubMed search on 2026-10-28 for the ingredient alongside sunscreen, UVA or photoprotection returned 12 records in total and none was read.[^pubmed]

## How It Works

Not described here, because nothing verified was found to describe. The monograph lists it as a permitted sunscreen active without characterising its absorption spectrum, and the 2019 rule discusses it only in the context of safety data gaps.[^cfr][^fr2019] Where this site can state a filter's mechanism it does, as on [[zinc-oxide]] and [[avobenzone]]; here it would be invention.

## The Evidence

**What the monograph actually permits.** 21 CFR 352.10 lists the permitted sunscreen actives with ceilings, including "Menthyl anthranilate up to 5 percent", alongside avobenzone at 3, homosalate at 15, octocrylene at 10 and oxybenzone at 6.[^cfr] Five percent is the ceiling, and the most prominent product using it sits exactly at it.

**It is genuinely rare, and never used alone.** openFDA's drug directory returns nine current products containing meradimate, and every one of them is a combination with other filters: four Pro-Tect professional sunscreens, a lip product, a scar-gel sunscreen, a Principal Secret product, and the one most readers will recognise, [[cerave-am-facial-moisturizing-lotion-spf-30]], which carries "Menthyl Anthranilate (Meradimate) 5%" alongside homosalate, octinoxate, octocrylene and zinc oxide.[^openfda] A filter that is always one of four or five in a formula is a filter whose own effect is hard to see.

**The Category III placement is about safety data, not a finding of harm.** Category III means the record is insufficient, not that the ingredient was shown to be unsafe, and the rule is explicit that it expects submitted data to resolve it.[^fr2019] The same proposal put zinc oxide and titanium dioxide forward as GRASE, which is the contrast drawn on [[zinc-oxide]]. Two filters in one product can therefore sit on opposite sides of that line, which is true of the CeraVe lotion above.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not medical advice. Nothing here is a reason to stop using a sunscreen you already tolerate: a tested SPF on a finished product is a tested SPF, and the sun-protection case overall is on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and [[sun-damage-photoaging]]. If you want a filter set the agency has proposed as GRASE, that is a reason to read the Drug Facts panel rather than to go without.

## Common Marketing Claims

**No marketing copy is quoted here.** Meradimate is not marketed as a hero ingredient by any brand page consulted for this entry; it appears in Drug Facts panels rather than on the front of a box, which is itself informative about how much selling power it carries.

**Mineral versus chemical, where this one sits.** Meradimate is an organic (so-called chemical) filter, and products pairing it with zinc oxide are hybrids rather than mineral sunscreens. The mislabelling pattern in that category is covered on [[zinc-oxide]] and [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].
