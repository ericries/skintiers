The citrus-smelling terpene, one of the most frequently declared fragrance ingredients in skincare. It is in a formula for scent, not for skin, which is why the grade here is `weak`. **Bottom line:** as with [[geraniol]] and [[citronellol]], the sensitiser is the OXIDISED form, and the honest position is that it tests positive often while its clinical relevance is still unsettled.

> **5.1% of 5,773 patch-tested dermatitis patients reacted to oxidised limonene, and the authors still would not recommend screening.** In a Swedish series running 2013 to 2020, "The prevalence rates of contact allergy to ox. linalool and ox. limonene were 7.0% and 5.1%, respectively", with "Significantly increasing trends of contact allergy" over the period.[^sukakul] Their conclusion is the sentence worth carrying: "More studies concerning the clinical relevance before recommending these substances for screening are required."[^sukakul]

## The Rubric

**Effect on skin: none claimed.** Limonene is there to smell of citrus. No efficacy evidence was sought because no benefit is advertised for it.

**Tolerability: common on patch testing, in a population selected for dermatitis.** The 5.1% above describes patients attending a contact dermatitis clinic, not the public. Read it as `among people already being investigated for a rash`, which is the only denominator the study offers.[^sukakul]

**Oxidation is the whole mechanism, exactly as with [[geraniol]].** Fresh limonene is a weak sensitiser; air exposure generates hydroperoxides that are not. This is why an old, often-opened bottle is not the same proposition as a fresh one, and why patch testing uses the oxidised material deliberately.

**It travels with oxidised linalool.** The same study tested both and found linalool the more common at 7.0%.[^sukakul] [[citronellol]] records the tightest version of this pattern: among patients reacting to oxidised citronellol, 75 to 91 percent also reacted to oxidised linalool or oxidised limonene.

**Timing changes the answer.** "More than 95% of contact allergy cases were identified on Day 3/4", so a patch test read earlier than that will miss most of them.[^sukakul] Anyone told their fragrance test was negative should know when it was read.

**Who reacts is not random.** Patients allergic to these two were "significantly younger than those with contact allergy to other fragrances and were predominantly female", while strong reactions were "associated with older age and multiple fragrance allergies".[^sukakul] The study reports these as observed associations and does not explain them.

## The Evidence

One large, well-matched source, and it argues against overclaiming. 5,773 consecutive dermatitis patients, patch tested with the baseline series, the individual constituents of fragrance mix I and II, and both oxidised terpenes, over eight years.[^sukakul] The size and the consecutive sampling are its strengths.

What it does not establish: that a positive patch test explains a given person's rash. The authors say so themselves in asking for clinical-relevance studies before screening is recommended.[^sukakul] A reader who takes 5.1% as `limonene causes dermatitis in one in twenty people` has misread both the denominator and the claim.

## Common Marketing Claims

There are none to quarantine. No brand advertises limonene as a benefit; it appears in ingredient and allergen-disclosure lists rather than in marketing copy. A scent molecule with a measurable sensitisation rate and no claimed upside is judged purely on tolerability, as on [[citronellol]].
