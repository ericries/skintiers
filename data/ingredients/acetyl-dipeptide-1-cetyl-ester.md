Acetyl Dipeptide-1 Cetyl Ester (INCI name, trade name Calmosensine, chemically hexadecyl N-acetyl-tyrosyl-arginate, C33H57N5O5) is a synthetic lipopeptide, a cetyl-esterified dipeptide of acetylated tyrosine and arginine, marketed as a "well-being" and anti-irritation [[peptides|peptide]] for [[sensitive-skin]] products.[^pubchem] It appears in leave-on facial products for self-reported sensitive skin, most visibly in La Roche-Posay's Toleriane line, and the independent evidence for it is thin: two systematic reviews of sensitive-skin actives class it as one of only a few peptides with any human data at all, but the one comparative in vitro/in vivo study that tested it head-to-head against a competing calming ingredient found it did not inhibit the TRPV1 pain receptor and was slower and less effective at reducing capsaicin-induced stinging than the comparator.[^ferreira][^resende][^sulzberger][^schoelermann]

> In a Beiersdorf in vitro assay, acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester "had no effect on receptor activation" of TRPV1 (the capsaicin/heat pain receptor), and in a subsequent single-blind, randomized, split-face clinical comparison in 31 women with sensitive skin, a product containing it produced significantly less reduction in capsaicin-induced burning at 1 and 2 minutes than a product containing 4-t-butylcyclohexanol.[^sulzberger][^schoelermann]

## The Rubric

**Skin barrier and sensitivity signaling (topical, in vitro)**
- **Effect size: unquantified in humans; positive but industry-only in cell culture.** An Oriflame-funded in vitro study in human primary keratinocytes reported that the ingredient "significantly upregulated epidermal barrier genes (Aquaporin 3 [AQP3], Filaggrin [FLG], caspase 14, and keratin 10)" and, together with niacinamide, "improved dermal gene expression (fibrillin-1, Collagen type 1 alpha 1, Decorin, Lysyl oxidase-like 1)" in fibroblasts.[^khmaladze] No fold-change or percentage figures are given in the abstract, and the compound was tested only as one of several actives in a multi-ingredient formula concept, not isolated with a dose-response.
- **Evidence quality: weak, cell-culture only, sponsor-funded, no human skin-barrier endpoint.** The gene-expression data come from cultured keratinocytes and fibroblasts, not from skin biopsies or barrier function measurements (e.g., transepidermal water loss) in volunteers, and the study was run by the marketer of a product using the ingredient.[^khmaladze]

**Immediate calming of subjective stinging/burning (topical, capsaicin-challenge model)**
- **Effect size: present but inferior to a comparator ingredient, no placebo arm.** In a controlled, single-blind, randomized split-face design, 31 women with self-perceived sensitive to very sensitive skin had capsaicin applied to both nasolabial folds, then received a product containing acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester on one side and a 4-t-butylcyclohexanol product on the other; "both products alleviated capsaicin-induced burning during the first 15 min," but the comparator gave "significant lower values for burning/stinging after one, and two minutes post-application" than the acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester side, with no significant difference at peak burning, overall severity, or duration.[^schoelermann]
- **Evidence quality: weak-moderate for immediate symptom relief, but no vehicle/placebo control exists.** The trial is randomized and blinded (assessor, not necessarily subject, given it compared two active products) with a defined sensitive-skin population and an objective capsaicin challenge, which is more rigorous than most peptide cosmetic studies. But it compares two active-containing products to each other, not either against an untreated or vehicle side, so it cannot show the ingredient beats doing nothing; it can only show it is the weaker of two actives tested.[^schoelermann]

**Mechanistic anti-inflammatory activity (in vitro, molecular target)**
- **Effect size: modest, and inferior to a comparator at the same design's own benchmark.** In the same Beiersdorf research program, acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester did reduce release of the inflammatory mediators tested, but licochalcone A "significantly decreased NFκB signalling and PGE2 secretion, at lower concentrations than acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester," and it had no measurable effect on TRPV1, the nerve receptor mechanism the researchers ultimately built their marketed active around instead.[^sulzberger]
- **Evidence quality: weak.** In vitro only, from the manufacturer of a competing calming-ingredient product, with no isolated-ingredient human trial.[^sulzberger]

## How It Works

Acetyl Dipeptide-1 Cetyl Ester is a synthetic dipeptide, N-acetyl-tyrosine linked to arginine, esterified with a cetyl (C16) alcohol tail to improve lipid solubility and skin penetration, marketed under the trade name Calmosensine.[^pubchem] Its proposed rationale in sensitive-skin formulations is to dampen the release of pro-inflammatory and neurosensory mediators implicated in stinging, burning, and reactive skin, alongside possible effects on epidermal barrier and dermal extracellular-matrix gene expression reported in a manufacturer-funded cell-culture study.[^khmaladze] Independent testing of the actual molecular mechanism is limited: a Beiersdorf research group that screened it alongside two other candidate calming ingredients found it had "no effect" on activation of TRPV1, the capsaicin- and heat-sensing ion channel thought to drive much of the burning/stinging sensation in reactive skin, and it required higher concentrations than licochalcone A to achieve comparable reductions in NFκB signalling and PGE2 secretion, two other inflammatory pathways.[^sulzberger] Two independent academic reviews of sensitive-skin cosmetic actives, surveying published and patent literature, both describe the human evidence base for this ingredient as sparse: one review found it and panthenol were among the only actives "studied on volunteers having sensitive skin" but flagged that clinical studies of sensitive-skin actives broadly "remain sparse and lack methodological quality," and the peptide-specific review noted that "available data is mostly found in patents and supplier brochures, and not in randomized placebo-controlled studies."[^ferreira][^resende]

## The Evidence

**Split-face capsaicin-stinging comparison versus 4-t-butylcyclohexanol (topical, comparative, no placebo).** Beiersdorf researchers ran a controlled, single-blind, randomized, split-face trial in 31 women aged 19 to 65 with self-perceived sensitive to very sensitive skin who had a positive stinging response to a 40 ppm capsaicin cream after a 3-day washout. A La Roche-Posay Toleriane product containing acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester was applied to one nasolabial fold and a Eucerin product containing 4-t-butylcyclohexanol to the other, with burning severity rated at 1, 2, 5, 10 and 15 minutes post-application. Both sides improved from baseline, but the 4-t-butylcyclohexanol side showed "significant lower values for burning/stinging after one, and two minutes post-application" than the acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester side, with no significant difference at peak intensity, overall severity, or duration of burning.[^schoelermann] *This was a real trial in a genuinely sensitive-skin population with an objective irritant challenge, but it was funded and run by the manufacturer of the winning comparator product, tested finished formulas rather than isolated actives at matched concentrations, and included no untreated or vehicle control, so it shows relative inferiority to one competitor rather than any absolute effect size.*

**In vitro mechanism screen plus in vivo testing of the comparator ingredients (mechanistic, industry).** The same Beiersdorf group screened acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester alongside 4-t-butylcyclohexanol and licochalcone A for effects on PGE2 release, NFκB activation, and TRPV1 activation or CGRP release. Acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester "had no effect on receptor activation" of TRPV1, while 4-t-butylcyclohexanol significantly reduced it; licochalcone A outperformed acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester on NFκB and PGE2 at lower concentrations. The paper's own in vivo trials went on to test only 4-t-butylcyclohexanol and licochalcone A, not acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester in isolation.[^sulzberger] *A competitor's own mechanism-screening study found this ingredient inactive on one of the three targets tested and less potent than its chosen alternative on the other two, which is a meaningfully skeptical data point precisely because the authors had no incentive to be generous to it.*

**Cell-culture gene-expression study within a multi-active formula (in vitro, industry, not isolated).** Oriflame researchers tested a formulation containing acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester alongside diglucosyl gallic acid, diacetyl boldine, and niacinamide in primary human keratinocytes and fibroblasts. The ingredient "significantly upregulated epidermal barrier genes (Aquaporin 3, Filaggrin, caspase 14, and keratin 10)" in keratinocytes and, together with niacinamide, improved fibroblast expression of fibrillin-1, Collagen type 1 alpha 1, Decorin, and Lysyl oxidase-like 1, plus anti-glycation and proteasomal activity.[^khmaladze] *Encouraging gene-expression signal in cell culture, but it is sponsor-funded, reports no numeric effect sizes in the available abstract, was not isolated with a dose-response curve, and has no corresponding measurement in real skin.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Calms and soothes reactive/sensitive skin."** Partially supported at best. The only head-to-head clinical test available shows a product containing this ingredient did reduce capsaicin-induced stinging from baseline, but a competing calming ingredient did so faster and to a greater degree in the same trial, and there is no published trial comparing it against a true vehicle or placebo.[^schoelermann] Two independent literature reviews both describe the underlying human evidence for sensitive-skin peptides, including this one, as sparse and largely confined to sponsor sources rather than randomized placebo-controlled studies.[^ferreira][^resende]
- **"Targets the molecular pathways of skin sensitivity."** Overstated by the one study that actually checked. A competitor's mechanism screen found it inactive against TRPV1, the specific neurosensory pain receptor most directly linked to stinging and burning, and less potent than an alternative ingredient on the inflammatory NFκB/PGE2 pathways it did affect.[^sulzberger] The "targets the molecular pathways" framing survives only loosely, and not as a strength relative to alternatives.
