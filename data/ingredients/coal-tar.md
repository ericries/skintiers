Coal tar is a byproduct of coal processing and one of the oldest topical treatments in dermatology, recognized by the FDA as an over-the-counter active for dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and psoriasis at 0.5% to 5%.[^fda] It slows the excessive skin-cell turnover and calms the inflammation that drive plaque and scaling, and it is still sold in medicated scalp and body products.

> Coal tar is a genuinely effective, FDA-monograph treatment for the scaling of psoriasis and seborrheic dermatitis, but it is old, modest, and messy: it stains, smells, and increases sun sensitivity, and newer agents have largely displaced it. The recurring worry, that a coal-derived product might be carcinogenic, has been tested directly: a 13,200-patient cohort found no increased cancer risk from therapeutic (as opposed to industrial) coal tar use.[^roelofzen]

## The Rubric

**Scaling of psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis, and dandruff**
- **Effect size: moderate and established, but modest and dated.** Coal tar is an FDA over-the-counter monograph active for controlling dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and psoriasis at 0.5% to 5%, reflecting decades of recognized use.[^fda] It works by slowing keratinocyte proliferation and reducing inflammation, which cuts the scaling and plaque of these conditions. The magnitude is modest and the high-quality modern trial base is thin relative to newer treatments, which have largely superseded it except in over-the-counter scalp products.
- **Evidence quality: regulatory and historical rather than fresh trials.** Its standing rests on FDA-monograph recognition and long clinical use more than on a deep base of recent randomized trials.[^fda] For seborrheic dermatitis it sits alongside the antifungals ranked on [[anti-dandruff-seborrheic-dermatitis-treatments-by-evidence]] as an older keratolytic-and-anti-inflammatory option.

**Safety: the carcinogenicity question (a separate axis, not an efficacy grade)**
- Occupational and industrial exposure to coal tar is a recognized carcinogen, which understandably raises concern about therapeutic use. That concern was tested: in a cohort of 13,200 patients with psoriasis and eczema (median coal tar exposure 6 months), coal tar "did not increase the risk of non-skin malignancies (hazard ratio (HR) 0.92; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.78-1.09), or the risk of skin cancer (HR 1.09; 95% CI 0.69-1.72)" versus a corticosteroid-treated reference group.[^roelofzen] The real day-to-day drawbacks are cosmetic and mild-medical instead: it stains skin, hair, and fabric, has a strong odor, and increases photosensitivity, so daytime sun protection matters during use.

## The Evidence

Coal tar is one of the few topical actives whose regulatory status is unambiguous: the FDA recognizes it as a safe and effective over-the-counter active for dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and psoriasis at 0.5% to 5%.[^fda] Mechanistically it is antiproliferative and anti-inflammatory, which addresses the excess scaling of these conditions. What it lacks, relative to newer agents, is a large modern randomized-trial base, and its use is now concentrated in over-the-counter scalp and body products.

On safety, the headline question is cancer, because industrial coal tar exposure is carcinogenic. The largest relevant study, Roelofzen and colleagues' cohort of 13,200 dermatology patients, found no increased risk of skin or non-skin cancer from therapeutic coal tar compared with corticosteroid treatment.[^roelofzen] The practical downsides are staining, odor, and photosensitivity rather than cancer risk.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Natural" coal tar.** Coal tar is a coal-processing byproduct; "natural" is a framing, not a safety statement, and the meaningful safety question (cancer) has been studied on its own terms.[^roelofzen]
- **Psoriasis or dandruff "cure."** Coal tar controls scaling while it is used; these are managed, relapsing conditions, and it does not cure them.[^fda]
