Zinc oxide is an inorganic (mineral) sunscreen filter that absorbs ultraviolet radiation across both UVB and UVA, and one of only two UV filters the US FDA proposed classifying as safe and effective in its 2019 sunscreen review.[^fda][^schneider]

Zinc oxide is one of the two mineral UV filters used in sunscreens, the other being titanium dioxide. It works across the ultraviolet band the FDA defines for sunscreens, roughly 290 to 400 nm, which covers UVB (sunburn and most of the DNA photodamage that drives skin cancer) and UVA (the deeper-penetrating wavelengths that drive photoaging).[^fda][^schneider] Two things set it apart from the older organic filters common on US shelves. Its absorption reaches further into long-wave UVA (UVA1) than titanium dioxide or most single organic filters, and it is photostable, so it does not break down in sunlight the way avobenzone, the main organic UVA filter in US products, does.[^schneider][^cavalcanti] The separate case that filtering UV actually prevents photoaging and skin cancer rests on randomized trials of whole sunscreens; that evidence, strongest for slowing photoaging, is covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

> Zinc oxide is a photostable mineral filter with real broad-spectrum coverage, absorbing from UVB through long-wave UVA, and the FDA has proposed it (with titanium dioxide) as generally recognized as safe and effective at up to 25%.[^fda][^cole][^schneider] Its distinctive value is stable long-UVA protection; its limits are that the strength a given product delivers depends on how much is applied and how evenly the particles are dispersed, and that standardized ways to measure its UVA protection are still disputed.[^cavalcanti]

## The Rubric

Grades are for zinc oxide as a UV filter. Framing is relative and names comparators (titanium dioxide, avobenzone, the organic filters).

**UVB protection (sunburn, DNA photodamage)**
- **Effect size: notable.** Zinc oxide absorbs in the UVB region and contributes genuine sunburn protection, but it is not the strongest UVB absorber among filters: Schneider and Lim report that titanium dioxide "provides better UVB protection," and the organic UVB filters (octinoxate, homosalate, octisalate) are efficient UVB absorbers by weight.[^schneider] "Notable: a real UVB absorber, though titanium dioxide and the organic UVB filters are more efficient in that band."
- **Evidence quality: solid.** Optical measurement and the FDA's safety-and-effectiveness review converge on zinc oxide's UVB performance.[^cole][^fda]

**UVA and long-wave UVA (UVA1) protection with photostability (photoaging, deeper damage)**
- **Effect size: notable.** This is zinc oxide's distinctive strength. Schneider and Lim describe it as having "a broad UVA-UVB absorption curve," reaching into long-wave UVA (UVA1) further than titanium dioxide, and it is photostable, unlike avobenzone, the older organic UVA filter still standard in US products, which degrades in sunlight ([[sunscreen-uv-filters]] carries that comparison).[^schneider][^cavalcanti] Short of "strong" because a single mineral filter's absolute UVA protection factor depends on particle size, loading, and dispersion, and the modern organic UVA filters used outside the US (bemotrizinol, bisoctrizole, the Mexoryls) can reach higher UVA protection in a cosmetically lighter formula.[^cavalcanti] "Notable: the broadest, most photostable single mineral filter in long-wave UVA, stronger there than titanium dioxide or photounstable avobenzone."
- **Evidence quality: solid.** The spectroscopic picture is consistent, but a 2025 critical review flags "discrepancies in UVA protection assessment" and the absence of standardized UVA protocols, so the exact protection a specific product delivers is less settled than the qualitative coverage.[^cavalcanti][^cole]

## What We Actually Know

Zinc oxide is a well-characterized mineral UV filter with genuine broad-spectrum coverage. It absorbs across the ultraviolet range the FDA regulates for sunscreens (roughly 290 to 400 nm), and its absorption extends into long-wave UVA further than titanium dioxide's, giving it the broader UVA reach of the two mineral filters.[^fda][^schneider] It is photostable, so it holds up in sunlight rather than degrading.[^cavalcanti] The human safety data are reassuring: a study applying zinc oxide nanoparticle sunscreen repeatedly to volunteers found "no evidence of ZnO-NP penetration into the viable epidermis," and stable-isotope tracing found that "the overwhelming majority of applied (68)Zn was not absorbed," with a companion study putting the absorbed fraction at a "minimal estimate <0.01% of the applied dose," most likely as dissolved zinc ions rather than intact particles.[^mohammed][^gulson2010][^gulson2012] On that safety record the FDA proposed zinc oxide and titanium dioxide as "Category I," meaning generally recognized as safe and effective, the only two of its reviewed sunscreen actives it placed there, against twelve organic filters it left in Category III for insufficient data.[^fda] Whether filtering UV in turn prevents photoaging and skin cancer is answered by randomized trials of whole sunscreens rather than of the isolated filter; regular broad-spectrum use has trial support for slowing photoaging and reducing melanoma, covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] and [[anti-aging]].

## How It Works

The label "physical blocker" implies zinc oxide works like a mirror, bouncing UV away. Optical measurement shows otherwise. Using an integrating sphere to separate reflection from absorption, Cole and colleagues found that across the UV range reflection by zinc oxide and titanium dioxide averaged only 4-5%, which they note is "less than SPF 2," and concluded the filters "provide UV protection primarily via absorption of UV radiation" and "not through significant reflection or scattering."[^cole] Zinc oxide is a semiconductor: it absorbs UV photons whose energy exceeds its band gap and re-emits that energy as heat, and only at wavelengths past the band gap (long UVA and visible light) does it become mainly a reflector.[^cole] Because that absorption is a property of the material rather than a fragile molecular bond, zinc oxide is photostable and does not photodegrade the way some organic filters do.[^cole][^cavalcanti] The protection actually delivered still depends on the applied amount and on how finely and evenly the particles are dispersed in the formula.[^cavalcanti]

## The Evidence

Primary evidence, by question:

**Spectral coverage and mechanism.** Cole et al. (Photodermatology, Photoimmunology & Photomedicine, 2016) used an integrating sphere and found that reflection accounted for only 4-5% of zinc oxide's and titanium dioxide's action across the UV range, with the remainder from "semiconductor band gap mediated absorbance of the UV photons."[^cole] Schneider and Lim's review in the same journal (2019) states the practical division between the two mineral filters plainly: "ZnO has a broad UVA-UVB absorption curve, while TiO2 provides better UVB protection."[^schneider] A 2025 critical review of the physical characterization of zinc oxide sunscreens describes the filter as offering "broad-spectrum efficacy, photochemical stability, and biocompatibility," while cautioning about "the lack of standardized in vitro and in vivo SPF protocols" and "discrepancies in UVA protection assessment."[^cavalcanti]

**Regulatory status.** In its 2019 proposed rule, the FDA concluded it had "sufficient safety data on both zinc oxide and titanium dioxide" and proposed that sunscreen products containing them "at concentrations of up to 25 percent" would be GRASE, classifying the two as "Category I."[^fda] It placed twelve organic filters (including avobenzone, oxybenzone, octinoxate, homosalate, octisalate, and octocrylene) in Category III, meaning the available data were insufficient to determine that they are GRASE.[^fda]

**Skin penetration and systemic absorption.** Because zinc oxide is often milled to nanoparticle size (under 100 nm) for cosmetic transparency, its safety turned on whether those particles cross the skin. Mohammed et al. (Journal of Investigative Dermatology, 2019) applied zinc oxide nanoparticle sunscreen to volunteers over 5 days and, using multiphoton imaging, found the particles "accumulated on the skin surface and within the skin furrows" but "did not enter or cause cellular toxicity in the viable epidermis," concluding there was "no evidence of ZnO-NP penetration into the viable epidermis."[^mohammed] Two stable-isotope tracer studies by Gulson and colleagues, which labeled the applied zinc so that absorbed metal could be distinguished from the body's own, found that a small amount of zinc did reach the blood: "The overwhelming majority of applied (68)Zn was not absorbed" (Toxicological Sciences, 2010), and a companion pilot (Science of the Total Environment, 2012) put the absorbed fraction at a "minimal estimate <0.01% of the applied dose," noting it was not known whether the zinc crossed as particles or as soluble ions.[^gulson2010][^gulson2012] The consistent reading across the three is that intact zinc oxide nanoparticles do not reach living skin, while a trace of zinc is absorbed, most plausibly as ions, with no demonstrated local toxicity.[^mohammed][^gulson2010][^gulson2012]

**Environmental note (separate from photoprotection).** "Mineral" is sometimes read as environmentally benign, but the marine evidence is mixed: an experimental study of tropical stony corals reported that "Uncoated ZnO induces a severe and fast coral bleaching," whereas two modified titanium dioxide forms tested alongside it did not.[^corinaldesi] This bears on environmental impact, not on human photoprotection.

## Uses

Well-supported: broad-spectrum UV filtering in leave-on sunscreens, where zinc oxide contributes UVB protection and, distinctively, photostable long-wave UVA coverage, at concentrations up to the 25% the FDA proposed as GRASE.[^fda][^schneider][^cavalcanti] It is one of the two filters (with titanium dioxide) available to formulators building a mineral-only sunscreen, and its photostability means it does not need the stabilizers that photounstable organic UVA filters require ([[sunscreen-uv-filters]] covers that filter landscape).[^cavalcanti] The main practical limits are cosmetic and formulation-dependent: Schneider and Lim note that milling the particles to under 100 nm makes them "much less visible after application," which addresses the white cast that larger mineral particles leave, and the delivered protection factor depends on applied amount and dispersion rather than on the presence of the filter alone.[^schneider][^cavalcanti]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Physical blocker that reflects UV away."** Mostly a mechanistic myth. Optical measurement shows reflection accounts for only 4-5% of zinc oxide's action across the UV range; it protects mainly by absorbing UV, the same basic mechanism as organic filters.[^cole]
- **"Nanoparticles absorb into your bloodstream," or conversely "zinc oxide is completely inert and nothing gets in."** Both overstate. Intact nanoparticles do not reach the viable epidermis, but a trace of zinc (well under 0.01% of the applied dose) is absorbed as ions, with no demonstrated toxicity.[^mohammed][^gulson2010][^gulson2012]
- **"The best or most complete UVA protection."** Zinc oxide is broad and photostable, but its absolute UVA protection factor depends on the formula, and a 2025 review notes "discrepancies in UVA protection assessment" and no standardized UVA protocol; titanium dioxide is the better UVB absorber of the two mineral filters.[^cavalcanti][^schneider]
- **"Mineral, so it is reef-safe."** Not an efficacy claim, and not clearly supported: an experimental coral study found uncoated zinc oxide nanoparticles caused "severe and fast coral bleaching."[^corinaldesi]
