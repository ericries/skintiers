Cholesterol (INCI Cholesterol) is one of the three physiologic lipids of the stratum corneum barrier, alongside [[ceramides]] and free fatty acids. Elias describes the barrier's extracellular matrix as built on "the approximately 1:1:1 molar ratio of ceramides (Cer), cholesterol, and nonessential fatty acids," in which "all three lipids are required to form the lamellar structures that regulate barrier function."[^2] In skincare it appears in some barrier-repair moisturizers as one component of a physiologic-lipid blend.

## The Rubric

Grades below are relative and name comparators. They describe cholesterol's role as a barrier lipid at the mechanism and formulation level, not proof that any finished product improves a clinical outcome.

**Barrier lipid replacement / barrier recovery**
- **Effect size: modest, and only as part of a complete, correctly proportioned lipid mixture.** In Man and colleagues' barrier-repair experiments, "three stratum corneum lipids, ceramides, cholesterol (CHOL), and free fatty acids (FA), are required for permeability barrier homeostasis," and "application of one or two of these lipids to perturbed skin delays barrier recovery; only equimolar mixtures allow normal recovery."[^1] Cholesterol on its own, or an unbalanced pair, delayed recovery rather than helping.
- **Evidence quality: mechanism and formulation level.** The findings come from experimental barrier-recovery models in perturbed skin,[^1] set against cholesterol's established structural role in the barrier lipid matrix.[^2] This is not evidence that a given cholesterol-containing product outperforms a plainer moisturizer in a clinical trial.

## How It Works

Cholesterol is a structural lipid of the stratum corneum's intercellular membranes. Elias describes the matrix as an "approximately 1:1:1 molar ratio of ceramides (Cer), cholesterol, and nonessential fatty acids," in which "cholesterol, instead, partners with the sphingoid base of Cer and acylCer," and states that "all three lipids are required to form the lamellar structures that regulate barrier function."[^2] Removing or unbalancing any one of the three degrades the lamellar organization that limits water loss.

## The Evidence

Man and colleagues tested physiologic lipid mixtures on perturbed skin and reported that all three barrier lipids are required, that "application of one or two of these lipids to perturbed skin delays barrier recovery; only equimolar mixtures allow normal recovery," and that from an equimolar base "further acceleration of barrier repair occurs as the ratio of any of these ingredients is increased up to 3-fold."[^1] This is the mechanistic basis for physiologic-lipid formulations, and it also sets a limit: the benefit depends on supplying all three lipids in the right proportion, so cholesterol is one necessary component rather than an active in its own right. The evidence is at the barrier-recovery and formulation level, not proof that a specific product containing cholesterol works better than a plainer moisturizer.

## Common Marketing Claims

Treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Barrier-repair complex that clinically restores your skin barrier."** The physiologic-lipid evidence is at the mechanism and formulation level, and it depends on the correct ratio of all three lipids; in Man and colleagues' work an incomplete or unbalanced mixture delayed barrier recovery.[^1] The experiments support cholesterol's role as a barrier lipid, not the claim that any particular product measurably repairs skin. Discounted.
