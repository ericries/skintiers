Sodium isethionate is a simple salt, sodium 2-hydroxyethane sulfonate, used in cleansing products as a processing and formulation aid rather than as an active. Its better-known relatives are the actual surfactants built from it, [[sodium-cocoyl-isethionate]] and [[sodium-lauroyl-isethionate]], and those are the ingredients doing the cleansing work in a syndet bar.

> **If you found this on an ingredient list, it is not why the product works.** There is no skin-efficacy literature on sodium isethionate at all: a PubMed search pairing it with skin or dermatology returns two records, one a 1984 paper about the cosmetic surfactants *derived* from it and one a 1969 study of anion effects on sodium transport.[^cosmsurf][^anion] The only substantial modern paper on the salt itself is a 2024 occupational risk assessment about **factory workers**, not consumers.[^ngra] So it is graded weak here for the plain reason that there is nothing to grade.

## The Rubric

**Any visible skin benefit**
- **Effect size: none established.** No trial, in any population, measures a skin outcome from sodium isethionate. It is not formulated to deliver one; the cleansing is done by the isethionate-based surfactants on [[sodium-cocoyl-isethionate]] and [[sodium-lauroyl-isethionate]].
- **Evidence quality: absent rather than weak.** The distinction matters. This is not a case of thin or conflicting trials, as with the mild surfactants on [[disodium-laureth-sulfosuccinate]] and [[caprylyl-capryl-glucoside]]. It is an absence of any relevant study.

**Being gentler than other cleansing aids**
- **Effect size: not assessable.** The claim is sometimes made for syndet bars as a category rather than for this ingredient, and the category argument belongs to the surfactants in them.
- **Evidence quality: none identified for this ingredient.** Nothing on this page should be read as a reason to choose a product for containing it.

## The Evidence

**What the one modern paper actually covers, and who it is about.** A 2024 Toxicology paper used sodium isethionate as the worked example for a Next Generation Risk Assessment workflow in an **occupational** setting. It estimated worker exposures across the chemical's life cycle, modelled internal dose, and derived Bioactivity Exposure Ratios that "demonstrated the safety for workers exposed to SI under current levels of factory specific risk management".[^ngra] Physiologically based kinetic modelling "indicated a worst-case plasma maximum concentration (Cmax) of 0.8 μM across the SI life cycle", against points of departure "ranged from 104 to 5044 µM".[^ngra]

**That is a workplace finding, and this page does not stretch it into a consumer one.** The exposures modelled are those of people manufacturing and handling the raw chemical, not of someone washing their face with a bar containing it. The paper's own purpose is to demonstrate a non-animal assessment method, and the authors are drawn from Unilever, Clariant and Vantage Specialty Chemicals alongside a consultancy, so it is also industry-authored.[^ngra] It is cited here because it is the only substantial modern study of the salt, not because it tells you anything about your skin.

**Why a page exists for an ingredient with no evidence.** Readers meet names like this on ingredient lists and reasonably assume each one was chosen for an effect. Most were not. Recording the absence plainly is more useful than leaving a gap that a marketing claim could later fill. Gentle cleansers that do have evidence behind them are ranked on [[best-gentle-cleansers-sensitive-skin]].
