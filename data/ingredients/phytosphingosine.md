Phytosphingosine (INCI Phytosphingosine) is a free sphingoid base that occurs naturally in the stratum corneum, both on its own and as part of the backbone of [[ceramides]]. Pavicic and colleagues describe it as "a lipid occurring naturally in the stratum corneum," and report that it has antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory and clinical tests of acne.[^1] It is used in some barrier and blemish-oriented formulations as a cosmetic active.

## The Rubric

Grades below are relative and name comparators. They describe phytosphingosine's role as a physiologic lipid and cosmetic active at the mechanism and formulation level, not proof that any finished product works.

**Physiologic barrier and antimicrobial lipid**
- **Effect size: present as a natural component.** Phytosphingosine is one of the free sphingoid bases of the stratum corneum. Fischer and colleagues report that "sphingoid bases found in the outer layers of the skin exhibit antimicrobial activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria," and tested phytosphingosine among them.[^2]
- **Evidence quality: mechanism level.** The antimicrobial role rests on in vitro work with skin sphingoid bases,[^2] not on outcome trials of a phytosphingosine product.

**Acne / anti-inflammatory (as a cosmetic active)**
- **Effect size: preliminary.** Pavicic and colleagues reported antimicrobial activity against acne-associated bacteria, anti-inflammatory activity, and clinical results in acne, concluding that phytosphingosine could "enhance or complement existing acne therapies acting as an active cosmetic ingredient."[^1]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary (single study).** The clinical signal comes from one combined in vitro and in vivo cosmetic-science study,[^1] not from replicated randomized trials of the kind behind drug acne treatments (see [[acne]] and [[benzoyl-peroxide]]).

## The Evidence

Pavicic and colleagues studied phytosphingosine in vitro and in vivo for acne, reporting "strong anti-microbial effectiveness" against acne-related bacteria and "excellent clinical results in the context of skin care in acne, based on both anti-inflammatory and anti-microbial activity."[^1] They frame it as an active cosmetic ingredient that might complement acne therapy rather than replace it.[^1] Fischer and colleagues provide mechanistic support for the antimicrobial role of skin sphingoid bases: tested against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, sphingoid bases including phytosphingosine caused ultrastructural damage, consistent with the sphingoid bases of the stratum corneum contributing to cutaneous antimicrobial defense.[^2] Taken together, the evidence establishes phytosphingosine as a physiologic barrier lipid with antimicrobial activity at the mechanism and formulation level. It does not establish that a given product containing it treats acne or repairs the barrier in a controlled comparison.

## Common Marketing Claims

Treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Antibacterial active that clears acne."** The acne evidence is a single cosmetic-science study plus mechanistic antimicrobial data,[^1][^2] not the replicated controlled trials that support drug acne treatments (see [[acne]]). The authors themselves position it as a complement to therapy, not a treatment.[^1] Discounted as proof that any product clears acne.
