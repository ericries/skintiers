Kaolin (kaolinite, historically called China clay) is a native hydrated aluminum silicate clay with an approximate composition of Al2O3 . 2SiO2 . 2H2O, used in cosmetics as an absorbent, bulking agent, and opacifying agent, most often in rinse-off face masks, cleansers, and pressed or loose powders.[^cir] It is a close cosmetic relative of [[bentonite]], and the two clays are frequently formulated together in the same mask or cleanser.[^zhang]

> Kaolin's job in a mask, cleanser, or powder is to physically soak up surface oil for an immediate, temporary matte feel. That mechanism is plausible from its classification as a cosmetic absorbent and its layered mineral structure, and its concentration of use and skin tolerability are documented by a regulatory safety review, but no controlled human trial isolates topical kaolin alone for a lasting change to skin. The one on-site clay-mask study that included kaolin tested it combined with bentonite and other ingredients, in an uncontrolled, manufacturer-run design, so it cannot show what kaolin itself does; see [[bentonite]] for that study.[^cir][^zhang]

## The Rubric

**Oil absorption and mattifying (topical, cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: plausible from its physical structure and function, but not measured for kaolin alone.** Kaolin is listed as functioning in cosmetics as an abrasive, absorbent, anticaking agent, bulking agent, opacifying agent, skin protectant, and slip modifier.[^cir] The only on-site study of a clay mask that included kaolin tested it combined with bentonite and other ingredients, with no control or vehicle arm, so it cannot isolate kaolin's contribution; the study and its findings are detailed on [[bentonite]].[^zhang] It is a cosmetic effect (soaking up oil already on the skin), not a treatment of any skin disease.
- **Evidence quality: thin.** A search of the published literature turns up no randomized or controlled human trial testing topical kaolin alone for oil absorption, mattifying, or acne. The controlled human trials that do exist for kaolin test an unrelated use, kaolin-impregnated dressings for wound hemostasis (for example, controlling bleeding during debridement of diabetic foot ulcers), not a cosmetic skin-care application.[^gauze] The regulatory safety review covers concentration of use and tolerability, not efficacy.[^cir]

## What We Actually Know

Kaolin is defined by a Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel safety assessment as a native hydrated aluminum silicate, with a typical mineral composition of about 45% silicon dioxide, 39% aluminum oxide, and 14% water, along with smaller amounts of iron, magnesium, calcium, potassium, sodium, and titanium oxides.[^cir] The Panel's 2023 review of survey data submitted to the FDA's Voluntary Cosmetic Registration Program found kaolin had 787 reported uses in cosmetic products, the most of any clay ingredient reviewed, with the majority in leave-on formulations.[^cir] Concentration-of-use survey data collected by the Personal Care Products Council put kaolin's highest reported use in leave-on dermal (face and neck) products at up to 16%, and separately report it used at up to 15% in face powders, up to 14.5% in lipstick, up to 8.5% in eye shadow, and up to 5% in bath soaps and detergents.[^cir] Outside cosmetics, kaolin (colloidal) is an FDA-recognized over-the-counter skin-protectant active ingredient at concentrations of 4% to 20%, a monograph category for products meant to protect injured or exposed skin, distinct from its use in cosmetic masks and powders.[^cir]

On tolerability, the Panel's review cites human repeated-insult patch tests finding no sensitization from a lip product containing 14.5% kaolin (54 subjects) or a clay mask containing 40% kaolin (51 subjects); in a separate patch test of a clay mask containing 14.5% kaolin (103 subjects), one subject developed moderate erythema that progressed through the induction and challenge phases.[^cir] A four-week clinical use test of a facial cleanser containing 2% bentonite and 2% kaolin (50 subjects) found no visible irritation, though some subjects reported perceived discomfort or irritation.[^cir] Based on its review of this data, the Expert Panel concluded that kaolin is safe in cosmetics in current practices of use and concentration, including in face powders that may be incidentally inhaled, a determination it could not extend to several of the other clays it reviewed for lack of inhalation data.[^cir]

## How It Works

Kaolin's layered structure pairs a single silica tetrahedral sheet with a single alumina octahedral sheet, described by the Cosmetic Ingredient Review Panel as a unit in which "the tips of the silica tetrahedrons and one of the layers of the octahedral sheet form a common layer."[^cir] That is a different, more tightly bonded arrangement than bentonite and montmorillonite, whose two-tetrahedral-sheet "smectite" structure has as its "preeminent feature... the ability of water and organic molecules to enter between unit layers and expand," which is why those clays swell and gel with water in a way kaolin does not.[^cir] A cosmetic-ingredient supplier reported to the Panel a median particle size of about 3.1 micrometers for kaolin, compared with about 61.1 micrometers for bentonite.[^cir]

This structure and fine particle size are the basis for kaolin's classification as a cosmetic absorbent, bulking agent, and opacifying agent: a powder or paste of small, unswelling clay particles can take up surface oil and scatter light for a matte look.[^cir] No laboratory measurement of kaolin's specific oil-absorption capacity (the kind of gram-per-gram uptake test used to compare other cosmetic powders) turned up in this search, so the mechanism is described here as plausible from kaolin's structure and regulatory classification, not as a quantified, kaolin-specific effect.

## The Evidence

No controlled human trial isolates topical kaolin alone for a cosmetic or dermatologic skin benefit. The controlled trials that exist for kaolin test an unrelated application, kaolin-impregnated dressings used to control bleeding during wound debridement, which says nothing about kaolin's effect on facial skin.[^gauze]

The closest on-site evidence for a kaolin-containing skincare product is an open-label, uncontrolled study, run by the manufacturer, of a commercial clay mask containing both kaolin and bentonite among other ingredients, which measured reductions in skin oiliness after use.[^zhang] Because the product combined multiple clays and other actives and the study had no comparison arm, it cannot show what kaolin itself contributes, only what the finished multi-ingredient mask did. The study, its design, and its full results are detailed on [[bentonite]], which the mask also contained.[^zhang]

The regulatory safety literature on kaolin, summarized above, establishes how it is used and how well it is tolerated, not whether it improves any measurable skin outcome.[^cir]

## Uses

Kaolin appears most often in rinse-off clay masks, where it is commonly combined with bentonite and other absorbent clays, and in facial cleansers and pressed or loose face powders, where its use levels run up to 16% in leave-on face and neck products and up to 15% in powders, according to industry survey data reviewed by the Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel.[^cir] It also appears in other leave-on cosmetics such as lipstick and eye shadow, and in bath soaps and detergents, at lower reported concentrations.[^cir] Separately, kaolin (colloidal) is recognized by the FDA as an over-the-counter skin-protectant active ingredient at 4% to 20%, a drug-monograph use distinct from its role in cosmetic masks and powders.[^cir]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Detoxifies" or "draws out impurities/toxins."** No mechanism by which kaolin removes toxins from skin is established in the regulatory or clinical literature reviewed here; the documented, physically plausible effect is surface-oil absorption from its structure and classification as a cosmetic absorbent, not a removal of "impurities."[^cir]
- **"Purifying" or "deep-cleansing."** The tolerability and use-concentration data describe kaolin as a well-characterized cosmetic absorbent and bulking agent, not as an ingredient shown to alter pore or oil-gland function beyond soaking up oil already on the skin surface.[^cir]
