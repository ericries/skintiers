Efinaconazole is a prescription azole antifungal painted on toenails daily for 48 weeks, approved in the United States in 2014. **Bottom line:** the evidence behind it is unusually strong for a topical nail treatment, two double-blind vehicle-controlled trials of about 1,650 people reviewed by the FDA, and the result those trials produced is modest. Roughly one in six people got a clear nail. The condition itself is covered at [[onychomycosis]].

> **The two numbers on the label that matter most are the ones furthest apart.** Efinaconazole achieved mycologic cure, meaning the fungus was gone on culture and KOH, in "55.2%" and "53.4%" of treated subjects across the two trials. Complete cure, which adds a toenail with no clinical evidence of disease, was "17.8%" and "15.2%".[^label] Killing the organism in about half of patients produced a normal-looking nail in about one in six. A nail grows back slowly and often does not grow back looking normal, which is the part marketing tends to leave out.

## The Rubric

**Clearing toenail onychomycosis**
- **Effect size: modest.** Complete cure was "117" of 656 ("17.8%") versus "7" of 214 on vehicle ("3.3%") in trial 1, and "88" of 580 ("15.2%") versus "11" of 201 ("5.5%") in trial 2.[^label] Those are absolute gains of about 14.5 and 9.7 percentage points over vehicle, so roughly 7 to 10 people need 48 weeks of daily treatment for one additional clear nail. Real, and far from a cure you can count on.
- **Evidence quality: gold-standard.** "two 52-week prospective, multicenter, randomized, double-blind clinical trials in subjects 18 years and older (18 to 70 years of age)", vehicle-controlled, replicated, with results published on an FDA-approved label.[^label] This is a different class of evidence from most things on this site, and the modest result is what good evidence honestly reports.

**A softer endpoint, and why it is reported separately**
- **Effect size: modest.** "Complete or Almost Complete Cure", defined on the label as "≤5% affected target toenail area involved and negative KOH and culture", reached "26.4%" versus "7.0%" and "23.4%" versus "7.5%".[^label] Loosening the bar from 0% to 5% involvement roughly adds another eight points.
- **Evidence quality: gold-standard, same trials.** Worth reading alongside complete cure rather than instead of it; the looser endpoint is the one more likely to be quoted.

**Tolerability**
- **Effect size: not applicable; a harms finding.** Across 1,227 treated and 413 vehicle subjects, reactions reported in at least 1% were ingrown toenail "28 (2.3%)" versus "3 (0.7%)", application site dermatitis "27 (2.2%)" versus "1 (0.2%)", application site vesicles "20 (1.6%)" versus "0 (0.0%)" and application site pain "13 (1.1%)" versus "1 (0.2%)".[^label]
- **Evidence quality: solid.** Low rates, collected inside the same controlled trials. Nothing systemic appears at this frequency on the label.

## The Evidence

**The trials excluded the hard cases.** Subjects had "20% to 50% clinical involvement of the target toenail, without dermatophytomas or lunula (matrix) involvement".[^label] So a nail more than half affected, one with a fungal clump inside the plate, or one infected at the growth matrix was not studied. Those are the nails people most want fixed, and the 17.8% figure does not describe them.

**A claim this page will not make.** Efinaconazole is often described as penetrating the nail plate better than older nail lacquers. The FDA label does not say that. Checked by string search of the full label text, it contains no occurrence of penetration, no mention of ciclopirox, lacquer or amorolfine, and no comparative superiority claim against any other product.[^label] No head-to-head trial against another topical appears on the label either, so nothing here ranks it against [[ciclopirox]]. The one pooled comparison this site has across topical antifungals is on [[otc-antifungals-by-evidence]], and it predates this drug.

**Forty-eight weeks is the treatment, not a detail.** The label instructs applying it "once daily for 48 weeks", covering "the toenail, the toenail folds, toenail bed, hyponychium, and the undersurface of the toenail plate", with complete cure assessed at week 52.[^label] Anyone weighing this against an oral antifungal should do so on the real comparison, which is a year of daily application versus a much shorter oral course, discussed at [[onychomycosis]] and [[terbinafine]].

**It is genuinely approved, not merely cleared.** The DailyMed record lists the marketing category as NDA, the code reserved for an approved new drug application, with initial United States approval in 2014.[^label] That distinction matters more on this site than usual, given how often it gets blurred; see [[fda-approved-vs-cleared-skincare]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Efinaconazole is prescription-only, and the approved indication is toenail onychomycosis caused specifically by *Trichophyton rubrum* and *Trichophyton mentagrophytes*, which means a clinician confirming the organism is part of using it correctly. Thickened or discoloured nails have causes other than fungus.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined from the graded assessment above. No brand or retailer copy was fetched for this page, so there is no advertising wording to quote; everything graded above comes from the FDA-approved label. One claim does circulate widely enough to name, and it is the reason this ingredient is usually presented as an advance: that efinaconazole penetrates the nail plate better than older nail lacquers. The label supports no part of it, containing no mention of penetration, no named competitor and no comparative superiority claim.[^label] The claim may well be true and testable from pharmacokinetic literature; it is simply not established by the document that establishes everything else on this page.
