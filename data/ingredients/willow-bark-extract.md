Willow bark extract is made from the bark of *Salix alba* and contains salicin, which is why cosmetic copy calls it a natural BHA and offers it as the gentle alternative to [[salicylic-acid]]. Five products covered here declare it.

> **The marketed function is the one nobody has tested.** Every study found for this page measured something else: visible ageing, dark circles, or cytokines in a keratinocyte cell line. None measured exfoliation, desquamation, comedone counts or anything else that would show it working the way [[salicylic-acid]], graded **good**, actually works. The evidence that does exist is two uncontrolled before-and-after studies and one cell-culture experiment.

## The Rubric

**Visible signs of ageing (0.5% salicin serum, twice daily, 12 weeks)**
- Effect size: unknown, because the study design cannot produce one. Thirty women aged 35 to 70 used the serum for 12 weeks at a single centre, and the investigator recorded statistically significant improvements in wrinkles, tactile roughness, pore size, radiance and overall appearance by week 1.[^gopaul] Every one of those comparisons is "against baseline".[^gopaul] There is no control group and no vehicle arm in the reported design, so nothing separates the salicin from the serum it was carried in, from 12 weeks of twice-daily moisturising, or from an unblinded grader's expectations.
- Evidence quality: preliminary. The paper is also unusually candid about where its premise came from: "Based on unpublished in-house comprehensive consumer clinical studies, it is believed salicin may have anti-aging capabilities when applied topically to human skin."[^gopaul] It further notes that salicin "has been researched as a potent anti-inflammatory agent when taken orally", which is a different route from the one being sold.[^gopaul]

**Dark circles (2% bark extract cream, 8 weeks)**
- Effect size: unknown, same reason. A 2023 study applied a cream containing 2% Salix alba bark extract to 29 subjects for 8 weeks and reported improvement in "the parameters measuring dark circles, skin microcirculation and elasticity".[^jeon] The abstract describes the clinical portion as subjects applying the cream, with no comparator named, so this is again a before-and-after.
- Evidence quality: preliminary. Most of the paper is in vitro: hyaluronic acid synthesis in cultured fibroblasts and endothelial permeability in a cell line.[^jeon]

**Acne**
- Effect size: not established in humans. The acne case rests on a 2018 experiment in HaCaT cells, an immortalised keratinocyte line, where white willow bark changed growth factor release, cytokine production and a transcription factor after the cells were stressed with bacterial lipopolysaccharide.[^bassino] The authors put it as "suggesting their potential role for the prevention of acne vulgaris".[^bassino]
- Evidence quality: anecdotal for the skin claim. Cells in a dish are not a person with acne, and this experiment tested willow bark alongside 1,2-decanediol rather than isolating it in every condition.[^bassino]

**Overall: tier weak.** Two small uncontrolled human studies at two different concentrations for two different claims, plus one cell-culture study, and no test at all of the exfoliating function the ingredient is sold for.

## The Evidence

What would move this grade is simple and absent: a vehicle-controlled trial of willow bark extract at a stated concentration, measuring an exfoliation endpoint. Until then the honest reading is that willow bark is a plausible anti-inflammatory botanical with thin human data, sold on a chemical association with an acid that has its own page and its own much better evidence.

Two practical points follow for reading a label. Products rarely state how much is present, and when a number does appear it tends to come from a retailer rather than the brand: [[cosrx-aha-bha-clarifying-treatment-toner]] is a case where a retailer listing says the product is "Made with 10% white willow bark and 10% of Apple Water" while the brand's own page discloses no percentage at all.[^cosrx] And the extract is frequently a minor ingredient in a formula whose work is being done by something else, as on [[paulas-choice-clinical-1-retinol-treatment]] and [[lilyana-naturals-retinol-cream]], where the graded active is [[retinol]].

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted terms are industry usage, **not** endorsed here.

- **"Natural BHA".** Beta hydroxy acid is a chemical class, and the member with the evidence is salicylic acid. No study found for this page tested willow bark as a keratolytic or exfoliant, so the label borrows the function of a different molecule.
- **A gentler alternative to salicylic acid.** Gentler is plausible, since nothing here reported a tolerability problem, but gentler than an active you are not receiving is not a benefit. The comparison also has no head-to-head behind it.
- **Anti-inflammatory, on the strength of salicin.** The anti-inflammatory research the 2010 paper points to is oral.[^gopaul]
