Witch hazel is one of the few botanicals in skincare that is also a recognised over-the-counter drug active in the United States, which makes it unusual on this shelf. The catch is that the use it is recognised for is not the use it is sold for.

> **It is an astringent for bites, cuts and scrapes.** Over a hundred current US over-the-counter drug labels list witch hazel as an active ingredient with the Purpose line "Astringent", typically at 86%, and the Uses line on a standalone bottle reads "for relief of minor skin irritations due to: insect bites minor cuts minor scrapes".[^otc] Nothing in that indication mentions pores, oiliness or acne, which is what the cosmetic versions are marketed for. The same labels declare "alcohol 14% by volume" as the inactive ingredient.[^otc]

## The Rubric

**Astringent use on minor irritation (the recognised one)**
- Effect size: not quantified anywhere this page could find, but the regulatory recognition is real and is more than most botanicals carry. The monograph purpose is astringent and the stated uses are insect bites, minor cuts and minor scrapes.[^otc]
- Evidence quality: this is a regulatory classification rather than a trial result, so it should not be read as a measured effect. No efficacy figure appears on the labels.

**Acne, pores and oil control (what it is actually sold for)**
- Effect size: not established in people. The most directly relevant paper tested a glycolic extract of *Hamamelis virginiana* bark against *Cutibacterium acnes*-induced inflammation in **HaCaT keratinocytes**, a cell line, and reported that the extract "inhibited" that release, at a half-maximal inhibitory concentration the abstract gives as 136.90 micrograms per millilitre.[^piazza] Its own framing is the useful part: witch hazel is "a plant widely used for skin inflammatory conditions, with some preliminary anti-inflammatory evidence on the skin, but lacking data on acne conditions".[^piazza]
- Evidence quality: anecdotal for the skin claim. Cells in a dish, and the authors say the acne data are missing.

**Inflammatory skin disease generally**
- Effect size: none demonstrated. A systematic review and meta-analysis in *JAAD International* covering complementary and alternative treatments for acne, atopic dermatitis and psoriasis concluded "there is insufficient evidence to support the efficacy and the recommendation of CAM for acne, AD, and psoriasis".[^jaad] Its pooled estimates for acne lesion count crossed zero in both comparisons.[^jaad]
- Evidence quality: the review is the right design and the answer is negative at the category level.

**The one randomised trial found, and it is not about faces**
- A 2026 trial in 60 infants compared topical breast milk against a diaper cream containing *Hamamelis virginiana* at 12 mg per 100 g for diaper dermatitis. Breast milk produced the greater improvement, and the authors note "baseline severity differences limit direct comparison".[^diaper] Witch hazel was the comparator arm, at a low concentration, in a condition unrelated to its cosmetic positioning.

**Overall: tier weak.** A genuine regulatory classification as an astringent, no human efficacy data for the uses it is marketed for, and a category-level meta-analysis that found insufficient evidence.

## The Evidence

The gap worth naming is between the two lists above. Regulatory recognition and clinical evidence are different things, and witch hazel has the first without the second. A shopper who reads "FDA-recognised active" and infers that the pore and oil claims have been tested has made a reasonable mistake.

Two practical consequences for reading a label. The alcohol content is declared on the drug versions and is often the reason the skin feels tighter immediately, which is easy to read as an effect. And in cosmetics the ingredient usually appears as *Hamamelis virginiana* water or extract well down the list, as on [[dr-dennis-gross-alpha-beta-universal-daily-peel]] and [[pixi-glow-tonic]], where the graded actives are the acids rather than this.

## Common Marketing Claims

> Industry usage, **not** endorsed here.

- **Shrinks or minimises pores.** No trial supports it, and the word astringent in the monograph refers to the bites-and-scrapes indication rather than to pore appearance.
- **Controls oil.** The same absence. An immediate tightening sensation on alcohol-containing versions is not sebum control.
- **A gentle, natural toner for sensitive skin.** The standalone drug product is 14% alcohol by volume.[^otc] Whether that matters for a given person is individual, but gentle is doing unearned work; see [[contact-dermatitis]] for how botanical sensitivity presents.
- **Anti-acne, on the strength of laboratory work.** The keratinocyte study is real and its authors say acne data are lacking.[^piazza]
