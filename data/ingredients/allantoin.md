Allantoin is a small heterocyclic compound found in comfrey (Symphytum officinale) root and now mostly made synthetically, formulated into cosmetics and OTC drug products typically between 0.5% and 2%.[^cir][^cfr] It has US regulatory recognition as a skin-protectant active ingredient at that same range,[^cfr] and it is common in cosmetic formulas as a supporting "skin-conditioning agent" rather than a headline active, turning up in 1,376 cosmetic products reviewed by the Cosmetic Ingredient Review (CIR) at concentrations up to 2%.[^cir] The evidence that allantoin itself, isolated from the plant material it is traditionally drawn from, drives a soothing effect is thinner than its ubiquity suggests.

> Allantoin has US regulatory standing as an OTC skin protectant and decades of cosmetic use at 0.5% to 2%, but the one controlled study identified that isolates pure allantoin's own anti-irritant effect in human skin found it performed worse than a comfrey-root extract standardized to the same allantoin content, cutting against the idea that allantoin alone accounts for comfrey's traditional soothing reputation.[^cfr][^cir][^savic]

## The Rubric

**Regulatory status and formulation use (0.5% to 2%)**
Well-established as an approved, widely used ingredient at a defined concentration range; this is a regulatory and usage fact, not a claim that it works. The FDA's OTC drug monograph lists "Allantoin, 0.5 to 2 percent" as an active ingredient for skin-protectant products.[^cfr] The CIR Expert Panel's safety review, covering allantoin and several allantoin salts and complexes, found them used in 1,376 cosmetic formulations at concentrations up to 2%, functioning as skin-conditioning agents, and concluded the safety data were "sufficient to support the safety of allantoin and the allantoin complexes in product categories and at concentrations reviewed."[^cir] Neither review evaluates whether allantoin visibly soothes skin; both establish only that it is approved and considered safe at these levels.

**Soothing / anti-irritant effect from isolated allantoin (topical)**
Effect size: limited, and outperformed by the plant extract allantoin is traditionally drawn from. In a controlled comparison, pure allantoin applied to artificially irritated skin of healthy volunteers scored worse on anti-irritant potential, measured by electrical capacitance, erythema index, and transepidermal water loss, than an aqueous comfrey-root extract standardized to the same allantoin content; the authors concluded the biological activity of the comfrey extract "cannot be attributed only to allantoin but is also likely the result of the interaction of different compounds" in the whole extract.[^savic] In a companion in vitro assay on epithelial (MDCK) and fibroblast (L929) cell lines, pure allantoin at 40 to 100 µg/mL showed only a "mild inhibitory effect" on proliferation, more pronounced on the epithelial line, so the same study found no proliferative or wound-healing signal from allantoin alone either.[^savic]

Evidence quality: preliminary and singular. This is the one identified study to isolate pure allantoin's own biological activity against a control (the comfrey extract standardized to it), rather than testing allantoin as one ingredient among several in a finished product; it used a small human irritation panel plus two cell lines, not a large placebo-controlled trial, and it found allantoin alone the weaker performer of the two.[^savic] Allantoin nonetheless remains a common supporting ingredient in barrier and moisturizing formulas; see [[moisturizing]] for the general evidence on barrier-repair moisturizers.

## The Evidence

**Regulatory and safety record.** The FDA's OTC skin-protectant monograph states: "(a) Allantoin, 0.5 to 2 percent."[^cfr] The CIR Expert Panel's 2010 final safety assessment, covering allantoin and its ascorbate, biotin, galacturonic acid, glycyrrhetinic acid, panthenol, and polygalacturonic acid complexes, reported concentration-of-use data up to 2% across 1,376 cosmetic product formulations and concluded the existing safety test data were sufficient to support the ingredients' safety in the categories and concentrations reviewed.[^cir] Neither source reports an efficacy trial; both are safety and regulatory-status reviews.

**Anti-irritant and cell-proliferation study.** Savić and colleagues compared pure allantoin (PA) with an aqueous extract of comfrey root (AECR) standardized to the same allantoin content.[^savic] Cell viability and proliferation of MDCK (epithelial) and L929 (fibroblast) lines were measured by MTT assay; pure allantoin showed a "mild inhibitory effect on proliferation of both cell lines at concentrations 40 and 100 µg/ml, but more pronounced on MDCK cells," while the comfrey extract was "significantly stimulatory for L929 but inhibitory for MDCK cells" at concentrations above 40 µg/mL. In the human arm, creams and gels containing PA or AECR were applied to artificially irritated skin of young healthy volunteers, with anti-irritant potential assessed by electrical capacitance, erythema index, and transepidermal water loss at 3 and 7 days; the paper reports that "pharmaceutical preparations that contained AECR showed better anti-irritant potential compared with PA," and that creams outperformed gels containing the same components on hydration and erythema index. The authors' conclusion: comfrey root's biological activity "cannot be attributed only to allantoin but is also likely the result of the interaction of different compounds present in AECR."[^savic]

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Soothes and calms irritated skin."** The one study to isolate pure allantoin's anti-irritant effect against a comparator found it the weaker of the two tested materials, not the stronger one; the better-performing material was a whole comfrey-root extract, not allantoin alone.[^savic]
- **"Heals and repairs skin."** No cited study shows isolated allantoin accelerating wound healing in human skin; the in vitro cell-proliferation data available show a mild inhibitory, not stimulatory, effect on two cell lines.[^savic]
