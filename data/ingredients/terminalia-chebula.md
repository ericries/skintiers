Terminalia chebula (Haritaki) is the dried fruit of a tree used in Ayurvedic medicine, which one cosmetic-chemistry study notes "is highly valued in medicine and cosmetics," added to some serums and creams as a tannin-rich botanical antioxidant.[^liang] In the lab it is a strong free-radical scavenger and inhibits the enzymes tyrosinase and elastase, which is why formulators pitch it for brightening and anti-aging; that is the mechanistic case, not proof of a visible result on skin.[^liang]

> Terminalia chebula's antioxidant activity and its in-vitro inhibition of tyrosinase (a pigment enzyme) and elastase (a collagen-and-elastin enzyme) are real and repeatable in test-tube assays, and an animal study supports an anti-inflammatory, antioxidant effect in mouse skin. But no controlled human trial tests a topical Terminalia chebula extract for a measurable change in wrinkles, pigmentation, or firmness on real skin, so the cosmetic benefit is plausible-in-theory and unproven in people. It grades weak.[^liang][^majed]

## The Rubric

**Antioxidant, brightening, and anti-aging (topical, cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: plausible from lab activity, but not measured on human skin.** A biological-activity-guided study found a 30% ethanol extract of Terminalia chebula had "good antioxidant capacity and potent inhibitory activity against tyrosinase and elastase," and identified 30 compounds in the active fraction, "including 11 gallotannins, 14 ellagitannins, and 5 other compounds."[^liang] Tyrosinase inhibition is the mechanism behind "brightening" claims and elastase inhibition behind "anti-aging" claims, but both were measured in enzyme and radical-scavenging assays, not on skin.
- **Evidence quality: thin.** No randomized or otherwise controlled human trial of a topical Terminalia chebula extract for a visible skin outcome is available; the supporting data are in-vitro assays and an animal model, a step removed from a cosmetic result a person would see. Unlike [[vitamin-c]] and [[niacinamide]], which have controlled human trials behind their brightening and antioxidant claims, Terminalia chebula has none.[^liang][^majed]

## How It Works

The proposed mechanism is antioxidant: Terminalia chebula is rich in hydrolyzable tannins (gallotannins and ellagitannins) that scavenge free radicals, which in turn is offered as the basis for protecting skin from oxidative stress.[^liang] The brightening rationale is tyrosinase inhibition (tyrosinase is the rate-limiting enzyme in melanin production) and the anti-aging rationale is elastase inhibition (elastase degrades elastin), both demonstrated for the extract in enzyme assays.[^liang] Neither study tested whether the extract penetrates intact skin or reproduces these enzyme effects in living tissue.

## The Evidence

No controlled human topical trial establishes a visible skin benefit for Terminalia chebula; the evidence is in-vitro and animal only. In the biological-activity-guided study, the extract was screened across DPPH, ABTS, hydroxyl, and superoxide-anion radical-scavenging assays, and its active 30% ethanol fraction showed antioxidant capacity plus inhibition of tyrosinase and elastase, with mass spectrometry identifying 30 compounds in that fraction, predominantly hydrolyzable tannins (11 gallotannins and 14 ellagitannins).[^liang] The nearest in-skin data is an animal study: in Swiss albino mice, Terminalia chebula attenuated oxidative stress and inflammation in a chemically-induced (DMBA/croton oil) skin model.[^majed] Together these make a mechanistic case but do not measure wrinkles, pigmentation, or firmness on real human skin over time, so the grade is weak: a plausible antioxidant botanical whose cosmetic claims outrun its human evidence.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Brightening" / "evens skin tone."** Rests on in-vitro tyrosinase inhibition by the extract, not on a human trial showing reduced pigmentation.[^liang]
- **"Anti-aging" / "firming."** Rests on in-vitro elastase inhibition and antioxidant activity, not on a human trial showing fewer wrinkles or measurably firmer skin.[^liang]
- **"Powerful antioxidant."** This is the best-supported claim mechanistically (strong radical-scavenging in multiple assays), but antioxidant capacity in a test tube is not itself a demonstrated skin outcome.[^liang]
