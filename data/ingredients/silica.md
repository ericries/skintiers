Silica (INCI Silica; silicon dioxide) is a mineral powder used in cosmetics as an absorbent, bulking agent, anticaking agent, and opacifying agent, most often in face powders, foundations, and other leave-on makeup, as well as mattifying moisturizers such as [[la-roche-posay-effaclar-mat]], where it soaks up surface oil and gives a matte, soft-focus finish.[^cir] The forms used on skin, amorphous silica, hydrated silica, and silica silylate (silica with a silicone-treated surface), are chemically distinct from crystalline silica (quartz), a recognized inhalation hazard in raw mineral-dust form that a Cosmetic Ingredient Review safety assessment explicitly excludes from its review of the cosmetic ingredient.[^cir][^iarc][^silylate] Silica's role in skincare is essentially cosmetic. It absorbs oil already on the skin surface and scatters light for a blurred look, rather than changing how much oil the skin produces, a distinction covered for oily skin generally on [[oily-skin]].

> Silica is an inert mineral powder that absorbs surface oil and produces a matte, soft-focus cosmetic finish. It does not reduce sebum production and treats no medical condition. Its case rests on a safety assessment finding cosmetic-grade amorphous silica non-irritating and well tolerated on skin, and on a laboratory comparison showing it among the more porous, oil-absorbent powders used in makeup and skincare formulas.[^cir][^bamford]

## The Rubric

Grades below are relative and name comparators. They cover silica's documented cosmetic function and topical safety, not a health benefit, since none is established for this use.

**Oil absorption and matte, soft-focus finish (cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: notable, as a formulation agent.** A comparative laboratory study in the International Journal of Cosmetic Science found silica has "high surface area and pore volume" among common cosmetic powders (291 square meters per gram; 0.58 cubic centimeters per gram), where "other common powder ingredients exhibit very small surface area and are non-porous."[^bamford] In an oil-uptake test, a newer high-capacity mineral absorbent "absorbed more than 110% of its weight, which is slightly more than silica," with the remaining powders tested "less potent absorbers," placing silica near the top of common cosmetic powders for oil capacity.[^bamford] That physical uptake of surface oil is what produces the matte look and soft-focus blur in the products where silica is common; a Cosmetic Ingredient Review safety assessment reports silica used at up to 82% in face and neck products and up to 66% in face powders.[^cir] The effect is on oil already on the skin, not on the glands that make it, a distinction detailed for oily skin generally on [[oily-skin]].
- **Evidence quality: solid for the physical mechanism, cosmetic in nature.** The oil-absorption comparison is a peer-reviewed laboratory study,[^bamford] and the use-level figures come from a regulatory safety assessment drawing on FDA registration and industry survey data.[^cir] Neither measures a change in skin biology.

**Topical tolerability (cosmetic-grade, amorphous forms)**
- **Effect size: notable (well tolerated).** Dermal irritation testing summarized in the Cosmetic Ingredient Review safety assessment found "very slight to no irritation" in rabbits given hydrated silica (up to 50% in olive oil) and silica (up to 12% in methyl ethyl cellulose), and human repeat-insult patch tests found silica (21.74% in formulation) and hydrated silica (up to 45%) "not sensitizing."[^cir] Silica silylate and related silicone-treated silicas were separately found "safe as used when formulated and delivered in the final product not to be irritating or sensitizing to the respiratory tract."[^silylate]
- **Evidence quality: solid.** These are the Expert Panel's own animal and human patch-test data, reviewed against the wider published and unpublished toxicology literature; the Panel concluded that synthetically manufactured amorphous silica and hydrated silica "are safe in the present practices of use and concentration when formulated to be non-irritating."[^cir]

## The Evidence

Silica is a solid inorganic oxide built from silicon-oxygen tetrahedra. The amorphous forms used in cosmetics, listed in the international ingredient dictionary as Silica and Hydrated Silica, have "an irregular tetrahedral pattern," while crystalline silica is "polymorphic, where each variety has a characteristic regular 3-dimensional arrangement of the tetrahedral" and shows "a well-defined x-ray diffraction pattern, whereas amorphous forms of Silica do not," a Cosmetic Ingredient Review safety assessment explains.[^cir] That structural difference is why the two are reviewed separately: the assessment states that "the current assessment is exclusive to amorphous Silica and Hydrated Silica when manufactured via synthetic methods," and does not cover crystalline silica.[^cir] Crystalline silica, in the form of quartz or cristobalite dust, is a recognized inhalation hazard: the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies it as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1), based on lung cancer among workers with long-term exposure to the dust in mining, quarrying, foundry work, sandblasting, and similar occupations, from inhaled dust, not from topical or cosmetic contact.[^iarc] The amorphous grade used in cosmetics carries a different classification: the Cosmetic Ingredient Review assessment reports that the International Agency for Research on Cancer "concluded that amorphous Silica is not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans based on inadequate evidence in humans and inadequate evidence of increased tumors in animals."[^cir]

On the cosmetic mechanism, the comparative powder study found silica's porous structure gives it an oil-absorption capacity close to the best-performing material tested, though it took "almost an hour" for a surface oil drop to fully absorb into a silica powder bed, against "less than 5 min" for the higher-capacity comparator, while silica still produced an immediate mattifying effect on application.[^bamford] The same paper notes that silica's light-scattering particles are also the ones most often responsible for the pale "flashback" cast some mattifying powders show in flash photography, and that some brands have responded by seeking silica-free finishing powders.[^bamford] Cosmetic regulators list silica's functions as an abrasive, absorbent, anticaking agent, bulking agent, and opacifying agent, and industry survey data cited in the safety assessment show it used at up to 82% in face and neck products, up to 50% in mascaras and lipsticks, and up to 66% in face powders, mostly in leave-on makeup.[^cir]

On safety, the dermal irritation and human patch-test data described above found silica and hydrated silica well tolerated on skin at the concentrations tested,[^cir] and the Expert Panel concluded the two ingredients are safe as currently used when the finished product is formulated to be non-irritating.[^cir]

## Common Marketing Claims

Treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Controls oil production" or "regulates sebum."** The oil-absorption data above describe surface oil already on the skin; the safety assessment and the comparative powder study describe silica as an absorbent and opacifying agent, not one that changes glandular output.[^cir][^bamford] The difference between absorbing surface oil and reducing how much the skin produces is covered on [[oily-skin]].
