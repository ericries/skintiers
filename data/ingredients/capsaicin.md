Capsaicin is the pungent compound of chili peppers and an agonist at TRPV1, a cation channel "widely expressed in multiple skin cell types, including sensory nerve fibers, keratinocytes, immune cells and fibroblasts".[^trpv1] On this site it matters less as something you put on skin for an appearance benefit than as the reagent used to provoke stinging in sensitive-skin research.

> **It is on the site mainly as a measuring instrument.** Sensitive-skin studies use the capsaicin pain test the way a stress test uses a treadmill: to provoke the symptom under controlled conditions. How well that instrument is validated is the subject of [[sensitive-skin-tests-by-what-validation-shows]] and is not repeated here. This page covers the molecule.

## The Rubric

**Any visible skin benefit**
- **Effect size: none established.** No cosmetic skin outcome is claimed for capsaicin on this site, and its FDA-recognised topical role is not a skin one. Across openFDA's label index, topical capsaicin products are over-the-counter EXTERNAL ANALGESICS whose stated use is to temporarily relieve "minor aches and pains of muscles and joints".[^openfda] That is a claim about muscles and joints, not about skin appearance.
- **Evidence quality: anecdotal for skin appearance.** Graded `weak` for the same reason as the functional ingredients on this site: having a real pharmacology is not the same as having a skin benefit.

**What it actually does on contact**
- **Effect size: a genuine and well-characterised mechanism, pointed away from cosmesis.** TRPV1 "can be activated by noxious heat, acidic conditions, capsaicin and a range of endogenous inflammatory mediators", and on activation "mediates Ca2+ influx, leading to neuropeptide release and the initiation of neurogenic inflammation".[^trpv1] Neurogenic inflammation is the burning and flushing that the sensitive-skin tests are designed to elicit, which is exactly why capsaicin makes a useful provocation.
- **Evidence quality: solid for the mechanism.** The review cited is a narrative review of TRPV1 in skin, not a trial of capsaicin as a cosmetic ingredient.

**The part worth watching, and not overstating**
- **Effect size: unknown.** The same review reports that TRPV1 "contributes to several aspects of cutaneous homeostasis, including epidermal barrier repair, wound healing and hair follicle biology", that aberrant TRPV1 has been "closely associated with" psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, rosacea, skin aging and melanoma, and that strategies TARGETING TRPV1 have "shown promising potential".[^trpv1] Note the direction: the therapeutic interest is in blocking or desensitising this channel, not in agonising it with capsaicin for cosmetic ends.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, and about the target rather than this ingredient.** Promising potential in a review is a research agenda. Nothing here supports putting capsaicin on the face.

## The Evidence

Nothing cited here tests capsaicin as a skincare ingredient. The two roles it genuinely has are an over-the-counter analgesic for muscle and joint pain, which is outside this site's scope, and a laboratory provocation agent, which is inside it only because the provocations appear in the trials this site reads.

Its one appearance in the product catalogue is as the thing being defended against rather than an active: [[la-roche-posay-rosaliac-ar-intense-serum]] records a manufacturer-referenced test in which the product reduced capsaicin-induced stinging. That is capsaicin used as the challenge, and the claim belongs to that product's page.

**A practical note.** Capsaicin creams sold for aches and pains are not facial skincare, and the burning they produce on broken, thin or already-reactive skin is the point of the molecule rather than a side effect. General guidance for reactive skin is on [[sensitive-skin]].

## Common Marketing Claims

> None are quoted here, because this ingredient is not marketed on this site's shelf.

Where capsaicin or capsicum extract does appear in cosmetic marketing, it is usually sold on warming or circulation-boosting language. No source cited on this page supports a cosmetic appearance claim for it, and the one measurable thing capsaicin reliably does to skin, provoke neurogenic inflammation, is the opposite of what a sensitive-skin product is trying to achieve.
