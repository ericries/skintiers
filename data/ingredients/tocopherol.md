Tocopherol (INCI Tocopherol; vitamin E) is a fat-soluble antioxidant used in skincare formulations. Keen and Hassan describe vitamin E as "an important fat-soluble antioxidant" that functions as a "free-radical scavenger," with alpha-tocopherol being "the most abundant vitamin E derivative in human tissues and sera."[^1] Its documented skincare-relevant function is antioxidant; the strength of clinical evidence for downstream benefits is limited.

## The Rubric

Grades below are relative and name comparators. They describe tocopherol's antioxidant function and the evidence for its skincare effects, not proof that any finished product works.

**Antioxidant / photoprotection**
- **Effect size: mechanism-supported, clinical benefit uncertain.** Keen and Hassan state that "experimental studies suggest that vitamin E has antitumorigenic and photoprotective properties," while noting that "multiple human studies have shown no effects of vitamin E on the prevention or development of skin cancers."[^1] Podhaisky and Wohlrab note the "photoprotective effect of vitamin E is attributed to its radical scavenger capacity."[^2]
- **Evidence quality: mechanism established, controlled clinical evidence sparse.** Keen and Hassan conclude that "there is a paucity of controlled clinical studies providing a rationale for well-defined dosages and clinical indications of vitamin E usage in dermatological practice."[^1]

**Scars and burns**
- **Effect size: none demonstrated.** Keen and Hassan report that "studies looking at the efficacy of vitamin E in the treatment of burns and scars have been disappointing."[^1]
- **Evidence quality: negative.** The available topical scar and burn studies did not show benefit.[^1]

## The Evidence

Tocopherol's skincare rationale is antioxidant. Keen and Hassan describe it as a fat-soluble antioxidant and free-radical scavenger, and Podhaisky and Wohlrab note that its photoprotective effect is usually attributed to that radical-scavenging capacity.[^1][^2] The gap is between mechanism and clinical outcome. Keen and Hassan state that "experimental studies suggest that vitamin E has antitumorigenic and photoprotective properties," but that "multiple human studies have shown no effects of vitamin E on the prevention or development of skin cancers," and that "studies looking at the efficacy of vitamin E in the treatment of burns and scars have been disappointing."[^1] Their overall assessment is that controlled clinical evidence for specific dermatological indications is scarce.[^1] The antioxidant function is well described; the evidence that a tocopherol-containing product delivers a measurable clinical benefit is limited.
