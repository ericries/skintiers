Fluconazole is an oral triazole antifungal, dispensed only by prescription and taken systemically rather than applied to the skin, described on its own FDA label as "the first of a new subclass of synthetic triazole antifungal agents."[^dailymed] Its FDA-approved indications are Candida infections (vaginal, oropharyngeal, and esophageal candidiasis, and systemic forms including candidemia) and cryptococcal meningitis, a separate fungal infection, not any skin condition.[^dailymed] Dermatologists also prescribe it off-label for a narrow set of fungal skin conditions, most notably Malassezia folliculitis ("fungal acne"), where a review reports "a clinical effect of 80%."[^saunte]

> **Bottom line:** Fluconazole clears a real but narrow slice of fungal skin disease, Malassezia folliculitis chief among them, when taken as a prescribed systemic course. None of its skin uses are FDA-approved indications, the supporting evidence is a review's reported figure rather than a randomized trial, and it is a systemic drug with hepatotoxicity, QT-prolongation, and drug-interaction warnings on its label, so its dermatologic use is a prescriber-managed decision, not a self-directed one.[^dailymed][^saunte]

## The Rubric

**Malassezia folliculitis ("fungal acne"), oral therapy for cases that do not clear topically.**
- **Effect size: notable, and directly comparable to itraconazole.** The same 2020 review of Malassezia-associated skin diseases that supports [[itraconazole]]'s use reports "fluconazole 100-200 mg daily for 1-4 weeks with a clinical effect of 80%," at the same dose range and duration as itraconazole, whose reported effect spans "69-100%."[^saunte] As reported on [[fungal-acne]], fluconazole is regularly weighed against itraconazole specifically because of its side effect profile rather than a larger effect: itraconazole has more supporting evidence for efficacy, but fluconazole is often preferred for its safety profile.[^statpearls]
- **Evidence quality: thin, and narrower than itraconazole's.** The 80% figure is a single number from a narrative review, not a pooled statistic from a systematic review or a randomized trial with a confidence interval. Itraconazole's Malassezia folliculitis evidence is reported as a range across several small studies; fluconazole's is a single point estimate, which is a thinner evidence trail even though the direction is consistent.

**Pityriasis (tinea) versicolor.**
- **Effect size: not quantified for fluconazole specifically.** Fluconazole is a recognized off-label oral option for widespread or recalcitrant pityriasis versicolor alongside itraconazole, but the same review's dedicated cure-rate statement is for itraconazole only ("oral itraconazole is also very effective in cases of pityriasis versicolor, 100 mg daily for 10 days"), and it does not report an equivalent number for fluconazole.[^saunte]
- **Evidence quality: thin.** Without a fluconazole-specific figure in the reviewed literature, its use for pityriasis versicolor rests on its general standing as an antifungal active against Malassezia rather than a dedicated cure-rate result.

Fluconazole is not a treatment for ordinary [[acne]]; like [[itraconazole]] and [[ketoconazole]], it targets yeast rather than the bacterial, comedone-driven process behind acne vulgaris. Malassezia folliculitis traces to the same yeast genus implicated in [[seborrheic-dermatitis]], and topical antifungal treatment is the usual first step before a systemic course like fluconazole is considered.[^saunte]

## The Evidence

Fluconazole's skin uses rest on a single 2020 review of Malassezia-associated skin diseases, which reports the 80% Malassezia folliculitis figure alongside itraconazole's comparable range, and on the StatPearls folliculitis chapter, which frames the choice between the two drugs as a side effect trade-off rather than an efficacy one.[^saunte][^statpearls] Neither source is a randomized trial with a confidence interval; both are narrative syntheses of the wider treatment literature. That is a thinner evidentiary base than fluconazole's FDA-approved Candida and cryptococcal indications, which rest on the controlled trials behind its original approval, not the review literature cited here.[^dailymed]

## Safety

Fluconazole is a systemic drug, not a topical, and its risk profile reflects that. The label states it "has been associated with rare cases of serious hepatic toxicity, including fatalities primarily in patients with serious underlying medical conditions," and instructs that patients who develop abnormal liver function tests during treatment "should be monitored for the development of more severe hepatic injury."[^dailymed] It also carries a QT-prolongation warning: "some azoles, including fluconazole, have been associated with prolongation of the QT interval on the electrocardiogram," with post-marketing reports of "QT prolongation and torsade de pointes in patients taking fluconazole."[^dailymed] The label identifies fluconazole as "a moderate CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 inhibitor" and "also a strong inhibitor of CYP2C19," the mechanism behind a long list of drug interactions that a prescriber screens for before and during treatment.[^dailymed] In pregnancy, the label describes "a pattern of distinct congenital anomalies in infants exposed in utero to high dose maternal fluconazole (400 to 800 mg/day) during most or all of the first trimester," including "brachycephaly, abnormal facies, abnormal calvarial development, cleft palate, femoral bowing, thin ribs and long bones, arthrogryposis, and congenital heart disease."[^dailymed] None of this rules out a prescriber-managed course of treatment, but it places fluconazole in a different risk category than any topical antifungal covered on this site.
