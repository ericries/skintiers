Lactobionic acid is a bionic acid, a polyhydroxy acid (PHA) built by bonding a sugar (galactose) onto gluconic acid, which makes it a larger molecule than its closest cousin [[gluconolactone-pha]], consistent with the PHA-class premise that larger molecules penetrate skin more slowly and so exfoliate with less irritation.[^cardoso][^grimes] It is marketed as a humectant, an antioxidant, and a milder alternative to classic AHAs like [[glycolic-acid]] and [[lactic-acid]]; the best controlled evidence supports the hydration and tolerability claims, while the antioxidant and metalloproteinase-inhibition claims rest on an in vitro assay and a narrative review from commercially affiliated authors, not a controlled skin trial.

> A head-to-head trial in 77 subjects found lactobionic acid produced "better skin performance" than glycolic acid, "particularly the lack of both skin irritation and skin barrier impairment," and small split-face trials consistently found it raised skin hydration at concentrations from 6% to 30%.[^tasic2010][^algiert2018] Its antioxidant activity has been shown in a free-radical-scavenging lab assay, not in a controlled human skin trial.[^tasic2012]

## The Rubric

**Hydration and moisturizing (topical, 6 percent to 30 percent, corneometry)**
- **Effect size: modest.** A split-face trial in 10 subjects applying 10% lactobionic acid to one side of the face and 30% to the other for eight weekly treatments found "a statistically significant improvement in hydration level after 8 treatments for all measuring points and both concentrations," with "the difference of the skin hydration level between 10% LA and 30% LA could not be determined."[^algiert2018] A separate split-face trial in 20 subjects comparing a 20% lactobionic acid peel against the same peel combined with aluminum-oxide microdermabrasion found "a higher hydration level" on the combined side and a statistically significant drop in transepidermal water loss across sessions on both sides.[^algiert2019] An earlier trial testing 6% lactobionic acid in an alkylpolyglucoside emulsion against glycolic acid in 77 subjects reported better skin performance for lactobionic acid on the same bioengineering measures.[^tasic2010] "Modest; several small trials at concentrations from 6% to 30% found lactobionic acid raised measured skin hydration, with no added benefit from higher concentrations in the one trial that compared them directly."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Four trials, two research groups, all unblinded split-face or single-emulsion designs with 10 to 77 subjects; none blinded or placebo-controlled.[^tasic2010][^algiert2018][^algiert2019][^tasic2012]

**Tolerability versus glycolic acid, and skin-surface pH (topical, controlled comparison and safety study)**
- **Effect size: modest, and better than glycolic acid on this comparison.** In the same 77-subject trial, lactobionic acid-containing samples "produced better skin performance when compared with corresponding GA-containing ones, particularly the lack of both skin irritation and skin barrier impairment," measured by erythema, melanin index, transepidermal water loss, capacitance, and pH over two to four weeks.[^tasic2010] A separate in vitro and in vivo safety study of a 10% lactobionic acid emulsion found it "significantly reduced" skin surface pH after a single application while showing "a satisfactory safety profile" on cytotoxicity and 24-hour occlusive irritation testing.[^tasic2019] "Modest; in one controlled comparison lactobionic acid produced less irritation and barrier disruption than glycolic acid at the concentrations tested, and a separate safety study found it lowered skin pH without triggering measured irritation."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One head-to-head trial (n=77) and one safety study (design and subject count not stated in the abstract) from the same research group, neither blinded against a comparator arm beyond glycolic acid and vehicle.[^tasic2010][^tasic2019]

**Antioxidant activity and matrix-metalloproteinase inhibition**
- **Effect size: unclear for skin.** Lactobionic acid showed "satisfying antioxidant activity" in a DPPH free-radical-scavenging and lipid-peroxidation-inhibition assay run in solution and in an emulsion, benchmarked against alpha-tocopherol.[^tasic2012] A narrative review co-authored by scientists at NeoStrata, a commercial developer of hydroxy acids, states that bionic acids "provide additional antioxidant/chelation, barrier strengthening, and moisturizing effects" and that they "inhibit matrix metalloproteinase enzymes in skin, providing a preventative antiaging benefit," without citing a specific controlled trial for that claim in the abstract.[^green2009] No controlled human trial demonstrates an antioxidant or anti-aging effect of lactobionic acid on skin. "Unclear for skin; the antioxidant signal comes from a lab free-radical assay, and the chelation and MMP-inhibition claims come from a review by authors affiliated with a hydroxy-acid manufacturer, not a controlled skin trial."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One in vitro assay and one narrative review with a commercial conflict of interest; no clinical trial measuring an antioxidant or antiaging endpoint on skin.[^tasic2012][^green2009]

## What We Actually Know

Lactobionic acid is a bionic acid, described in a food-chemistry review as gluconic acid "bonded to a galactose," which makes it structurally larger than [[gluconolactone-pha]], a PHA built from gluconic acid alone.[^cardoso] A review tracing the hydroxy-acid class back to Van Scott and Yu's original AHA work groups gluconolactone and lactobionic acid together as PHAs that "do not cause the sensory irritation responses that can limit the use of classical AHAs" while adding "humectant and moisturization properties."[^grimes] The strongest controlled evidence is a head-to-head trial (n=77) in which lactobionic acid produced less measured irritation and barrier disruption than glycolic acid, and several small, unblinded split-face trials (10 to 20 subjects) found it raised corneometry-measured hydration at concentrations from 6% to 30%, with no added benefit from higher concentrations in the one trial that compared them directly.[^tasic2010][^algiert2018][^algiert2019] Its antioxidant reputation rests on a lab free-radical-scavenging assay, not a controlled skin trial, and the chelation and matrix-metalloproteinase claims trace to a narrative review by authors affiliated with NeoStrata, a manufacturer of hydroxy acids.[^tasic2012][^green2009]

## How It Works

Lactobionic acid is formed by bonding a galactose unit onto gluconic acid, the same aldonic acid that makes up gluconolactone, which is why the literature classifies it inconsistently: one trial calls it an alpha-hydroxy acid, a review of the hydroxy-acid class calls it a polyhydroxy acid alongside gluconolactone, and a separate review distinguishes "bionic acids" like lactobionic acid from the smaller polyhydroxy acids.[^tasic2010][^grimes][^green2009] The added sugar unit makes the molecule larger than gluconolactone's, consistent with the PHA-class premise that bigger molecules penetrate skin more slowly and so exfoliate with less irritation, and its multiple hydroxyl groups give it the same humectant capacity credited to gluconolactone.[^cardoso][^grimes]

## The Evidence

Primary evidence, by use.

**Tolerability versus glycolic acid.** In a two-part trial, skin bioengineering measurements (erythema, melanin index, transepidermal water loss, electrical capacitance, and pH) were taken in 77 healthy volunteers testing lactobionic acid and glycolic acid in gel and emulsion vehicles over two weeks, then lactobionic acid alone in an alkylpolyglucoside emulsion over four weeks.[^tasic2010] "LA-containing samples has produced better skin performance when compared with corresponding GA-containing ones, particularly the lack of both skin irritation and skin barrier impairment," and the authors concluded 6% lactobionic acid "could be proposed as an alternative to low-molecular AHAs in cosmeceuticals."[^tasic2010]

**Skin-surface pH and safety.** A 10% lactobionic acid emulsion was tested for acute irritation potential in a cytotoxicity assay and in vivo for transepidermal water loss, stratum corneum hydration, and erythema index after 24-hour occlusion, alongside pH measurements before and one hour after a single application.[^tasic2019] "The pH of the skin was significantly reduced after application of LA-containing emulsion," and the in vitro and in vivo results together indicated "a satisfactory safety profile."[^tasic2019]

**Hydration, 10% versus 30%.** Ten subjects aged 26 to 73 received eight weekly split-face peels, 10% lactobionic acid on one side and 30% on the other, with corneometry before each session.[^algiert2018] Hydration rose significantly at all measured points on both sides, and "the difference of the skin hydration level between 10% LA and 30% LA could not be determined."[^algiert2018]

**Hydration, elasticity, and TEWL with microdermabrasion.** Twenty subjects received six weekly split-face treatments, a 20% lactobionic acid peel on one side and the same peel preceded by aluminum-oxide crystal microdermabrasion on the other, with corneometry, tewametry, and cutometry at intervals.[^algiert2019] The combined side showed a higher hydration level and both sides showed significant elasticity gains and falling TEWL, though "greater exfoliation of the epidermis in combined procedures resulted in slightly higher TEWL values" than the peel alone.[^algiert2019]

**Antioxidant activity (in vitro).** Lactobionic acid was tested alone and in an alkylpolyglucoside emulsion using DPPH free-radical-scavenging and lipid-peroxidation-inhibition assays, benchmarked against alpha-tocopherol.[^tasic2012] It "showed satisfying antioxidant activity," which the authors framed as supporting its use as a "mild multifunctional cosmeceutical" for photoaged skin, though the assays were run in solution and in a test emulsion, not on skin.[^tasic2012]

## Uses

Modestly supported as a hydrating topical across a wide concentration range (6% to 30%), with corneometry gains in every trial that measured it and no added benefit from higher concentrations where tested directly.[^tasic2010][^algiert2018][^algiert2019] Modestly supported, in one head-to-head trial, as better tolerated than glycolic acid on measured irritation and barrier disruption.[^tasic2010] Its overlap with the general evidence for barrier-supportive moisturizing is covered on [[moisturizing]]; its position among the exfoliating acids by penetration depth and strength is covered on [[exfoliating-acids-by-strength]].

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Antioxidant powerhouse."** The antioxidant evidence is a lab free-radical-scavenging assay run in solution and in a test emulsion, not a controlled trial measuring an antioxidant effect on skin.[^tasic2012]
- **"Anti-aging" / "firming" via metalloproteinase inhibition.** That claim traces to a narrative review whose authors are affiliated with NeoStrata, a manufacturer of hydroxy acids, and it does not cite a controlled trial isolating lactobionic acid's effect on matrix metalloproteinases in skin.[^green2009]
- **"As effective as AHAs with none of the irritation."** The controlled comparison found less measured irritation and barrier disruption than glycolic acid at the concentrations tested, not an absence of irritation.[^tasic2010]
