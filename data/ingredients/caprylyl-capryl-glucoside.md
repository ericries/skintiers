Caprylyl/capryl glucoside is a short-chain alkyl polyglucoside: a mild non-ionic surfactant made by joining plant-derived C8 and C10 fatty alcohols to glucose. It is the family sibling of [[decyl-glucoside]], [[lauryl-glucoside]] and [[coco-glucoside]], and it does double duty as a gentle co-surfactant in cleansers and as a solubiliser that keeps fragrance and oily actives dissolved in watery leave-on products.

> This is formulation chemistry, not an active: it does nothing visible to skin, so judge it on tolerability. The alkyl glucoside family is a recognised emerging contact allergen, and the one study that patch tested this specific molecule found that most people sensitised to any alkyl glucoside react to more than one. But the published case reports point mostly at [[lauryl-glucoside|lauryl]] and [[decyl-glucoside|decyl]] glucoside rather than this one, and no study cited here reports a reaction rate for caprylyl/capryl glucoside on its own. Treat it as guilty by family association, which is a real signal and a thin one.

## The Rubric

**Mild co-surfactant and solubiliser (functional, not a skin-health active)**
- **Effect size: none on skin itself.** Its job is to lower surface tension so a cleanser foams gently, and to hold fragrance and poorly water-soluble actives in solution. It has no anti-aging, barrier-repair or disease-modifying claim, and nothing here grades it as delivering a visible benefit.
- **Evidence quality: not applicable to benefit; the relevant question is tolerability.** It is graded `weak` for the same reason as [[decyl-glucoside]], [[lauryl-glucoside]] and [[coco-betaine]]: a formulation function plus a tolerability caveat is not a skin benefit.

**Allergic contact dermatitis risk**
- **Effect size: a real family-level signal, with no agent-specific rate published here.** Two UK centres patch tested 5775 patients over three and a half years; of the 2796 tested with a series including five alkyl glucosides, one of them caprylyl/capryl glucoside, "Twenty-nine (1.04%) of the 2796 patients tested with the cosmetic/alkyl glucoside series had a positive patch test reaction to at least one of the alkyl glucosides".[^bhoyrul] Among those 29, "Twenty-three (79.3%) patients were sensitized to multiple alkyl glucosides", and twelve (41.4%) had a background of atopic dermatitis.[^bhoyrul] The authors conclude that "there are frequent concomitant reactions between different alkyl glucosides".[^bhoyrul]
- **Evidence quality: solid for the family, absent for this molecule alone.** Three limits matter. That 1.04% is the rate among people **already referred for suspected allergic contact dermatitis**, so it is not a general-population prevalence. The abstract gives no breakdown of how many of the 29 reacted to caprylyl/capryl glucoside specifically, so its own rate is unknown rather than low. And the family's reputation rests mostly on its longer-chain relatives: a 2017 review records that "numerous cases of allergic contact dermatitis have been published, mostly to lauryl and decyl glucosides, and these compounds are considered emergent allergens".[^loranger] The title of the patch-test study is the fair summary in both directions, since concomitant reactions were "common but not ubiquitous", meaning roughly a fifth of sensitised patients reacted to only one family member.[^bhoyrul]

## Why Leave-On Products Matter Here

The exposure picture has shifted, and it is the part worth knowing. Alkyl glucosides began as rinse-off ingredients but are "currently found in rinse-off products such as shampoos, liquid cleansers, and shower gels, but also in leave-on products that include moisturizers, deodorants, and sunscreens".[^loranger] Caprylyl/capryl glucoside in particular earns its place in leave-on formulas as a solubiliser rather than a detergent, which is why it turns up well down the ingredient list of serums rather than only in washes. On this site it is declared in [[cos-de-baha-az15-azelaic-acid-15-serum]], [[tirtir-azelaic-acid-12-serum]], [[anua-azelaic-acid-serum]] and [[strivectin-tl-advanced-tightening-neck-cream]], all of which stay on the skin. A sensitiser matters more in a product left on the face than in one rinsed off in seconds.

The same review notes a related trap: decyl glucoside is a component of the sunscreen filter Tinosorb M, where it acts as "a hidden allergen", and most reported reactions to that filter are probably sensitisation to the glucoside instead.[^loranger] That is about decyl glucoside rather than this molecule, but it illustrates why these surfactants are easy to miss as the culprit.

## The Evidence and What It Does Not Tell You

Nothing cited here establishes that caprylyl/capryl glucoside is safer or riskier than its longer-chain relatives. It was in the tested panel and the family cross-reacts frequently, which is the honest basis for caution; a per-agent figure would be needed to say more, and the sources reviewed for this page do not provide one.

If you have reacted to an alkyl glucoside before, the concomitant-reactivity finding is the practical takeaway: assume the others in the family are suspect until patch testing says otherwise, and raise it with a dermatologist rather than self-diagnosing. General guidance for reactive skin is on [[sensitive-skin]].
