Hypochlorous acid (HOCl) is a weak acid that immune cells naturally generate to fight microbes; it is also an integral part of the innate immune system, and skincare brands sell electrolyzed or chemically stabilized versions as leave-on sprays, mists, and gels marketed for acne-prone, eczema-prone, and post-procedure skin.[^haralovic][^gold] The strongest human evidence involves a related but distinct intervention, dilute sodium hypochlorite bleach baths for eczema, while the stabilized topical HOCl products sold today rest mainly on an animal model, an expert-panel wound-care review, and narrative reviews rather than a controlled human trial of the product category itself.

> Hypochlorous acid is a genuine antimicrobial the immune system itself produces, and a related bleach-bath intervention has randomized human evidence for reducing eczema severity when combined with an antibiotic ointment,[^huang] but the stabilized HOCl sprays marketed for acne and general purifying claims are supported by a mouse model,[^fukuyama] an expert-panel wound-care consensus,[^gold] and narrative reviews[^krynicka][^haralovic] rather than a controlled trial of the sprays themselves.

## The Rubric

**Eczema and atopic dermatitis**

Effect size: preliminary and mechanism-driven, not yet shown for the leave-on spray form. A randomized, investigator-blinded, placebo-controlled trial in 31 pediatric patients with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis and clinical signs of secondary bacterial infection combined dilute bleach baths (sodium hypochlorite, which forms hypochlorous acid in water) with intermittent intranasal mupirocin, and found "significantly greater mean reductions from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index scores" versus placebo at both the 1-month and 3-month visits, concluding the regimen "decreased the clinical severity of atopic dermatitis in patients with clinical signs of secondary bacterial infections."[^huang] Because bleach baths were paired with an antibiotic ointment, the trial cannot isolate hypochlorite's own contribution, and it tested a dilute bath, not a stabilized topical spray. In a mouse model of atopic dermatitis (NC/Nga mice sensitized with house dust mite allergen), a topical 0.05% HOCl hydrogel prevented lesion development and reduced lesions and scratching "to a similar extent as" the topical steroid 0.1% betamethasone dipropionate, lowered dendritic-cell IL-12 production, and blunted sensory neurons' response to itch triggers, which the authors read as "a direct reduction in sensory response by HOCl, leading to significantly reduced itch and inflammation in vivo."[^fukuyama] That result is in mice, not people. A 2022 narrative review of sodium hypochlorite in atopic dermatitis notes the standard bath protocol is a 0.005% solution and that "NaOCl has an antimicrobial effect but probably at higher concentrations than those used in AD treatment," while crediting unspecified prior studies with showing anti-inflammatory and anti-itch effects that reduce reliance on steroids and antibiotics.[^krynicka] "Preliminary: one small, antibiotic-confounded pediatric bleach-bath RCT, one steroid-comparable but mouse-only gel study, and a review whose own text concedes the antimicrobial dose gap."

Evidence quality: preliminary. The only randomized human data (Huang) tested a bath, not a spray, alongside an antibiotic, in 31 children; the mechanism data (Fukuyama) is a mouse model; the review (Krynicka) synthesizes prior work rather than reporting new trial numbers.[^huang][^fukuyama][^krynicka]

**Wound care and post-procedure skin**

Effect size: not established by controlled trial data found here. An expert-panel review describes stabilized topical HOCl as "highly active against bacterial, viral, and fungal microorganisms," active "against biofilm," and able to increase "oxygenation of the wound site," and discusses its use before, during, and after dermatologic and plastic-surgery procedures, including possible reduction of hypertrophic scars and keloids.[^gold] The panel's own methods describe clinicians meeting to "discuss literature search results and their own current clinical experience," which makes this a consensus synthesis, not a randomized trial reporting its own numbers. "Not established by controlled data here: an expert-panel consensus describing HOCl's broad antimicrobial and biofilm activity, without a reported trial of wound outcomes."

Evidence quality: weak. Expert-panel consensus and literature synthesis, not primary trial data.[^gold]

**Acne**

Effect size: unresolved. A broad 2025 review of HOCl's clinical uses across dermatology lists "acne vulgaris" among the skin conditions where HOCl is used, alongside seborrheic dermatitis and pruritus, crediting HOCl's "anti-inflammatory/antimicrobial/immunomodulatory and healing properties," but its abstract reports no acne-specific trial or outcome numbers.[^haralovic] No randomized controlled trial testing a topical HOCl spray specifically on acne patients was identified. The antibacterial rationale against acne-associated bacteria is extrapolated from HOCl's general antimicrobial spectrum, not demonstrated in an acne-specific study, which leaves it well behind the acne evidence for [[benzoyl-peroxide]] and [[azelaic-acid]]. "Unresolved: acne is named as a use in a broad dermatology review, with no acne-specific trial identified."

Evidence quality: anecdotal. A general antimicrobial rationale named in a broad review, with no dedicated acne trial found.[^haralovic]

## How It Works

Hypochlorous acid is described in the dermatology literature as "an integral component of the human innate immune system," produced naturally and available commercially "in solution, dermal spray, and scar gel forms."[^haralovic] The wound-care panel attributes its antimicrobial breadth and biofilm activity to this natural immune origin, without detailing the underlying chemistry in the reviewed text.[^gold] The clearest mechanistic data come from the mouse atopic-dermatitis model: topical HOCl reduced IL-12 production by dendritic cells and impaired dorsal-root-ganglion sensory neurons' response to several itch-triggering compounds (pruritogens), which the authors interpret as a direct sensory effect that lowers itch and inflammation, separate from any antimicrobial action.[^fukuyama]

## The Evidence

**Eczema and atopic dermatitis.** The randomized, investigator-blinded, placebo-controlled trial randomized 31 pediatric patients with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis and clinical signs of secondary bacterial infection to a regimen of dilute bleach baths plus intermittent intranasal mupirocin, or to placebo baths plus placebo ointment. Treated patients had "significantly greater mean reductions from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index scores" than placebo at both the 1-month and 3-month visits, and the authors concluded that "chronic use of dilute bleach baths with intermittent intranasal application of mupirocin ointment decreased the clinical severity of atopic dermatitis in patients with clinical signs of secondary bacterial infections."[^huang] Because every treated patient also received the antibiotic ointment, the trial cannot separate the bath's own contribution, and it tested a dilute bleach bath, not a stabilized leave-on HOCl spray.

In a mouse model, NC/Nga mice sensitized with house dust mite allergen were treated with a topical 0.05% HOCl hydrogel. The hydrogel "prevented the development of lesions and scratching bouts during the whole observation period" and reduced lesions and scratching "to a similar extent as" the positive-control topical steroid, 0.1% betamethasone dipropionate ointment. It also significantly reduced IL-12 production by dendritic cells and impaired dorsal-root-ganglion sensory neurons' response to several itch-triggering compounds; the authors concluded this indicates "a direct reduction in sensory response by HOCl, leading to significantly reduced itch and inflammation in vivo."[^fukuyama] This result has not been shown in human skin.

A 2022 narrative review of sodium hypochlorite in atopic dermatitis states that the standard bath protocol is "a 0.005% water bath solution," and that "NaOCl has an antimicrobial effect but probably at higher concentrations than those used in AD treatment," while attributing anti-inflammatory and anti-itch effects, and reduced need for topical steroids and antibiotics, to unspecified prior research.[^krynicka] As a narrative review, it synthesizes existing literature rather than reporting new trial numbers.

**Wound care and post-procedure skin.** An expert-panel review states that stabilized topical HOCl is "highly active against bacterial, viral, and fungal microorganisms," works "against biofilm," and "increases oxygenation of the wound site," and discusses its pre-, peri-, and post-procedural use in dermatologic and plastic surgery, including possible reduction of hypertrophic scars and keloids.[^gold] The panel's methods describe a group of clinicians who met to "discuss literature search results and their own current clinical experience," making this a consensus synthesis rather than a trial reporting its own patient outcomes.

**Acne.** A 2025 review of HOCl's clinical uses across dermatology, surgery, dentistry, ophthalmology, and rhinology lists "acne vulgaris" among the skin conditions treated with HOCl, alongside "ulcus cruris (and wound care), diabetic ulcers, atopic dermatitis, seborrheic dermatitis, pruritus," and attributes this to HOCl's "anti-inflammatory/antimicrobial/immunomodulatory and healing properties."[^haralovic] Its abstract names acne as a use but reports no acne-specific trial design or outcome numbers, and no randomized controlled trial of a topical HOCl product on acne patients was identified.

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Clinically proven for acne."** No randomized controlled trial of a topical HOCl spray on acne patients was identified; acne is named as a use in a broad dermatology review without trial data, and the acne evidence sits well below [[benzoyl-peroxide]] and [[azelaic-acid]].[^haralovic]
- **"A gentler version of bleach for eczema, with the same benefit."** The randomized human evidence for a hypochlorite-based intervention in eczema tested a dilute bleach bath combined with an antibiotic ointment, not a stabilized leave-on spray, and cannot isolate the hypochlorite's own effect from the antibiotic's.[^huang]
- **"Kills bacteria, viruses, and fungi on skin."** The broad antimicrobial spectrum is described in an expert-panel review and a general dermatology review, both secondary syntheses rather than trials measuring a kill rate on skin.[^gold][^haralovic]
