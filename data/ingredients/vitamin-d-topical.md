Vitamin D reaches the skin two ways that get conflated in marketing: the body makes it there, when UVB light converts a cholesterol precursor in the epidermis into vitamin D3, and a small group of prescription drugs apply synthetic vitamin D analogs to the skin to treat psoriasis. The well-evidenced use is the prescription one; a cosmetic vitamin D3 serum for anti-aging or barrier support is a different, thinly supported product.

> Prescription topical vitamin D analogs (calcipotriene/calcipotriol, calcitriol, tacalcitol) are a real, decades-established psoriasis treatment, most effective combined with a topical corticosteroid rather than used alone.[^devaux][^ghafoor] Skin is also where most of the body's vitamin D is made, converting a cholesterol precursor into vitamin D3 under UVB light,[^wacker] and while a sunscreen's UVB-blocking mechanism can suppress that lab-measured synthesis by 95 to 98%,[^wacker] a randomized controlled trial and a systematic review of real-world use both found no meaningful drop in blood vitamin D from routine sunscreen use.[^marks][^neale] Cosmetic "vitamin D skincare" products built around cholecalciferol or similar have no controlled trials behind them.

## The Rubric

**Psoriasis (prescription topical vitamin D analogs: calcipotriene/calcipotriol, calcitriol, tacalcitol)**

This is the well-evidenced use, and it is a drug, not a cosmetic ingredient. A systematic review of vitamin D analog trials found monotherapy less effective than combining the analog with a topical corticosteroid, with a pooled meta-analysis finding "a probability of success twice higher" for the combination.[^devaux] A 2022 review of scalp psoriasis treatment likewise concluded the combination of betamethasone and calcipotriene "is the most effective topical therapy."[^ghafoor] Calcipotriene and calcitriol are synthetic vitamin D3 analogs designed to slow keratinocyte proliferation, prescribed and dosed as drugs, most often as part of a fixed-dose combination with a corticosteroid rather than alone; the pharmacology and dosing are unrelated to a cosmetic serum.

**Skin's own vitamin D synthesis (a biological process, not a skincare product)**

Sunlight, not a topical product, drives this pathway: UVB radiation converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in the epidermis to previtamin D3, which isomerizes to vitamin D3, then undergoes hydroxylation in the liver and kidney to reach its active hormonal form.[^wacker] Epidermal 7-dehydrocholesterol content, and the resulting vitamin D3 response to a given UV dose, declines with age.[^wacker] This is a biological fact about sun exposure and aging skin, not something a topical vitamin D cream is shown to restore; no reviewed study tests a topical product for reversing that decline.

**"Sunscreen causes vitamin D deficiency" (a popular claim, not supported in real-world use)**

The lab-measured mechanism behind the claim is real, but it does not hold up as a real-world deficiency risk. See The Evidence below: a randomized controlled trial and a systematic review of field and observational data both found routine sunscreen use does not meaningfully lower blood vitamin D, even though a sunscreen's UVB-blocking action can suppress lab-measured synthesis by 95 to 98%.[^wacker][^marks][^neale] General sunscreen mechanism and filter choice are covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

**Cosmetic topical vitamin D (cholecalciferol, "vitamin D serum") for anti-aging or barrier support**

No controlled trial of a cosmetic, non-prescription topical vitamin D3/cholecalciferol product for anti-aging, barrier repair, or general skincare turns up in the primary literature. The evidence on this page is entirely for the prescription vitamin D analog drugs used in psoriasis and for endogenous UVB-driven synthesis; neither supports a marketed vitamin D serum or cream sold as a general skincare ingredient.

## The Evidence

**Psoriasis, topical vitamin D analogs.** A systematic review covering 51 articles, with application periods of 3 to 52 weeks, found vitamin D analog monotherapy produced a "satisfactory response rate between 22% to 96%" and full "treatment success rate ranging from 4% to 40%," while the analog combined with a topical corticosteroid raised those to a "satisfactory response rate between 35% to 86%" and "treatment success rate ranging from 27% to 53%." The pooled meta-analysis found "a probability of success twice higher with VDS [vitamin D plus steroid] than with VD [vitamin D alone] in adult plaque psoriasis," with a cost-per-success ratio "1.2-1.8 times higher for VDS than for VD."[^devaux] A 2022 review of scalp psoriasis treatment concluded that "the combination treatment of topical betamethasone and calcipotriene is the most effective topical therapy."[^ghafoor]

**Skin synthesis mechanism.** "During exposure to sunlight 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin absorbs UV B radiation and is converted to previtamin D3 which in turn isomerizes into vitamin D3," which is then "metabolized sequentially in the liver and kidneys into 25-hydroxyvitamin D... and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D."[^wacker] Epidermal 7-dehydrocholesterol concentrations were "inversely related to age," and in one comparison the vitamin D3 rise after UV exposure was "at least three-fold higher" in adults aged 20-30 than in adults aged 62-80.[^wacker]

**Sunscreen and vitamin D, lab estimate versus real-world trials.** The same review reports that an SPF 30 sunscreen "absorbs approximately 95-98% of solar UVB radiation" and, on that basis, "reduces the capacity of the skin to produce vitamin D3... by the same amount."[^wacker] Tested directly, that lab estimate has not translated into a real deficiency risk. A randomized, double-blind controlled trial randomized 113 Australian adults aged 40 and over to a daily broad-spectrum SPF 17 sunscreen or a placebo cream over an Australian summer; 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 rose by a similar amount in both groups (about +12.8 mmol/L placebo vs +11.8 mmol/L sunscreen), and "no person, including those aged 70 years and over, developed any vitamin D levels outside the normal reference range" during the trial.[^marks] A 2019 systematic review of experimental, field-trial, and observational studies published 1970-2017 found that lab studies under artificial UV showed sunscreen "considerably abrogated" vitamin D production, but randomized field trials using a moderate SPF (about 16) "found no effect of daily sunscreen application," and observational studies "mostly found no association or that self-reported sunscreen use was associated with higher 25(OH)D concentration." The reviewers concluded "there is little evidence that sunscreen decreases 25(OH)D concentration when used in real-life settings, suggesting that concerns about vitamin D should not negate skin cancer prevention advice," while flagging a gap in trials of the higher-SPF products now common.[^neale]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Sunscreen causes vitamin D deficiency," used to argue against daily sunscreen.** The lab mechanism is real (an SPF 30 sunscreen can block 95 to 98% of the UVB that drives synthesis),[^wacker] but a randomized controlled trial and a systematic review of real-world use both found no meaningful drop in blood vitamin D from routine sunscreen use.[^marks][^neale]
- **Topical "vitamin D serum" or cholecalciferol-based cosmetics for anti-aging or barrier repair.** No controlled trial of a cosmetic topical vitamin D product for these uses appears in the primary literature; the evidence base for topical vitamin D is specific to the prescription psoriasis drugs (calcipotriene, calcitriol, tacalcitol), not a general skincare active.[^devaux][^ghafoor]
