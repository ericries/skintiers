Licochalcone A is a flavonoid from licorice, specifically "a flavonoid compound extracted from Glycyrrhiza uralensis", and it is the licorice constituent used in anti-redness formulas.[^review] **Bottom line:** the mechanistic literature is large and the clinical literature is not. The 2025 review that gathers it all says so itself. Note this is a different compound from the glabridin and dipotassium glycyrrhizate graded on [[licorice-root-extract]].

> **The review making the case also states the case against.** After surveying licochalcone A in acne, atopic dermatitis, rosacea, pigmentation disorders and skin tumours, its authors write that "current research is limited by geographical bias, a lack of high-quality randomized controlled trials, and reliance on preclinical models".[^review] That is the sentence that sets the grade on this page. Everything else here is read in its light.

## The Rubric

**Redness and rosacea**
- **Effect size: minimal on current evidence.** Rosacea is one of the five conditions the review covers, and licochalcone A is the licorice fraction that anti-redness products are built around.[^review] No controlled redness outcome with numbers was found for it.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** The review reports that "Clinical studies have also confirmed its ability to reduce inflammatory lesions, improve skin barrier function, and suppressing hyperpigmentation", but names no trial, sample size or effect size in its abstract, and then states the lack of high-quality randomized trials.[^review] Graded options for redness are on [[facial-redness-relief]] and [[rosacea]].

**Acne and inflammatory lesions**
- **Effect size: minimal, and better supported in combination than alone.** A separate 2026 systematic review of L-carnitine found that topical L-carnitine at 1% to 5% "reduces acne lesions, sebum production, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and cellulite, particularly when combined with agents like licochalcone-A or salicylic acid".[^carnitine] Read that carefully: it is a finding about L-carnitine formulas, with licochalcone A named as a partner, not a measurement of licochalcone A on its own. [[l-carnitine]] grades that side.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Same gap as above.

**Pigmentation**
- **Effect size: minimal.** Pigmentation disorders are within the review's scope and melanogenesis is among the pathways it describes, but no clinical pigment outcome is quantified.[^review] The graded options are on [[brightening]] and [[hyperpigmentation]], and nothing here competes with them.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.**

**Overall tier: weak.** Not because licochalcone A is implausible, but because the strongest available synthesis of it reports a reliance on preclinical models and an absence of high-quality randomized trials.[^review] That is what weak means on this site: thin clinical evidence, not disproof.

## How It Works

The review describes licochalcone A acting on "multiple signaling pathways such as NLRP3 inflammasome, NF-κB, PLC/ERK/STAT3, AP-1, and Nrf2/HO-1, thereby modulating inflammatory cascades, oxidative stress, melanogenesis, and tumorigenic processes".[^review] Those are plausible anti-inflammatory and antioxidant routes, and they are also why the mechanistic literature outruns the clinical one: pathway work is far easier to produce than a randomized trial in rosacea.

## The Evidence

**What is actually established.** That licochalcone A is a licorice-derived flavonoid with anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant and antitumour activity in preclinical work, and that reviews consider it promising across several inflammatory skin conditions.[^review] Its authors' own prescription is "multicenter randomized controlled trials, advanced delivery systems, and mechanistic investigations", which is a fair description of what is missing.[^review]

**No product on this site is known to contain it.** A search of the indexed pages returns no mention of licochalcone A, which is weaker than saying no covered product contains it: many product pages do not publish a full ingredient list. If you are checking a specific product, read its own ingredient section.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Rosacea and atopic dermatitis have prescription treatments with far better evidence than any botanical; see [[rosacea]] and [[atopic-dermatitis]].

## Common Marketing Claims

No brand copy is quoted on this page, because no product on this site is known to contain licochalcone A and no brand page was fetched for it. The claims assessed here are the ones made in the scientific literature, which are more cautious than consumer advertising for botanicals usually is: the review's own summary calls licochalcone A promising while stating that high-quality randomized trials are lacking.[^review] If you meet a product promising redness reduction from licochalcone A, that gap is the thing to weigh.
