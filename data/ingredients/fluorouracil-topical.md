Topical fluorouracil is a prescription chemotherapy cream applied to the skin, FDA-approved for "the topical treatment of multiple actinic or solar keratoses" and, at 5%, for "superficial basal cell carcinomas when conventional methods are impractical".[^dailymed] It is a cancer drug, not a cosmetic active, and it is prescribed and monitored by a clinician. The condition it treats is on [[actinic-keratosis]].

> This is the rare topical where **looking dramatically worse is the treatment working as designed.** The label describes the expected course as "erythema, usually followed by vesiculation, desquamation, erosion and re-epithelialization", meaning redness, blistering, peeling and raw skin before healing.[^dailymed] It is approved for precancerous sun damage rather than for appearance, and it is **contraindicated in pregnancy**, with reported birth defects.[^dailymed] Nothing on this page is a reason to seek it out cosmetically; it is here because the site covers [[actinic-keratosis]] and readers encounter the drug by name.

## The Rubric

**Actinic (solar) keratosis, as topical field treatment**
- **Effect size: large, and it is a treatment rather than a cosmetic improvement.** This is an FDA-approved indication, not an off-label or inferred use: the label states fluorouracil "is recommended for the topical treatment of multiple actinic or solar keratoses".[^dailymed] The mechanism is cytotoxic, destroying abnormal cells across a whole sun-damaged area rather than spot-treating visible lesions.
- **Evidence quality: approved-drug grade for the indication, with the caveat that this page cites the label rather than trials.** Its marketing category is an ANDA, an abbreviated new drug application, meaning a generic of an approved product rather than an OTC monograph ingredient.[^dailymed] That is a higher regulatory bar than anything cosmetic on this site. The trial literature behind the approval is not reviewed here; the grade reflects approved status for this specific use.

**Superficial basal cell carcinoma (5% strength only)**
- **Effect size: approved but second-choice by the label's own wording.** The 5% strength "is also useful in the treatment of superficial basal cell carcinomas when conventional methods are impractical, such as with multiple lesions or difficult treatment sites".[^dailymed] The phrase "when conventional methods are impractical" is the operative one: this is positioned as an alternative where surgery or other standard treatment is difficult, not as a first choice for skin cancer.
- **Evidence quality: approved for the narrow indication; the label adds that "Safety and efficacy in other indications have not been established" and that "The diagnosis should be established prior to treatment".**[^dailymed] Self-treating a suspected skin cancer is exactly what that sentence rules out.

## The Evidence: What To Expect, And Why It Looks Alarming

The label sets out the reaction sequence plainly: applying fluorouracil to a lesion produces "erythema, usually followed by vesiculation, desquamation, erosion and re-epithelialization".[^dailymed] People stop early because they assume something has gone wrong; the inflammation is the drug destroying damaged cells, and it is the reason this is a course of treatment supervised by a prescriber rather than something to improvise with.

Handling matters too. The label directs that it "should be applied preferably with a nonmetal applicator or suitable glove", and that if applied with the fingers, "the hands should be washed immediately afterward".[^dailymed] Application to mucous membranes "should be avoided due to the possibility of local inflammation and ulceration", and occlusion increases absorption.[^dailymed]

## Who Must Not Use It

**Pregnancy is a contraindication.** The label states fluorouracil "may cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman", records a reported cleft lip and palate and a reported ventricular septal defect along with miscarriages, and says it "is contraindicated in women who are or may become pregnant during therapy".[^dailymed] It also flags miscarriage and birth defects reported specifically when fluorouracil was applied to mucous membrane areas during pregnancy.[^dailymed]

**DPD enzyme deficiency.** The label explains that "A large percentage of fluorouracil is catabolized by the DPD enzyme" and that deficiency "can result in shunting of fluorouracil to the anabolic pathway, leading to cytotoxic activity and potential toxicities".[^dailymed] This is a genuine pharmacogenetic risk, and another reason the drug belongs with a prescriber.

**Nursing.** The label notes it is unknown whether fluorouracil passes into human milk, that there is some systemic absorption after topical use, and that a decision must be made whether to stop nursing or the drug.[^dailymed]

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Actinic keratoses and skin cancers are diagnosed by examination and sometimes biopsy, not by appearance alone, and this drug is prescription-only for good reason.
