Sodium ascorbyl phosphate is a water-soluble, phosphate-bonded derivative of [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]], formulated because it resists the oxidation that degrades pure L-ascorbic acid in solution; the tradeoff is that it is inactive as applied and depends on skin enzymes cleaving the phosphate group to release free ascorbic acid before it can do anything. Its best controlled human evidence is for [[acne]], at 5% concentration, not for the brightening or anti-aging claims it is usually marketed on.

> A randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial of 5% SAP lotion in acne found it produced statistically significant improvement over vehicle across investigator- and subject-rated scores and lesion counts, with a tolerability profile similar to placebo.[^woolery] A separate randomized comparison found 5% SAP performed about as well as 0.2% retinol on inflammatory lesion counts over 8 weeks.[^ruamrak] Direct evidence that SAP itself brightens skin or fights photoaging in humans, at any specific concentration, is thin.

## The Rubric

**Acne (topical, 5% concentration)**
- **Effect size: modest to moderate, and the best-supported SAP use.** A randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial of 50 subjects using 5% SAP lotion for 12 weeks found "statistically significant improvement when compared to vehicle in all of the parameters measured" (investigator and subject global assessments, lesion counts), with adverse events "similar to vehicle."[^woolery] A separate randomized comparison found 5% SAP cut inflammatory lesions 48.82% at 8 weeks versus 49.50% for 0.2% retinol, essentially matching it, and a combination of both did better still at 63.10%.[^ruamrak] SAP sits below the better-evidenced topical acne actives [[azelaic-acid]] and benzoyl peroxide on trial base and independence.
- **Evidence quality: solid on one vehicle-controlled RCT, corroborated by a smaller comparative trial and manufacturer-run open studies.** Woolery-Lloyd 2010 is the only placebo-controlled human trial found and is independently authored.[^woolery] Ruamrak 2009 is a randomized comparator trial but did not report p-values in its abstract.[^ruamrak] A third open (non-blinded, no vehicle arm), manufacturer-run 12-week study of 60 subjects reported investigators rated efficacy "excellent" or "good" in 76.9% of subjects, and the same paper's in vitro work found 1% SAP produced a 5-log reduction of *Cutibacterium acnes* after 8 hours; its lead author was affiliated with an SAP manufacturer (DSM Nutritional Products), so it corroborates rather than anchors the case.[^klock]

**Conversion to active ascorbic acid in skin (mechanism, not a demonstrated human topical benefit)**
- **What is shown: dose-dependent conversion in an animal skin model, not a human clinical endpoint.** SAP has no direct antioxidant activity itself; it must be dephosphorylated by phosphatase enzymes to release free ascorbic acid.[^nayama] In cultured mouse skin, 2, 20, and 100 mM sodium-L-ascorbyl-2-phosphate raised tissue ascorbic acid 1.03-, 2.17-, and 6.27-fold over control after 3 hours, and the 20 mM dose kept ascorbic acid within normal limits after UVB exposure (versus roughly a 50% drop in untreated skin) while significantly reducing UVB-induced sunburn cells, DNA fragmentation, and lipid peroxidation.[^nayama] This establishes that the conversion happens and is dose-dependent, but it is an ex vivo mouse-skin model at millimolar doses, not a trial of a marketed human serum, so it supports plausibility for downstream vitamin C effects (covered on [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]) rather than a measured human benefit for SAP itself.

**Brightening, general antioxidant, and anti-aging claims (topical, marketed uses)**
- **No SAP-specific controlled human trial located.** The literature found for SAP on skin covers acne, the mouse-skin conversion mechanism, and cosmetic-ingredient safety review; no vehicle-controlled human trial isolating SAP for pigmentation or wrinkle appearance turned up. Marketing claims to that effect rest on inference from ascorbic acid's own evidence base plus the animal conversion data above, not on a trial of SAP itself.

## The Evidence

**Acne.** Woolery-Lloyd, Baumann, and Ikeno randomized 50 subjects to 5% sodium L-ascorbyl-2-phosphate lotion or vehicle for 12 weeks in a double-blind, controlled trial; the active arm showed "statistically significant improvement when compared to vehicle in all of the parameters measured," including Investigator's and Subjects' Global Assessment Scores and lesion counts, and adverse-event frequency and tolerability were "similar to vehicle."[^woolery] Ruamrak, Lourith, and Natakankitkul randomized subjects to 5% SAP, 0.2% retinol, or the combination for 8 weeks; inflammatory lesions fell 20.14% (SAP) and 21.79% (retinol) at 4 weeks, and 48.82% (SAP) and 49.50% (retinol) at 8 weeks, versus 29.28% and 63.10% for the combination at those same timepoints, which the authors attributed to synergistic effects on lipid peroxidation and sebaceous gland function and to retinol-induced desquamation improving SAP penetration.[^ruamrak] Klock and colleagues (DSM Nutritional Products) reported 1% SAP achieved "a log reduction of 5 after 8 h" against *Propionibacterium acnes* in vitro, that an SAP oil-in-water formulation "significantly prevents the UVA-induced sebum oxidation up to 40%" in 20 subjects, and that an open, uncontrolled 12-week study of 60 subjects using 5% SAP lotion rated "efficacy ranked as excellent and good of SAP was 76.9%."[^klock]

**Conversion mechanism.** Nayama and colleagues applied sodium-L-ascorbyl-2-phosphate at 2, 20, and 100 mM to cultured mouse skin and measured tissue ascorbic acid at 1.03-, 2.17-, and 6.27-fold over control after 3 hours; pretreatment at 20 mM kept post-UVB ascorbic acid "within normal limits, even after 24 h" (versus roughly halved in untreated skin) and "significantly prevented" UVB-induced sunburn cell formation, DNA fragmentation, and lipid peroxidation.[^nayama]

**Regulatory safety review.** The Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel assessed sodium ascorbyl phosphate at reported cosmetic use concentrations of 0.01% to 3% as part of a group review with ascorbic acid and its other salts/esters, concluding the ingredient group "does not present a risk of skin sensitization" and that ascorbic acid "is not genotoxic"; a 10% ascorbic acid formulation was "nonirritant in a 4-day minicumulative patch assay on human skin," and National Toxicology Program studies found ascorbic acid "was not carcinogenic in either sex of both rats and mice."[^cir] This is a safety finding, not an efficacy one, and its reviewed concentration range (0.01-3%) sits below the 5% used in the acne trials above.

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Brightens and evens skin tone."** No vehicle-controlled human trial of SAP for pigmentation was located; this claim is extrapolated from ascorbic acid's own evidence (see [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]) plus the animal conversion data, not tested for SAP directly.[^nayama]
- **"Fights signs of aging / boosts collagen."** No SAP-specific human trial measuring collagen synthesis or wrinkle appearance was located.
- **"A stable, gentler form of vitamin C that works just like the pure form."** Stability is real: SAP is a phosphate-bonded derivative built to resist oxidation. But it is inactive until phosphatase enzymes convert it to ascorbic acid, and the only dose-response data on that conversion are from a mouse-skin model at millimolar concentrations, not a human topical trial.[^nayama]
