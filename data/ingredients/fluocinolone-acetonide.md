Fluocinolone acetonide is a synthetic [[topical-corticosteroids|topical corticosteroid]] marketed at low strengths, 0.01% (Synalar solution, and the steroid component of [[tri-luma-hydroquinone-tretinoin-fluocinolone|Tri-Luma]] melasma cream) and 0.025% (Synalar cream and ointment), that sits at the low-to-medium end of the US seven-class potency system rather than among the high-potency agents like [[betamethasone-dipropionate]].[^dailymed-solution][^dailymed-cream][^statpearls] Its own FDA label indication is generic to the drug class, "the relief of the inflammatory and pruritic manifestations of corticosteriod-responsive dermatoses" [sic], and it carries the same mechanism, monitoring needs, and atrophy/HPA-axis precautions as any other topical corticosteroid, which is covered in full on [[topical-corticosteroids]] rather than repeated here.[^dailymed-solution]

> A real, well-established anti-inflammatory tool for corticosteroid-responsive skin conditions, at a lower potency than workhorse steroids like betamethasone dipropionate, which caps both how fast it works and how much atrophy risk it carries at typical use. Its most visible role on this site is not as a standalone treatment but as the steroid third of Tri-Luma, a melasma combination cream, where the product-specific trial evidence lives on that product's own page rather than here.

## The Rubric

**Corticosteroid-responsive dermatoses (topical, on-label anti-inflammatory/antipruritic use)**
- **Effect size: real but lower-potency than the class's workhorse agents.** Fluocinolone acetonide's FDA label indication mirrors the rest of the topical corticosteroid class, relief of inflammatory and pruritic skin manifestations, but a standard US potency reference table places fluocinolone acetonide 0.025% cream or ointment in Class IV-V (medium potency) and fluocinolone acetonide 0.01% cream in Class VI (low potency), several rungs below betamethasone dipropionate's Class I-III.[^dailymed-solution][^dailymed-cream][^statpearls] The class-wide efficacy case for topical corticosteroids as a group, including large RCTs in eczema and psoriasis, is made on [[topical-corticosteroids]]; this page does not have a fluocinolone-specific RCT to add on top of that class evidence.
- **Evidence quality: the indication is FDA-labeled and durable, but this page lacks a fluocinolone-specific efficacy trial.** The DailyMed labels for both the 0.01% solution and 0.025% cream confirm the approved indication and strength, and the potency classification is a standard reference-table placement rather than a trial finding; readers should treat the class-wide RCT evidence on [[topical-corticosteroids]] as the load-bearing efficacy case, with fluocinolone acetonide positioned within it as a lower-potency member.[^dailymed-solution][^dailymed-cream][^statpearls]

**Melasma, as the steroid component of a triple-combination cream (indirect)**
- Fluocinolone acetonide 0.01% is the corticosteroid in [[tri-luma-hydroquinone-tretinoin-fluocinolone|Tri-Luma]], the only FDA-approved triple-combination cream for [[melasma]].[^tri-luma-dailymed] The product-specific trial evidence for that combination, including how it compares to two-ingredient versions of itself, is graded on the Tri-Luma product page rather than here, since the trials tested the fixed combination, not fluocinolone acetonide alone.

**Safety tradeoff (the reason this is graded "good" and not higher)**
- The class-wide atrophy, telangiectasia, striae, tachyphylaxis, and HPA-axis-suppression risks apply to fluocinolone acetonide the same as any topical corticosteroid, scaled to its lower potency; see [[topical-corticosteroids]] for the evidence behind those risks rather than a restatement here.[^dailymed-solution]

## How It Works

Fluocinolone acetonide binds the same glucocorticoid receptor as every other topical corticosteroid, the mechanism, and the collagen-thinning basis of atrophy risk that follows from it, is covered on [[topical-corticosteroids]].[^dailymed-solution] What is specific to this molecule is potency: a standard US potency reference table places fluocinolone acetonide 0.025% cream or ointment in Class IV-V and its 0.01% cream in Class VI, well below high-potency agents like [[betamethasone-dipropionate]] (Class I-III depending on vehicle), which is why it is formulated for lower-intensity or more sensitive-site use, including as the steroid component of the melasma combination cream [[tri-luma-hydroquinone-tretinoin-fluocinolone|Tri-Luma]].[^statpearls]

## The Evidence

**FDA-approved label, two dosage forms (regulatory basis, not a published trial).** DailyMed carries current FDA prescribing information for SYNALAR Solution 0.01% and SYNALAR Cream 0.025% (0.25 mg/g), each approved for "the relief of the inflammatory and pruritic manifestations of corticosteriod-responsive dermatoses" [sic].[^dailymed-solution][^dailymed-cream] Neither label publishes a clinical-trial results table; like most decades-old generic topical corticosteroids, the approved indication rests on the FDA's original review of the sponsor's efficacy data rather than on a public trial this page can cite directly, so the efficacy case here is regulatory, not an independently reported RCT for this molecule. The randomized-trial evidence for the drug class as a whole lives on [[topical-corticosteroids]]; the randomized-trial evidence for fluocinolone acetonide's specific role in Tri-Luma lives on [[tri-luma-hydroquinone-tretinoin-fluocinolone]].

**Potency classification (reference table, not a head-to-head trial).** A StatPearls reference chapter's potency table places fluocinolone acetonide 0.025% cream or ointment in Class IV-V (medium potency) and fluocinolone acetonide 0.01% cream in Class VI (low potency), versus betamethasone dipropionate at Class I-III depending on vehicle.[^statpearls] This is a standard clinical reference classification, not a randomized comparison of the two drugs against each other, so treat the potency gap as an established clinical ranking rather than a trial-measured effect-size difference.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Milder steroid, so safer for everyday use."** Directionally fair versus a high-potency agent like betamethasone dipropionate, since lower-potency corticosteroids generally carry lower atrophy risk at equivalent use, but it is still a corticosteroid: the same class-wide monitoring guidance on duration, occlusion, and site applies, and the FDA label carries the same HPA-axis-suppression warning language as higher-potency members of the class.[^dailymed-solution]
