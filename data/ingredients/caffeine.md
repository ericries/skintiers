Caffeine (INCI name, chemically 1,3,7-trimethylpurine-2,6-dione, a xanthine alkaloid, C8H10N4O2) is used in leave-on cosmetics for periorbital puffiness and dark circles and, at higher concentrations, as an anti-cellulite agent, typically formulated around 1% to 3%.[^pubchem][^herman] Its proposed mechanisms are phosphodiesterase inhibition driving lipolysis and antioxidant activity, but there is no isolated-ingredient, placebo-controlled trial for periorbital appearance, every under-eye study bundles caffeine with other actives, and the one recent placebo-controlled trial of caffeine alone for cellulite found only a modest, non-clearly-separated improvement over a vehicle that also improved.[^lupo][^ahmadraji][^rajabi][^ponto]

> In the one randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of caffeine alone for cellulite, a 2% caffeine nano-cream reduced cellulite score from 3.96 (95% CI 3.16-4.76) to 2.50 (95% CI 1.70-3.30) over 12 weeks, versus placebo going from 3.88 (95% CI 3.08-4.67) to 2.83 (95% CI 2.03-3.63); the authors describe this as a "moderate effect" for caffeine (effect size 0.475) against a "small effect" for placebo (effect size 0.286), with overlapping confidence intervals between the two arms.[^ponto]

## The Rubric

**Periorbital puffiness and dark circles (topical)**
- **Effect size: unquantifiable for the isolated ingredient.** No study tests caffeine alone against a vehicle control for under-eye puffiness or dark circles. A single-blind pad trial reported reduced "depth of wrinkles and dark circles" after 4 weeks of a 3% caffeine plus 1% vitamin K pad versus a water-only pad in 11 women, evaluated only visually and with a multi-skin-test MC 900 instrument, with no statistics reported.[^ahmadraji] A multicenter open-label study of a cream combining human growth factors, cytokines, caffeine, bisabolol, glycyrrhetinic acid, and sodium hyaluronate found average 14% to 28% improvement in dark circles and other periorbital signs after 6 weeks in 37 women, with no control arm.[^lupo] A 12-week open-label study of a multi-ingredient eye cream containing vitamin C, peptides, botanicals, chrysin, and caffeine found significant improvement in dark circles by chromameter and imaging in 37 completers, again with no vehicle arm and caffeine as one of at least six actives.[^rajabi] None of these designs can attribute any observed change to caffeine specifically.
- **Evidence quality: weak.** A 2024 narrative review of eye-cream ingredients that includes caffeine concludes there is a "lack of clinical trials specifically targeting eye cream formulations and periorbital skin" and that "variability in study designs, sample sizes, and concentrations of active ingredients across studies complicates direct comparisons."[^hamie] Every caffeine-containing periorbital study identified is open-label or single-blind, uncontrolled or minimally controlled, small (11 to 40 subjects), and combines caffeine with multiple other actives, so the ingredient's isolated contribution to puffiness or dark-circle appearance is not established.

**Cellulite (topical)**
- **Effect size: modest at best, and not clearly separated from placebo in the one isolated-ingredient trial.** A 2026 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of a 2% caffeine nano-cream applied to the thighs twice daily for 12 weeks found cellulite score improved from 3.96 to 2.50 in the active arm versus 3.88 to 2.83 in the placebo arm; the authors call this a "moderate effect" (effect size 0.475) versus a "small effect" for placebo (effect size 0.286), but the reported 95% confidence intervals for the two arms' endpoints overlap substantially (1.70-3.30 active vs 2.03-3.63 placebo).[^ponto] This is a real improvement in a well-designed trial, but the placebo arm also improved meaningfully, which is typical for subjective cellulite grading and undercuts a strong caffeine-specific claim.
- **Evidence quality: weak to moderate.** The Ponto trial is the first placebo-controlled clinical test of isolated topical caffeine for cellulite identified in this review and used a rigorous double-blind, paired design with confirmed enhanced skin penetration via a nano-cream vehicle.[^ponto] Older cellulite literature on caffeine is almost entirely formulation and penetration-enhancement research (nanoemulsions, solid lipid nanoparticles, microneedle pretreatment) conducted in vitro, ex vivo, or in rats, which demonstrates that caffeine can be delivered into skin but says nothing about clinical efficacy in humans.[^fouad][^hameed] The mechanistic rationale, phosphodiesterase-mediated lipolysis, is plausible but has not been confirmed as the operative mechanism in living human skin at cosmetic concentrations.[^herman]

## How It Works

Caffeine is a small, highly water-soluble xanthine alkaloid that is frequently used as a model hydrophilic penetrant in skin-permeation research, meaning it crosses the stratum corneum relatively easily compared to larger, more lipophilic actives.[^herman] A 2013 mechanism review states that caffeine "stimulates the degradation of fats during lipolysis through inhibition of the phosphodiesterase activity," which raises intracellular cyclic AMP and is the textbook rationale for its use in anti-cellulite products, and separately describes caffeine as having "potent antioxidant properties" that "helps protect cells against the UV radiation and slows down the process of photoaging of the skin."[^herman] The same review states that caffeine in cosmetics "increases the microcirculation of blood in the skin," which is notable because the popular under-eye rationale is usually framed as vasoconstriction (shrinking visible blood vessels to reduce the bluish look of dark circles); the cosmetic-science literature identified here does not support a simple vasoconstriction story and instead describes increased, not decreased, local blood flow.[^herman] No mechanism study in this review measured caffeine's effect on periorbital microvasculature specifically.

## The Evidence

**Cellulite, isolated 2% caffeine nano-cream versus placebo, paired thighs (topical).** A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, paired trial (design and thigh-pairing detail per the published methods) applied a 2% caffeine nano-cream formulated with a lanolin-based penetration enhancer to one thigh and a matched placebo cream to the other, twice daily for 12 weeks, after in vitro permeation testing (HPLC) confirmed the nano-cream delivered more caffeine into skin than an aqueous caffeine solution or a commercial caffeine cellulite product. Cellulite score fell from 3.96 (95% CI 3.16-4.76) to 2.50 (95% CI 1.70-3.30) with active cream and from 3.88 (95% CI 3.08-4.67) to 2.83 (95% CI 2.03-3.63) with placebo; effect size was 0.475 for active versus 0.286 for placebo. The authors conclude the formulation "demonstrated efficacy and tolerability, both objectively and subjectively."[^ponto] *A rigorous placebo-controlled trial found caffeine outperformed placebo on a calculated effect size, but the raw score changes and confidence intervals overlap enough that a skeptical read sees a modest edge over a substantial placebo response, not a dramatic caffeine-specific effect.*

**Periorbital dark circles and puffiness, caffeine as one of six-plus actives, open-label (topical).** A 12-week, IRB-approved, open-label study enrolled 40 women aged 35 to 60 with moderate-to-severe under-eye dark circles and moderate puffiness, applying a cream containing tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate (vitamin C), a prebiotic botanical extract, peptides, chrysin, and caffeine; 37 completed. Chromameter, VISIA imaging, and Laser Doppler measurements showed "significant improvement of infraorbital dark circles, mainly by the reduction in microvasculature congestion and permeability, melanin, and hemoglobin degradation-related pigmentation."[^rajabi] *A real improvement was measured with objective instruments, but the product combines caffeine with five other actives and there was no vehicle-only control arm, so none of the improvement can be attributed to caffeine specifically.*

**Periorbital rejuvenation, caffeine as one of five actives, open-label multicenter (topical).** A multicenter study gave 37 women aged 36 to 65 a cream combining human growth factors and cytokines with caffeine, bisabolol, glycyrrhetinic acid, and sodium hyaluronate, applied twice daily for 6 weeks. Clinical grading found "significant improvement" (reported as averaging 14% to 28%) in wrinkles, lower eyelid bags or sagging, dark circles, and skin texture, with subjects reporting a 32% average improvement in "tired look."[^lupo] *Another combination product with caffeine as a minor component showed improvement on an open-label design with no control, which cannot isolate caffeine's role and leaves growth factors and cytokines as the more novel, better-marketed actives in the formula.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"De-puffs and brightens the under-eye area."** No study identified tests isolated caffeine against a vehicle for puffiness or dark circles; every positive result comes from open-label or minimally controlled trials of multi-ingredient products where caffeine is one of several actives.[^lupo][^rajabi][^ahmadraji] A 2024 review of eye-cream actives concludes the periorbital-specific clinical evidence base overall is thin.[^hamie] The claim is plausible given caffeine's known ability to penetrate skin, but it is not clinically established for caffeine alone.
- **"Breaks down fat cells to fight cellulite."** The mechanistic story (phosphodiesterase inhibition raising cyclic AMP and stimulating lipolysis) is a real, textbook pharmacological effect of caffeine, and rat histology has shown reduced subcutaneous fat thickness with a caffeine-loaded nanoparticle gel.[^herman][^fouad] But the only placebo-controlled human trial of isolated topical caffeine for cellulite found a modest improvement that was not clearly separated from a meaningful placebo response, so "fights cellulite" overstates what has been shown in people.[^ponto]
