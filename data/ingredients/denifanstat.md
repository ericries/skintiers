Denifanstat is an investigational oral fatty-acid-synthase inhibitor that has been trialled for acne. It is not a skincare ingredient, not purchasable, and not approved by the FDA for anything: a query of the agency's own approvals database returns no record for it.[^fda] It is graded here because [[acne]] and [[chen-2026-denifanstat-phase2-acne]] both reference it and neither had an ingredient page to link to.

## The Rubric

**Moderate-to-severe acne (health)**
Graded minimal on preliminary evidence, which is the honest reading of one Phase 2 trial that split its endpoints. The trial's lesion-count result and its miss on the stricter Investigator's Global Assessment responder measure are set out with the figures on [[chen-2026-denifanstat-phase2-acne]] and are not repeated here. One mid-stage trial with a mixed outcome and no regulatory review is near the bottom of what this site will grade.

**Availability**
Not applicable, and worth stating plainly because it is the practical answer. There is no approved denifanstat product in the United States, so nothing on this page describes something a reader can obtain or use.[^fda]

## How It Works

It inhibits fatty acid synthase. What that is expected to do to sebum and to acne is the trial's rationale rather than a finding, and this page does not re-derive it; the published reasoning sits with the trial on [[chen-2026-denifanstat-phase2-acne]].

## The Evidence

The registry entry is the useful corrective to how such drugs get written about. The trial was registered as NCT05104125, a Phase II randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multi-centre, multi-dose study, with the drug named throughout as ASC40 rather than denifanstat, sponsored by Ascletis Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd. It enrolled 180 participants, ran from 12 January 2022, reached primary completion on 10 April 2023 and is recorded as completed. The registered interventions are ASC40 tablets at 25 mg, 50 mg and 75 mg, each taken orally once daily for 12 weeks.[^ctgov]

Three things follow. The trial is the manufacturer's own, which is the norm at this stage and is disclosed in the registry. Twelve weeks is short for an acne endpoint. And a single Phase 2 is where most drugs that never reach market also are; the site's standing comparison for what approved acne drugs look like is [[isotretinoin]] and the topical options graded on [[best-acne-products-by-evidence]].

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined: there is no consumer marketing for an unapproved drug, but the phrase that travels with it is worth marking.

"First-in-class" describes a mechanism nobody has brought to market before. It is a statement about novelty, not about efficacy, and on this page it sits beside a Phase 2 result that missed its stricter endpoint.
