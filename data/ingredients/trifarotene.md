Trifarotene is a synthetic fourth-generation retinoid and the active ingredient in the prescription cream Aklief (trifarotene 0.005%). Skincare retinoids sit on a conversion pathway: a retinyl ester is converted to [[retinol]], retinol to retinaldehyde, and retinaldehyde to retinoic acid, the only form that binds the skin's retinoic acid receptors directly. Trifarotene skips that chain. Like [[tretinoin]] (which is retinoic acid itself) and the synthetic retinoids [[adapalene]] and [[tazarotene]], it acts on the receptors directly instead of needing enzymatic conversion, but it differs from them in binding almost exclusively to one receptor subtype, RAR gamma.[^2][^3] The FDA approved it in October 2019 for acne vulgaris in patients 9 years of age and older, and it is the first topical retinoid whose trials prospectively measured acne on the trunk (chest, shoulders, back) as well as the face.[^1][^2] Its controlled evidence is confined to acne, so this profile grades that use and marks anti-aging as unestablished.

## The Rubric

Grades differ by use. Framing below is relative and names comparators.

**Moderate acne, facial (inflammatory and comedonal)**
- **Effect size: modest.** In two 12-week randomized vehicle-controlled trials, the facial success rate on the Investigator's Global Assessment (clear or almost clear with at least a 2-grade improvement) was 29.4% versus 19.5% for vehicle in PERFECT 1 and 42.3% versus 25.7% in PERFECT 2, a placebo-adjusted gap of roughly 10 to 17 percentage points.[^1][^2] That sits in the same modest band as other topical retinoids, and no head-to-head trial shows trifarotene clears acne better than [[adapalene]] or [[tretinoin]]. "Modest: statistically clear across two large trials, but the benefit over vehicle matches the retinoid class rather than exceeding it."
- **Evidence quality: solid.** Two large (2,420 patients combined) double-blind, vehicle-controlled phase 3 RCTs, plus FDA approval on that basis.[^1][^2] Short of gold-standard because both trials were vehicle-controlled and manufacturer-funded, with no independent trial testing trifarotene against an established retinoid.

**Moderate acne, truncal (chest, shoulders, back)**
- **Effect size: modest.** Truncal success on the Physician's Global Assessment was 35.7% versus 25.0% for vehicle in PERFECT 1 and 42.6% versus 29.9% in PERFECT 2, a placebo-adjusted gap similar to the face.[^1][^2] The distinction is coverage, not magnitude: trifarotene is the first topical retinoid whose registration trials formally measured a truncal endpoint, which trials of the comparators above generally did not. "Modest but distinctive: the effect resembles facial acne, and the novelty is that the trunk was tested at all."
- **Evidence quality: solid.** The same two RCTs; truncal response was a prospectively measured secondary endpoint in both.[^1][^2] Short of gold-standard for the same reasons as the facial grade.

**Photoaging / anti-aging**
- **Effect size: none demonstrated.** No graded effect can be assigned.
- **Evidence quality: anecdotal.** No controlled trial has evaluated trifarotene for wrinkles or photoaging, and its FDA indication is limited to acne.[^2] It shares a receptor pathway with [[tretinoin]], which does have anti-aging trial evidence, but applying that to trifarotene is mechanism-based extrapolation, not evidence.

## What We Actually Know

Trifarotene has two large, separately randomized phase 3 trials, PERFECT 1 and PERFECT 2 (2,420 patients combined), showing it clears moderate acne better than its vehicle over 12 weeks, on both the face and the trunk, with the primary endpoints reaching P < .001.[^1] The FDA reviewed those trials and approved the 0.005% cream for acne vulgaris from age 9.[^2] Two honest limits sit beside that. First, the benefit over vehicle is modest and typical of the retinoid class: facial success rates ran about 29% to 42% against 20% to 26% for vehicle, and no published trial pits trifarotene directly against [[adapalene]], [[tretinoin]], or [[tazarotene]], so claims that it outperforms older retinoids are unproven.[^1][^2] Second, all of this evidence is for acne, the trials were funded by the manufacturer, and there is no controlled evidence for photoaging or any use beyond acne.[^1]

## How It Works

Trifarotene is a receptor agonist, not a pro-drug that the skin must convert. The FDA label states it "is an agonist of retinoic acid receptors (RAR), with particular activity at the gamma subtype of RAR," and that activating the receptor modulates genes involved in cell differentiation and inflammation.[^2] A 2021 review reports the selectivity is unusually narrow: trifarotene is "a strong and selective agonist of RAR-γ, with lower activity on RAR-β and RAR-α (16- and 65-fold, respectively)."[^3] RAR gamma is the dominant RAR subtype in the epidermis, which is the rationale given for targeting it. This contrasts with [[tretinoin]], which activates all three RAR subtypes, and with [[retinol]], which has no direct receptor activity until the skin converts it through retinaldehyde to retinoic acid. Whether the narrow selectivity produces a clinically different result from older retinoids has not been tested head-to-head.[^1][^2]

## The Evidence

Primary clinical evidence:

**Acne (PERFECT 1 and PERFECT 2).** Tan and colleagues ran two phase 3, double-blind, randomized, vehicle-controlled 12-week studies of trifarotene 0.005% cream once daily for moderate facial and truncal acne (2,420 patients, age 9 and older).[^1] At week 12 the facial success rate on the Investigator's Global Assessment (clear or almost clear with at least a 2-grade improvement) was 29.4% versus 19.5% for vehicle in PERFECT 1 and 42.3% versus 25.7% in PERFECT 2; truncal success on the Physician's Global Assessment was 35.7% versus 25.0% and 42.6% versus 29.9%; and reductions in inflammatory and non-inflammatory lesion counts on both the face and trunk favored trifarotene, with the endpoints reported as "highly significant (P < .001)."[^1][^2] The authors concluded that "Trifarotene appears to be safe, effective, and well tolerated in treatment of both facial and truncal acne."[^1] The most common adverse events were local cutaneous irritation (dryness, redness, scaling, stinging), mainly in the first weeks of treatment.[^1]

**Regulatory status.** The FDA approved Aklief (trifarotene 0.005% cream) in October 2019; the label indication reads: "AKLIEF Cream is a retinoid indicated for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris in patients 9 years of age and older."[^2] The 2021 review notes it was "the latest new retinoid approved for the treatment of acne by the FDA in over 20 years," and that trifarotene had earlier (2014) received an FDA orphan-drug designation for congenital ichthyosis. That designation is a development incentive, not a marketing approval, and trifarotene is not approved for ichthyosis.[^3]

## Uses

Well-supported: moderate acne vulgaris on the face and, distinctively, on the trunk, in patients from age 9, where two phase 3 trials establish a modest benefit over vehicle.[^1][^2] Not established by controlled evidence: superiority over older retinoids such as [[adapalene]], [[tretinoin]], or [[tazarotene]] (no head-to-head trial exists), photoaging or wrinkle reduction (untested), or any use beyond acne. As with all retinoids, tolerability (dryness, redness, scaling) is the main practical limit, and trifarotene is a prescription drug.[^1][^2]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"The only retinoid that targets RAR-gamma, so it works better."** The receptor selectivity is real: the FDA label and the review describe trifarotene as acting mainly on RAR gamma, the dominant epidermal subtype.[^2][^3] But narrow selectivity is a mechanism, not a demonstrated clinical advantage. No head-to-head trial shows it clears acne better, or irritates less, than [[adapalene]] or [[tretinoin]].[^1][^2] Discount the jump from "selective" to "superior."
- **"The first new retinoid in over 20 years."** Accurate as a regulatory fact: the review calls it "the latest new retinoid approved for the treatment of acne by the FDA in over 20 years."[^3] The novelty of the molecule is not evidence of a larger effect; the measured benefit over vehicle is modest and class-typical.[^1] Discount the implied efficacy claim.
- **"Treats body acne, the first for the trunk."** Supported, with a caveat: trifarotene's trials did prospectively measure truncal acne and found a modest benefit over vehicle (for example 42.6% versus 29.9% success in PERFECT 2), which trials of older retinoids generally did not test.[^1] "First studied on the trunk" is fair; "uniquely able to treat body acne" overstates it, because the effect size resembles facial acne rather than exceeding it.[^1][^2]
