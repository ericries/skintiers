Cyperus rotundus is purple nutsedge, a globally distributed weed whose rhizomes ("tubers") are used in traditional medicine across the Middle East, India, China and Japan.[^antiinflam] Two different preparations are sold and studied under the same plant name, and they are not interchangeable: a **hydrodistilled essential oil**, marketed since roughly 2024 as a natural hair reduction treatment, and **alcohol or solvent extracts** of the rhizome, used in research on inflammation, pigment and hair growth.[^hairgrowth][^antiinflam] This page covers every claimed effect found for the plant, on body and face, plus what is and is not known about risk.

> **Bottom line.** Treat the viral hair reduction claim as unproven, not as disproven. Every human trial of this oil for any purpose comes from a single author at one Egyptian centre, none has been independently replicated, and the best known of them is an open-label pilot whose own abstract calls it that while the title claims efficacy "comparable" to laser.[^hair2014] Separately, the most recent laboratory work on a Cyperus rhizome extract found it **promotes** hair growth rather than reducing it, which is the opposite direction from the marketing.[^hairgrowth] The safety picture is not reassuring either, but for a different reason: nobody has published the studies that would settle it. A PubMed search on 2026-10-05 returned no dermal absorption study and no phototoxicity study for this plant, while an independent laboratory did find that one of its sesquiterpenes behaves as a phytoestrogen in cells.[^serm] That combination, real hormonal activity in vitro and no absorption data, is an open question rather than a demonstrated harm. If your goal is less hair, the options with actual controlled evidence are on [[laser-hair-removal]].

## The Rubric

**Reducing unwanted body hair, mainly underarm (the marketed claim)**
- **Effect size: not reportable.** The one PubMed-indexed trial randomised 65 people with unwanted axillary hair to oil, saline or Alexandrite laser, with 60 completers, so roughly 20 per arm.[^hair2014] Its result was that "Overall results did not differ significantly between C rotundus oil and the Alexandrite laser (P>.05)", and the only statistically significant finding favouring the oil was "decreased growth of white hair (P<.05)".[^hair2014] No hair count, percentage reduction or confidence interval appears in the abstract, so the magnitude of any effect cannot be stated from it, only its claimed direction.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Three things cap this grade. The design is described by the paper itself as "an open-label pilot study" and is assigned "LEVEL OF EVIDENCE: 3", so neither participants nor assessors of self-reported outcomes were blinded.[^hair2014] The headline is an equivalence claim built from a non-significant difference, which roughly 20 people per arm cannot support: failing to detect a difference between two treatments is not the same as showing they match. And the single significant result is in white hair, the one subgroup where the comparator is known to underperform, because laser hair removal works by targeting pigment in the follicle.[^cochrane] Beating a pigment-dependent laser on unpigmented hair is the least surprising outcome in the trial, not evidence that the oil is powerful.

**Reducing unwanted facial hair (hirsutism)**
- **Effect size: not graded here.** The facial claim traces to an earlier 2012 report by the same author which is **not indexed in PubMed** (searched 2026-10-05, where all four of this author's Cyperus papers are from 2014 or later). Its full text was not read for this page, so no design detail, participant count or result from it is asserted here.
- **Evidence quality: not assessable from the indexed record.** This is the honest position: the most-repeated version of the claim, that the oil thins facial hair in hirsutism, rests on a report that the main biomedical index does not carry. That is a gap in what can be checked, and it leaves the report's conclusions unexamined rather than refuted. For facial hair driven by hormones, the relevant medical pathways are a clinician's call rather than a cosmetic one.

**Lightening underarm and genital hyperpigmentation**
- **Effect size: not reportable, and the source abstract contradicts itself.** A 153-participant study assigned people to the oil, hydroquinone or cold cream for axillary hyperpigmentation and measured pigment with a tri-stimulus colorimeter.[^pigment] Its results section states that the oil "had significantly (P < 0.001) better depigmenting effects than HQ" and then, in the very next sentence, that the oil and hydroquinone "did not differ significantly in terms of depigmentation effects (P > 0.05)".[^pigment] Those two statements cannot both be true of the same comparison. A reader cannot tell which was intended, so no depigmenting effect size can be taken from this paper at all. A companion 308-participant study of genital pigmentation by the same author reported the oil and arbutin both beating cold cream, again without usable numbers in the abstract.[^genital]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Beyond the internal contradiction, both papers come from the same author group as the hair trials, and neither has an independent replication cited here. There is a further observation a reader should weigh: two separate trials by this author published in 2022, one on axillary pigmentation and one on post-laser side effects, each report exactly 153 participants split across three groups.[^pigment][^postlaser] That is a coincidence worth noticing in a literature with no outside replication. For pigment approaches that do have independent controlled evidence, see [[hyperpigmentation]], [[hydroquinone]], [[arbutin]], [[niacinamide]] and [[tranexamic-acid]].

**Soothing pain and redness after laser hair removal**
- **Effect size: not reportable.** A 153-participant study compared the oil against hydrocortisone and saline after laser hair removal, rating pain on the Wong-Baker FACES scale and erythema on a six-point scale.[^postlaser] The oil and hydrocortisone "did not differ significantly (p > 0.05)" on anti-inflammatory effect, while the analgesic comparison was reported as favouring the oil.[^postlaser] The abstract prints that p-value as "(P.05)", with no inequality sign, so even the direction of the threshold is unreadable as published.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Same author, same single centre, no replication, and the anti-inflammatory conclusion is once again a non-significant difference presented as parity with an active drug.

**Calming inflamed skin (dermatitis, general irritation)**
- **Effect size: measurable in mice, unknown in people.** This is the strongest-designed work on the plant and it is preclinical. An independent Brazilian group applied a rhizome ethanol extract to mouse skin inflamed with arachidonic acid or TPA, and reported it "reduced ear edema and cellular infiltrate in acute and chronic skin inflammation models" plus a decrease in keratinocyte hyperproliferation.[^antiinflam] Two details are genuinely interesting: topical treatment "did not caused skin atrophy or changes in lymphoid organ weight", and the effect "was not influenced by the GR antagonist" mifepristone, meaning it did not appear to work through the glucocorticoid receptor that topical steroids use.[^antiinflam]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, and about a different preparation.** Mouse ear-edema models screen for anti-inflammatory activity, they do not establish a human dose, a safe concentration or a clinical benefit. This study used an ethanol extract standardised to contain "45 μg/g of chlorogenic acid", not the hydrodistilled essential oil that hair reduction products are sold as, so its findings do not transfer to the oil.[^antiinflam] No human trial of Cyperus for eczema or dermatitis is cited here. For sensitive and reactive skin with human evidence behind it, see [[sensitive-skin]].

**Anti-pigment and anti-photoaging activity at the cell level**
- **Effect size: notable in isolated cells, untested on skin.** Two papers from an independent Korean group isolated single compounds and measured ion-channel and pigment effects. Valencene from the hexane fraction "potently inhibited capsaicin-induced TRPV1 and ORAI1 currents at 90 μM" and "decreased the melanin content after UVB irradiation in murine B16F10 melanoma cells by 82.66 ± 2.14% at 15 μg/mL".[^valencene] Oleanolic acid from the ethyl acetate fraction inhibited TRPV1 activity "by 61.4 ± 8.0 %" at 90 µM.[^oleanolic]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, and the weakest link is the leap to skin.** These are electrophysiology recordings and a melanoma cell line, which is several steps away from a person's face. An 82% drop in melanin in cultured B16F10 cells is a screening signal, not a predicted brightening result, and the concentrations are laboratory concentrations rather than anything a product declares. The authors themselves frame it as worth "further investigation".[^valencene] Nothing here has been tested on human skin.

**Promoting hair growth (the opposite of the marketed claim)**
- **Effect size: measurable in cells and cultured follicles.** A 2026 study from China Pharmaceutical University tested a rhizome ethanol extract on mouse and human dermal papilla cells and reported that it "activated the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway by upregulating Wnt10b", while "simultaneously downregulating transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), BMP4, and dickkopf-related protein 1 (DKK1)", increasing IGF1, KGF and VEGF secretion and "prolonging the anagen phase", confirmed in an ex vivo hair follicle organ culture model.[^hairgrowth] It identifies "the petroleum ether fraction (CRP), enriched in sesquiterpenes, as the primary bioactive component".[^hairgrowth]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, and it matters most as a contradiction.** There is no human trial, and the paper describes itself as "the first comprehensive assessment" of the plant for hair loss, which is to say the work is new and unreplicated.[^hairgrowth] Its importance here is directional. One body of work says a Cyperus preparation reduces hair; another says a Cyperus preparation grows it. The honest reading is not that one side wins. It is that nobody has established what this plant does to a hair follicle, and the two results are not even formally in conflict, because the preparations differ: a hydrodistilled essential oil in the hair reduction trials, a sesquiterpene-enriched solvent fraction in the growth-promoting work.[^hair2014][^hairgrowth] A reader buying "Cyperus oil" has no way to know which chemistry is in the bottle.

## How It Works

The marketed mechanism and the researched mechanism are not the same mechanism, and this is the most useful thing to understand about the ingredient.

The hair reduction trial states its rationale as fact in its own background section: "The oil's flavonoids have antiandrogenic activity on androgenic hair."[^hair2014] That sentence is the origin of the antiandrogen story repeated across social media. It does not appear to be demonstrated anywhere in the indexed literature. A PubMed search on 2026-10-05 for Cyperus rotundus together with 5-alpha reductase or androgen receptor returned **zero** papers. So the proposed route by which the oil would reduce hair has not, in the record searched here, been measured even once.

What independent laboratories have actually found is hormonal activity in the other direction. A Korean group fractionated a methanol extract specifically looking for estrogenic activity and isolated six sesquiterpenes, of which 4α,5α-oxidoeudesm-11-en-3-one "exhibited the most potent estrogenic activity" in an E-screen assay on MCF-7 BUS cells.[^serm] It bound ER-β more strongly than ER-α and "showed both estrogenic and antiestrogenic effects depending on the E2 concentration", which the authors proposed "could be useful as an alternative HRT".[^serm] That is a phytoestrogen profile, not an antiandrogen profile, and the compound class responsible, sesquiterpenes, is the same class the hair growth paper names as its active fraction.[^hairgrowth][^serm]

Set against that, the skin-level mechanisms that have been measured are ordinary botanical pharmacology with no hormonal component: inhibition of the TRPV1 and ORAI1 calcium channels by valencene and oleanolic acid, and an anti-inflammatory effect in mouse skin that did not run through the glucocorticoid receptor.[^valencene][^oleanolic][^antiinflam]

## The Evidence

Taken as a whole, the evidence base has a shape worth seeing before any individual result, because that shape is what caps every grade above.

**Two preparations, treated as one ingredient.** The human trials used an essential oil hydrodistilled for the study; the anti-inflammatory and hair growth work used ethanol or solvent extracts, and the anti-pigment work used single compounds purified from hexane and ethyl acetate fractions.[^hair2014][^antiinflam][^hairgrowth][^valencene][^oleanolic] These are chemically different materials from the same rhizome. Results do not transfer between them, and a consumer product labelled with the plant name does not say which it contains.

**All human data comes from one author.** The four PubMed-indexed human trials of this plant for a skin or hair indication share the same first author at Suez Canal University, Ismailia, across hair reduction, post-laser side effects, axillary pigmentation and genital pigmentation.[^hair2014][^postlaser][^pigment][^genital] No independent group has published a clinical trial of the oil for any of these uses. Independent work exists, but it is all preclinical: Brazilian, Korean and Chinese groups working in mice, cell lines and cultured follicles.[^antiinflam][^valencene][^oleanolic][^hairgrowth][^serm]

**The same statistical error recurs across those trials.** Three of the four build a headline claim of parity with an established treatment out of a failure to detect a difference: against Alexandrite laser for hair, against hydrocortisone for inflammation, against hydroquinone for pigment.[^hair2014][^postlaser][^pigment] With arms in the range these trials report, that is the expected result whether or not the treatments differ, so it carries very little information. A non-significant difference is not a demonstration of equivalence, and "as effective as the Alexandrite laser" is the strongest form of that overstatement in this literature.[^hair2014]

**Two problems are visible in the abstracts themselves.** The axillary pigmentation paper asserts in consecutive sentences both that the oil significantly outperformed hydroquinone and that the two did not differ significantly.[^pigment] And two of these 2022 trials each report exactly 153 participants split across three groups.[^pigment][^postlaser] Neither observation establishes anything about why, but in a literature with no outside replication both are reasons to hold the numbers loosely.

**What would change the picture.** An independent randomised trial of a characterised oil with reported hair counts, a published dermal absorption measurement, and a formal safety assessment. As of 2026-10-05 none of the four human papers is retracted or carries an expression of concern in PubMed, so the published record is intact. It is simply thin, unreplicated, and internally inconsistent in places.

## Risks and What Is Not Known

This section is deliberately separate from the integrity questions below, because missing safety data and disputed papers are different problems.

**No absorption data.** A PubMed search on 2026-10-05 for Cyperus rotundus with percutaneous, dermal absorption or transdermal returned **zero** results. Nobody appears to have published how much of this oil crosses human skin, which is the measurement any systemic concern depends on.

**No phototoxicity data.** The same search for phototoxicity returned **zero** results, which is a notable gap for an essential oil applied to skin that then sees sun.

**Real hormonal activity in cells, unknown relevance to topical use.** The phytoestrogen finding above is a genuine in vitro result from an independent group in a mainstream journal.[^serm] What it does not do is establish risk from rubbing the oil on your legs or underarms: an E-screen assay on a cell line says nothing about how much compound reaches a receptor in a person. Both overreading and dismissing it would be wrong. The accurate statement is that the plant contains constituents with demonstrated estrogen-receptor activity in cells, and no study has measured whether topical use delivers them anywhere they would matter.

**Reported absence of side effects is weak reassurance.** The trials report no adverse effects, including "No side effects were detected" in the axillary hair trial and "no adverse effects were found in the CREO group" in the genital pigmentation study.[^hair2014][^genital] Trials of this size, run for months, are built to measure a cosmetic endpoint and cannot detect uncommon or delayed harms. Short trials reporting nothing wrong do not establish long-term safety.

**No standardisation.** Essential oil composition varies with the plant's origin, growing conditions and distillation, and these are unregulated cosmetic products rather than drugs, so concentration and constituent profile differ between brands and batches. The trials used oil hydrodistilled for the study, so a commercial carrier-oil blend is not automatically the same material that was tested.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Unwanted hair that is new, rapidly increasing or accompanied by irregular periods or other changes can signal an underlying hormonal condition that deserves a medical workup rather than a cosmetic oil.

## Questions Raised About the Research Itself

Everything above is appraisal of what the papers report. This section is different: it records criticisms made of the research as research. The separation is deliberate, because an unreplicated trial with a weak design is a different problem from a trial whose data have been questioned, and the two should not be collapsed into a single verdict.

Cosmetic chemist Michelle Wong, who holds a PhD in chemistry, has publicly argued against using the oil, citing duplicated before-and-after photographs between the two hair papers, a table containing repeated blocks of identical numbers, inconsistent timelines between the two reports, and an implausible number of p-values reported as 0.00.[^labmuffin] She also raises the safety argument that an effect large enough to reduce hair implies delivery deep enough to reach the bloodstream, making endocrine disruption a live concern.[^labmuffin] This page does not endorse or repeat the inference that the research is fraudulent, and does not treat the absorption argument as established, because the full texts were not examined here and no absorption study turned up in the PubMed search described above, in either direction. Her documented objections are recorded because a reader weighing an unreplicated single-author literature should know they have been made.

Two framings are worth resisting in both directions. A rounded p-value is not by itself proof of misconduct, and follicle depth is not a measurement of systemic absorption. Equally, a plant being traditional and an oil being natural is not a safety argument, and a trial reporting no side effects is not a toxicological assessment.

## Practical Takeaway

There is no reason to pay for this oil on the current evidence, and no basis to promise anyone it is safe. If the goal is less hair, [[laser-hair-removal]] has a Cochrane review behind it, which found roughly 50% hair reduction with alexandrite and diode lasers up to six months, little evidence for other devices, and no documented long-term removal, so even the best-evidenced option is more modest than its marketing.[^cochrane] Mechanical and medical routes are covered there and on [[ingrown-hairs]] and [[pseudofolliculitis-barbae]], where reducing the hair itself is the point. If the goal is lighter underarms, [[hyperpigmentation]] carries the ingredients with independent controlled trials behind them.

No product profiled on this site declares Cyperus rotundus in its ingredient list.
