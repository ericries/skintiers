Tranexamic acid is a synthetic lysine derivative used as a systemic antifibrinolytic drug to control heavy bleeding, and, off-label in dermatology, to lighten melasma; the FDA-approved oral tablet is indicated only for "cyclic heavy menstrual bleeding in females of reproductive potential."[^label]

> For melasma, oral tranexamic acid is one of the better-evidenced options: a placebo-controlled randomized trial and several meta-analyses show it lowers pigmentation scores while taken, though the effect largely relapses after stopping, and its skin use is off-label rather than FDA-approved.[^rct][^kim][^zhang] Its established, approved role is hemostatic, not cosmetic, and the label warns that thrombosis and thromboembolism have been reported.[^label]

## The Rubric

Grades differ by route and by use. Framing below is relative and names comparators.

**Melasma, oral (250 mg twice daily)**
- **Effect size: modest to notable, and durable only while taken.** In the key independent placebo-controlled RCT, oral tranexamic acid cut the modified Melasma Area and Severity Index (mMASI) by 49% versus 18% for placebo at 3 months.[^rct] A meta-analysis of 11 studies (667 participants) put the monotherapy effect at a MASI reduction of 1.60 (95% CI 1.20 to 2.00; p<0.001).[^kim] The benefit is not permanent: in the same RCT, three months after treatment stopped the mMASI reduction had fallen to 26% for tranexamic acid versus 19% for placebo, and a 561-patient retrospective series reported a relapse rate of 27.2%.[^rct][^lee] It competes with, rather than clearly beats, the standard depigmenting agent [[hydroquinone]], and network meta-analyses find combinations outperform any single agent.[^liu] "Modest to notable while taken, comparable to standard topical depigmenting agents such as hydroquinone and [[azelaic-acid]], with substantial relapse after stopping."
- **Evidence quality: solid.** One independent double-blind placebo-controlled RCT, three meta-analyses (including two network meta-analyses), and a large retrospective series converge on a real but modest effect.[^rct][^kim][^zhang][^wang][^liu][^lee] Short of gold-standard because the one placebo-controlled independent RCT is small (44 enrolled, 39 completed), the pooled trials are heterogeneous, and the effect relapses off-treatment.

**Melasma, topical**
- **Effect size: minimal to modest, and harder to pin down.** A systematic review of topical melasma treatments gave topical tranexamic acid a strong recommendation, but head-to-head and placebo-controlled topical data are thinner than for the oral route, and marketed serums combine it with other actives so its isolated contribution is unclear.[^austin][^desai] "Minimal to modest, with weaker and more mixed evidence than the oral route."
- **Evidence quality: mixed.** Supported by a topical-treatment systematic review, but the strongest quantitative melasma data (RCT and meta-analyses) rest on oral dosing, and the leading topical clinical study tested a three-active combination that cannot isolate tranexamic acid.[^austin][^desai][^rct][^kim]

**General "brightening" / non-melasma hyperpigmentation**
- **Effect size: not established.** The controlled evidence is specifically for melasma. There is no comparable RCT base for generic radiance or for evening out otherwise untroubled skin.[^rct][^kim][^zhang]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Cell and animal work shows tranexamic acid can inhibit melanin synthesis directly, but that is mechanism, not a clinical brightening claim for healthy skin.[^hushcha]

## What We Actually Know

Tranexamic acid is first a hemostatic drug: the FDA oral label describes it as a synthetic lysine derivative that "diminishes the dissolution of hemostatic fibrin by plasmin," approved for heavy menstrual bleeding, not for the skin.[^label] Its dermatologic use is off-label. For melasma, the strongest data are for the oral route: a double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT (44 enrolled, 39 completed) found a 49% mMASI reduction on 250 mg twice daily versus 18% on placebo at 3 months, concluding that "oral TA appears to be an effective treatment for moderate-to-severe melasma with minimal side effects."[^rct] A meta-analysis of 11 studies (667 participants) found tranexamic acid monotherapy reduced MASI by 1.60 (95% CI 1.20 to 2.00; p<0.001) and by a further 0.94 as an add-on to routine treatment (95% CI 0.10 to 1.79; p=0.03), and a separate systematic review of 21 trials found reductions in MASI and Melanin Index but no significant change in Erythema Index.[^kim][^zhang] The benefit is largely tied to continued use: the RCT's advantage narrowed to 26% versus 19% three months after stopping, and a 561-patient retrospective series reported relapse in 27.2%.[^rct][^lee] The main honest limits are that the one independent placebo-controlled RCT is small, the pooled trials are heterogeneous, and the drug's own label lists thrombosis and thromboembolism as reported harms.[^rct][^kim][^label]

## How It Works

Tranexamic acid is a synthetic analogue of the amino acid lysine. Its established action, on which the FDA approval rests, is antifibrinolytic: the label states that "in the presence of tranexamic acid, the lysine receptor binding sites of plasmin for fibrin are occupied," which stabilizes clots and reduces bleeding.[^label] The pigment effect is understood to run through the same plasmin pathway in the skin. A clinical evaluation of a topical tranexamic acid serum describes it as "a potent plasmin inhibitor" that is "proposed to control pigmentation by inhibiting the release of inflammatory mediators involved in triggering melanogenesis," in particular by damping ultraviolet-driven signaling that stimulates melanocytes.[^desai] Laboratory work supports a more direct effect as well: a 2026 cell-biology study reported that tranexamic acid inhibited melanin synthesis in cultured melanoma cells, with an additive effect when combined with alpha-arbutin, and attenuated inflammatory gene expression in keratinocytes and macrophages through the plasminogen/plasmin pathway.[^hushcha] These mechanisms are described in the sources as proposed and preclinical; they explain why the drug plausibly acts on melasma without themselves proving a clinical brightening effect for ordinary skin.

## The Evidence

Primary clinical evidence, by route:

**Oral, placebo-controlled.** In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, patients with moderate-to-severe melasma took 250 mg of tranexamic acid or placebo twice daily with sunscreen for 3 months, followed by 3 months of sunscreen only.[^rct] At 3 months the tranexamic acid arm showed a 49% reduction in mMASI versus 18% for placebo; three months after treatment stopped, the reductions from baseline were 26% versus 19%. "No serious adverse events were noted in either group," and the authors concluded oral tranexamic acid "appears to be an effective treatment for moderate-to-severe melasma with minimal side effects."[^rct]

**Pooled oral and mixed-route data.** A 2017 meta-analysis of 11 studies (667 participants) found tranexamic acid monotherapy reduced MASI by 1.60 (95% CI 1.20 to 2.00; p<0.001) and added a further 0.94-point reduction when used alongside routine treatment (95% CI 0.10 to 1.79; p=0.03), concluding that "these results support the efficacy and safety of tranexamic acid."[^kim] A 2018 systematic review of 21 trials found tranexamic acid reduced MASI and Melanin Index with no significant change in Erythema Index, and reported adverse effects such as mild gastrointestinal upset, oligomenorrhoea, and skin irritation.[^zhang]

**Dose.** A network meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (599 patients, 8 to 12 weeks) assessed oral dosing and reported that 750 mg per day for 12 weeks was optimal, while noting "250 mg twice daily may be an acceptable option in poorly adherent patients," the same regimen used in the placebo-controlled RCT.[^wang][^rct]

**Comparative placement.** A network meta-analysis of 59 RCTs across 14 melasma therapies placed oral tranexamic acid among the effective systemic options, recorded its adverse-event rate at 17.6% among treatments with more than 80 participants, and found that 27 of 31 studies showed combination therapy outperforming single-agent therapy.[^liu] A large retrospective series of 561 patients on oral tranexamic acid reported improvement in 89.7%, relapse in 27.2%, and adverse events in 7.1%, and found patients without a family history of melasma responded better than those with one (90.6% vs 60.0%, P=.01).[^lee]

**Topical.** A systematic review of 35 RCTs of topical melasma treatments gave tranexamic acid a strong clinical recommendation alongside cysteamine and triple-combination therapy, while noting it carries a theoretical thrombosis risk.[^austin] A 12-week clinical evaluation of a serum containing 3% tranexamic acid with kojic acid and niacinamide reported a decrease in melanin index by Mexameter, but the three-active formula cannot isolate tranexamic acid's own contribution.[^desai]

**Regulatory status.** Tranexamic acid has no FDA approval for melasma or any skin use. The oral tablet is indicated only for "cyclic heavy menstrual bleeding in females of reproductive potential," and its label contraindicates it in active thromboembolic disease, a history of thrombosis or thromboembolism, and an intrinsic risk of thrombosis, warning that "venous and arterial thrombosis or thromboembolism" have been reported.[^label]

## Uses

Best supported: melasma, taken orally at 250 mg twice daily, where an independent placebo-controlled RCT and several meta-analyses show a real reduction in pigmentation scores while treatment continues.[^rct][^kim][^zhang] It is comparable to, rather than clearly better than, standard depigmenting agents such as [[hydroquinone]], the long-established first-line skin-lightening agent for melasma, and [[azelaic-acid]], which in trials beats hydroquinone 2%; combinations tend to outperform any single agent.[^liu] Weaker and more mixed: topical tranexamic acid, which has a strong recommendation from one systematic review but thinner quantitative data than the oral route.[^austin] Not established by controlled evidence: general skin brightening or hyperpigmentation outside melasma. The practical limit throughout is safety: because tranexamic acid is an antifibrinolytic, its label contraindicates it in people with clotting risk and lists thrombosis among reported harms, which is why systemic use is a medical decision rather than a cosmetic one.[^label]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Brightens and evens skin tone" (general).** The controlled evidence is specifically for melasma, where oral dosing reduced MASI and Melanin Index.[^rct][^kim][^zhang] Generic radiance or brightening claims for otherwise even skin are extrapolation from mechanism, not tested outcomes.
- **"A gentle, safe alternative to hydroquinone."** For efficacy it is comparable to rather than clearly better than [[hydroquinone]], and combinations outperform either alone.[^liu] "Safe" applies mainly to topical and short oral courses: the oral drug is an antifibrinolytic whose label contraindicates it in anyone with clotting risk and lists thrombosis and thromboembolism among reported harms.[^label]
- **"Works fast and the results last."** The placebo-controlled RCT showed benefit at 3 months on treatment, but the advantage narrowed toward placebo three months after stopping, and a 561-patient series found relapse in 27.2%.[^rct][^lee] The effect is tied to continued use.
- **"A topical serum delivers the same benefit as the pills."** The strongest quantitative melasma data are for oral dosing; the leading topical study tested a three-active combination that cannot isolate tranexamic acid, and topical evidence is weaker and more mixed.[^austin][^desai][^rct]
