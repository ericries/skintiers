Topical erythromycin is a prescription macrolide antibiotic, sold as a 2% gel[^dailymed] (and, in other formulations, as a solution, ointment, and a fixed benzoyl-peroxide combination), FDA-labeled for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris.[^dailymed] It blocks bacterial protein synthesis at the ribosome's 50S subunit, but the FDA label itself says "the exact mechanism by which erythromycin reduces lesions of acne vulgaris is not fully known."[^dailymed] A 2026 network meta-analysis found only mild, inconsistent benefit over placebo, and *Cutibacterium acnes* resistance to erythromycin is common enough, and prescribing has fallen far enough, that dermatologists have largely stopped using it as monotherapy.[^lyu][^austin]

> A 2026 network meta-analysis of 34 randomized trials (22,645 patients) found erythromycin 2% significantly reduced inflammatory lesions by 24.0 counts versus placebo (95% CI -45.7 to -2.1) and non-inflammatory lesions by 42.0 counts (95% CI -69.8 to -14.0), but no topical antibiotic, including erythromycin, showed a significant effect on total lesion counts.[^lyu] A 2025 meta-analysis of 23 studies (2,046 *C. acnes* isolates) found a pooled erythromycin resistance rate of 29.20% (95% CI 22.14-37.43%), rising over time, and a 20-year U.S. prescribing-trend study found erythromycin use for acne had declined "to near zero."[^zhu][^austin]

## The Rubric

**Acne vulgaris (topical, prescription; inconsistent benefit, largely abandoned as monotherapy)**
- **Effect size: mixed across lesion types, and not significant for the outcome that matters most.** In the Lyu network meta-analysis, erythromycin 2% was one of only two topical antibiotics (alongside dapsone 5%) to significantly beat placebo on inflammatory lesion counts (-24.0, 95% CI -45.7 to -2.1) and it also beat placebo on non-inflammatory lesion counts (-42.0, 95% CI -69.8 to -14.0), but no topical antibiotic tested, erythromycin included, showed a statistically significant reduction in total lesion counts versus placebo, and the authors conclude that "topical antibiotic monotherapies yield inconsistent efficacy."[^lyu] "A real but narrow signal on some lesion counts that does not add up to a reliable overall effect."
- **Evidence quality: a large, recent multi-trial synthesis, undercut by high and rising bacterial resistance.** The 24.0-lesion and 42.0-lesion reductions come from a 2026 network meta-analysis pooling 34 RCTs and over 22,000 patients, a substantial evidence base.[^lyu] But a separate 2025 meta-analysis of 2,046 *C. acnes* isolates found erythromycin resistance pooled at 29.20% (95% CI 22.14-37.43%) and increasing over time, and a U.S. prescribing-trend study covering 1993-2012 found erythromycin's use for acne had fallen to near zero as resistance became widely recognized.[^zhu][^austin] "A sizeable trial base, but real-world resistance and abandoned clinical use both argue the effect measured in trials does not hold up in practice."

## What We Actually Know

Erythromycin is FDA-labeled for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris, applied as a 2% gel (the FDA label reviewed here), among other formulations, by prescription.[^dailymed] Pharmacologically it inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding the ribosome's 50S subunit, but the FDA label does not claim to know why that translates into an acne benefit, stating only that the effect "appears to be due in part to the antibacterial activity of the drug."[^dailymed] A 2026 network meta-analysis of 34 RCTs (22,645 patients) found erythromycin 2% significantly reduced inflammatory and non-inflammatory lesion counts versus placebo, but like every other topical antibiotic tested, it failed to show a significant effect on total lesion counts, and the review's authors concluded that routine topical antibiotic monotherapy should be limited given "the lack of robust evidence for consistent superiority over placebo and the global concern for antibiotic resistance."[^lyu] *C. acnes* resistance to erythromycin is well documented, pooled around 29% of isolates worldwide in a 2025 meta-analysis and rising over time.[^zhu] A U.S. prescribing-trend study covering two decades of ambulatory-care visits found erythromycin use for acne had declined to near zero by the early 2010s, a trend the authors title "the extinction of topical erythromycin therapy."[^austin] The label separately warns that topical erythromycin, like other antibacterial agents, carries a risk of pseudomembranous colitis.[^dailymed]

## How It Works

The FDA prescribing information states that erythromycin "acts by inhibition of protein synthesis in susceptible organisms by reversibly binding to 50S ribosomal subunits, thereby inhibiting translocation of aminoacyl transfer-RNA and inhibiting polypeptide synthesis," a general antibacterial mechanism.[^dailymed] The same label adds that "the exact mechanism by which erythromycin reduces lesions of acne vulgaris is not fully known."[^dailymed] The label also notes that "antagonism has been demonstrated in vitro between erythromycin, lincomycin, chloramphenicol, and clindamycin," meaning combining erythromycin with clindamycin is pharmacologically counterproductive, not additive.[^dailymed] The resistance problem runs the other direction: because erythromycin and clindamycin act on the bacterial ribosome by related mechanisms, cross-resistance between the two is common, a dynamic covered in more general terms on [[clindamycin-topical]].

## The Evidence

**Efficacy, pooled across RCTs.** The Lyu network meta-analysis (2026, Clinical Drug Investigation) synthesized 34 randomized controlled trials across 22,645 patients comparing topical antibiotic monotherapies with placebo or each other for acne vulgaris. Erythromycin 2% significantly reduced inflammatory lesion counts by 24.0 versus placebo (95% CI -45.7 to -2.1) and non-inflammatory lesion counts by 42.0 (95% CI -69.8 to -14.0); azithromycin 2%, metronidazole 2%, and minocycline 4% also beat placebo on non-inflammatory counts, and dapsone 5% also beat placebo on inflammatory counts. No treatment in the analysis, including erythromycin, showed a statistically significant reduction in total lesion counts compared with placebo.[^lyu]

**Bacterial resistance.** The 2025 Zhu systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 23 studies (2,046 *C. acnes* isolates) published between January 2005 and April 2025 and found a pooled erythromycin resistance rate of 29.20% (95% CI 22.14-37.43%), with macrolide resistance (erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin, roxithromycin) generally higher than resistance to tetracyclines, and erythromycin resistance specifically found to be "progressively increasing over time."[^zhu]

**Clinical abandonment.** Austin and Fleischer (2017, Journal of Dermatological Treatment) analyzed U.S. National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey data from 1993 to 2012 covering an estimated 94.5 million acne visits. Multivariate analysis showed the likelihood of erythromycin use "declined to near zero" (p < 0.001) over the study period, while clindamycin use rose; the authors linked the decline to a rising volume of "erythromycin AND resistance" publications over the period, and concluded that "topical erythromycin use for acne has essentially ceased."[^austin]

## Uses

FDA-labeled for topical treatment of acne vulgaris, by prescription.[^dailymed] Pooled trial evidence shows a real but narrow effect on some lesion counts (not on total lesion count), and *C. acnes* resistance and clinical prescribing trends both suggest erythromycin's real-world usefulness as monotherapy has largely lapsed; current guidance favors other topical antibiotics such as [[clindamycin-topical]], generally paired with [[benzoyl-peroxide]], over erythromycin.[^lyu][^austin] General acne evidence and other topical options are covered on [[acne]].

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Kills acne-causing bacteria."** Erythromycin does inhibit bacterial protein synthesis, but the FDA label states its mechanism in treating acne is not fully known, and about 29% of *C. acnes* isolates worldwide are already resistant to it, a share that has been rising over time.[^dailymed][^zhu]
- **"A proven acne antibiotic."** It did beat placebo on inflammatory and non-inflammatory lesion counts in a large 2026 pooled analysis, but not on total lesion count, and U.S. prescribing data show dermatologists had already largely abandoned it as monotherapy by the early 2010s over resistance concerns.[^lyu][^austin]
