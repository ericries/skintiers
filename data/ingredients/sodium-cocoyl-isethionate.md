Sodium cocoyl isethionate (SCI) is the coconut-derived anionic surfactant that makes syndet (synthetic detergent) cleansing bars possible, and it is the mildness story most "soap-free" bars are built on.[^ghosh] On this site it appears in [[cetaphil-gentle-skin-cleanser]] and [[vanicream-z-bar-pyrithione-zinc]].

> SCI is a cleansing surfactant, not a treatment active: it does nothing visible to skin on its own, so judge it on tolerability. There its evidence is unusually good for a surfactant, and more interesting than the marketing. It is reliably gentler than SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate), and there is a real mechanical explanation for why. But in the test that best mimics ordinary washing, SCI came out **no milder than plain soap**, and it was not the mildest detergent in its own comparison.[^tupker] Syndet therefore gentle is a claim about chemistry, not a finished verdict about your skin.

## The Rubric

**Mild cleansing surfactant (functional, not a skin-health active)**
- **Effect size: none on skin itself; this is a formulation function.** SCI is the primary detergent in syndet bars, where it replaces the alkaline fatty-acid salts that make traditional soap. It has no anti-aging, barrier-repair, or disease-modifying claim of its own, and nothing here grades it as delivering a visible benefit.
- **Evidence quality: good for the narrow question of mildness.** Unlike most cleansing-base ingredients, SCI has both a human comparative irritancy study measured by transepidermal water loss and a mechanistic in vitro investigation.[^tupker][^ghosh] That is why this page sits above [[coco-betaine]], whose own evidence is two case reports. The grade is `mid` because the evidence supports a tolerability property, not a skin benefit.

**Gentleness relative to other detergents (the actual claim worth testing)**
- **Effect size: clearly milder than SLS; roughly equal to soap under realistic washing.** A 1999 study ranked four anionic detergents by three exposure methods, scored visually and by transepidermal water loss. In the one-time occlusive patch test the order was "SOAP > or = SLS > or = ISE > SUC", but in the repeated occlusive and repeated open tests it changed to "SLS > ISE > or = SOAP > SUC" (higher means more irritating).[^tupker] So SCI beat SLS in every model, but once exposure was repeated rather than one-off, it landed level with soap rather than ahead of it. Disodium lauryl 3-ethoxysulfosuccinate came last (least irritating) in both stated rank orders, which means SCI is not the gentlest option available, only a markedly better one than SLS.
- **Evidence quality: solid for the comparison, with an important caveat about method.** The authors' own conclusion is that "the choice of exposure model and evaluation method may be important variables influencing the outcome of irritancy testing", and they propose that "the repeated open test is the best way to simulate most in-use situations where the uncovered skin is exposed to detergents".[^tupker] By that standard the realistic ranking is the one where SCI ties with soap. Concordance between methods was high when measured by transepidermal water loss and lower when scored by eye, which is a reason to trust the instrument reading over a visual grade.[^tupker]

## How It Works

The mechanism is unusually concrete for a cosmetic ingredient, and it is about size rather than chemistry.

Surfactants damage the skin barrier partly by penetrating it. A 2007 MIT study measured SCI micelles by dynamic light scattering and compared them against the aqueous pores in the stratum corneum. An "SCI micelle of radius 33.5 +/- 1 Angstrom... experiences significant steric hindrance and cannot penetrate into the SC through aqueous pores that have an average radius of 29 +/- 5 Angstrom".[^ghosh] In short, the micelle is slightly too big to fit through the door. The same group had previously shown that SLS forms small micelles that *can* get through, which is the contrast that explains the irritancy gap.[^ghosh]

The supporting evidence for that explanation is a dose-response result: using radiolabelled SCI on pig skin, the authors showed "SCI skin penetration is dose-independent", which is what you would expect if the micelles are excluded by size rather than simply diluted.[^ghosh]

Two limits are worth keeping attached to this. It is an in vitro model using pig skin and a porous-pathway framework, not a clinical outcome. And the pore and micelle radii overlap within their stated error bars (33.5 +/- 1 against 29 +/- 5), so the exclusion is a tendency across a population of pores rather than an absolute barrier.

## The Evidence and What It Does Not Cover

A finished cleanser is not its primary surfactant. SCI bars also contain fatty acids, humectants and secondary surfactants, and the 1999 rankings tested detergents individually rather than as marketed products, so they do not predict how any specific bar behaves. The in-use tolerability of a formulated syndet bar is a separate question from the irritancy rank of its main ingredient.

Nothing cited here establishes a sensitization or contact-allergy rate for SCI, in either direction. That is an absence of data in the sources reviewed for this page, not evidence that it does not sensitize.

For the broader question of choosing a cleanser when skin is reactive or compromised, see [[sensitive-skin]], [[skin-barrier-repair]] and [[atopic-dermatitis]].
