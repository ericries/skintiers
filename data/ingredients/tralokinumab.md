Tralokinumab is an injectable monoclonal antibody that blocks interleukin-13, sold in the United States as Adbry and approved under BLA 761180 on 27 December 2021 to LEO Pharma.[^bla] It is a prescription systemic drug rather than a skincare active, and it is graded here because the [[atopic-dermatitis]] hub needs it alongside [[dupilumab]], the biologic it is usually compared to.

Its label indication reads: "ADBRY is indicated for the treatment of moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis in adults and pediatric patients 12 years of age and older whose disease is not adequately controlled with topical prescription therapies or when those therapies are not advisable."[^label]

## The Rubric

**Moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (health)**
Graded modest on gold-standard evidence. Three randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials sit in the label itself: ECZTRA 1 and ECZTRA 2 as monotherapy and ECZTRA 3 added to a mid-potency topical corticosteroid, 1934 adults in total, mean baseline EASI 32 with half the subjects at the severe end of the investigator's scale. At week 16 the proportion reaching an Investigator's Global Assessment of clear or almost clear was 16% against 7% on placebo in ECZTRA 1 and 21% against 9% in ECZTRA 2, differences of 9 and 12 percentage points. EASI-75, meaning at least a 75% improvement in eczema severity, reached 25% against 13% and 33% against 10%. Added to a topical steroid in ECZTRA 3, clear or almost clear reached 38% against 27%, a difference of 11 points whose confidence interval runs from 1% to 21%, and EASI-75 reached 56% against 37%.[^label]

The evidence grade is as high as this site awards. The effect grade is what keeps the overall tier at good rather than best: on monotherapy, 79% and 84% of patients did not reach clear or almost clear at four months, and the combination trial's headline endpoint is separated from placebo-plus-steroid by a margin that only just clears zero.

**Itch in atopic dermatitis (health)**
Graded modest on gold-standard evidence, and worth grading separately because itch is what patients report. At least a 4-point improvement on the 11-point worst daily pruritus scale, from a baseline weekly average of 8, was reached by 20% against 10% in ECZTRA 1, 25% against 9% in ECZTRA 2, and 46% against 35% on background steroid in ECZTRA 3.[^label]

**Do not read these numbers against dupilumab's directly.** No head-to-head trial is cited in either label, and the endpoints are not defined identically: this label counts a responder as any subject at IGA 0 or 1, while dupilumab's requires IGA 0 or 1 *and* at least a 2-point improvement.[^label] Tralokinumab's lower response rates are therefore measured on the more permissive of the two definitions, which makes the gap between the drugs wider than the raw percentages suggest, not narrower. Anyone choosing between them is making a clinical decision, not a product comparison.

## How It Works

The label describes it as "a human IgG4 monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to human interleukin-13 (IL-13) and inhibits its interaction with the IL-13 receptor" alpha-1 and alpha-2 subunits.[^label] IL-13 is one of the type 2 cytokines driving the inflammation and barrier disruption that define the disease; the disease side of that story is on [[atopic-dermatitis]] and the barrier side on [[skin-barrier-repair]]. Dupilumab blocks the shared IL-4 receptor alpha subunit and so blocks IL-4 signalling as well, which is the mechanistic difference between the two, and it is a difference in target rather than evidence quality.

## The Evidence

What the three trials establish is a real but partial effect over placebo at four months, in adults selected for disease severe enough that topicals had already failed. The trial regimen was 600 mg on day 0 then 300 mg every other week.[^label]

What they do not establish is durability, and the label's own maintenance data is re-randomised from responders only. The longest follow-up summarised on this site, [[gooderham-2026-tralokinumab-4-year-atopic-dermatitis]], reports most patients held at clear-to-mild disease over roughly four years, but it is a single-arm open-label extension restricted to people who had already completed a year of treatment, so it describes what happens to responders and cannot speak to how many patients get there.

The label carries no boxed warning, verified absent from the current SPL. Its warnings are hypersensitivity including anaphylaxis and angioedema, conjunctivitis and keratitis, pre-existing helminth infection, and avoidance of live vaccines. Through week 16 across the pooled trials, conjunctivitis was reported in 7.5% on monotherapy against 3.1% on placebo and in 13.6% against 4.9% on background steroid; upper respiratory tract infections in 23.8% against 20.4%; injection-site reactions in 7.4% against 4.1%; and eosinophilia in 1.4% against 0.5%.[^label] The eye signal is the one that distinguishes this class from a topical.

## Common Marketing Claims

Quarantined: these are the manufacturer's own promotional lines, not findings, and nothing above rests on them.

The brand site's headline invitation is to "See the possibilities of clearer skin, itch relief and long-term control."[^brand] Read that against the label's own Table 2, where clear or almost clear at week 16 was reached by 16% and 21% of monotherapy patients. "Possibilities" is doing real work in that sentence.

The page also offers to let visitors "See real results from real patients on Adbry", and carries both the caption "A real Adbry patient" and the caption "Actor portrayal" along with repeated "Individual results may vary" disclaimers.[^brand] Which caption labels which image is not something a text capture can establish, so no claim is made here about any specific photograph; the point is only that the page mixes patient imagery with actor portrayal and says so.

A copay line, "You could pay as little as $0* for Adbry", is a commercial offer with an annual cap and eligibility conditions, not evidence about the drug.[^brand]
