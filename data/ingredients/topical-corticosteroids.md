Topical corticosteroids are synthetic glucocorticoids applied to the skin as creams, ointments, gels, foams, or solutions to suppress local inflammation; in the United States they are ranked into seven potency classes, from class I "super-potent" agents like clobetasol propionate 0.05% down to class VII mild agents like [[hydrocortisone-otc-topical-corticosteroid|hydrocortisone]] 0.5% to 2.5%, with mid-to-high-potency workhorses such as [[betamethasone-dipropionate]] in between.[^solman][^kerkhof] They are the best-evidenced anti-inflammatory topicals in dermatology, first-line for [[atopic-dermatitis]] flares and a core component of [[psoriasis]] regimens, but that evidence base sits alongside well-documented, potency- and duration-dependent harms: skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, tachyphylaxis, and a withdrawal syndrome on stopping high-potency use, which is why this class is graded good rather than best despite its huge randomized-trial base.[^hajar][^kim]

> In a randomised, double-blind trial of 376 adults with moderate to severe atopic dermatitis, after flares were stabilised with daily fluticasone propionate, applying it twice weekly during a 16-week maintenance phase cut the risk of relapse versus emollient alone by a hazard ratio of 5.8 (95% CI 3.1 to 10.8, P<0.001) for the cream and 1.9 (95% CI 1.2 to 3.2, P=0.010) for the ointment, with median time to relapse over 16 weeks on active treatment versus 6 weeks on emollient alone.[^berthjones]

## The Rubric

**Atopic dermatitis / eczema flares (topical, first-line)**
- **Effect size: large and well established across decades of RCTs.** A 2019 summary of systematic reviews on eczema treatment found "reasonable evidence of benefit for topical corticosteroids," equivalent in efficacy to topical calcineurin inhibitors ([[tacrolimus-topical]], [[pimecrolimus]]), with topical corticosteroids preferred as standard of care because the calcineurin inhibitors cost more and carry more adverse reactions; the review also found "no evidence that multiple applications are better than once-daily application."[^solman] In the maintenance setting, twice-weekly fluticasone propionate added to emollients after a flare was stabilised substantially cut the relapse risk relative to emollient alone over 16 weeks (hazard ratios 5.8 and 1.9 for cream and ointment respectively, both statistically significant).[^berthjones]
- **Evidence quality: high, one of the deepest RCT literatures in topical dermatology.** Decades of randomized, vehicle- and active-controlled trials across potency classes and vehicles underpin routine use; the Berth-Jones maintenance trial alone randomized 295 patients across 39 centres in 6 countries.[^berthjones][^solman]

**Plaque and scalp psoriasis (topical, alone or combined with vitamin D analogs)**
- **Effect size: large for higher-potency agents, and additive when combined with a vitamin D analog.** In an 8-week, double-blind trial of 1,417 patients with scalp psoriasis, betamethasone dipropionate 0.5 mg/g alone achieved "absence of disease" or "very mild disease" in 61.0% of patients versus 43.4% on calcipotriol alone, and a fixed combination of calcipotriol plus betamethasone dipropionate reached 68.4%, significantly higher than both betamethasone dipropionate alone (P=0.0079) and calcipotriol alone (P<0.0001).[^kerkhof] The corticosteroid component is doing most of the clinical work in that comparison, with the vitamin D analog adding an incremental but real benefit.
- **Evidence quality: high for the isolated corticosteroid, from a large, well-controlled, multicentre trial.** 1,417 patients randomized across three arms is a substantial and well-powered dataset, though it is a single named trial architecture (repeated in several similarly designed studies from the same manufacturer) rather than an independent Cochrane synthesis.[^kerkhof]

**Long-term / high-potency or occluded use (safety limits)**
- **Effect size of harm: real and potency/duration-dependent, not a rare idiosyncratic reaction.** A systematic review of topical corticosteroid withdrawal ("steroid addiction") screened 294 studies and included 34 meeting criteria; withdrawal was reported mostly on the face and genital area (99.3%) and in women (81.0%), primarily "in the setting of long-term inappropriate use of potent TCS," with burning/stinging in 65.5% and erythema in 92.3% of cases, subdividing into papulopustular and erythematoedematous subtypes.[^hajar] A 2026 nationwide Korean cohort of 685,389 adults found potent TCS use was associated with a higher adjusted risk of incident type 2 diabetes (aHR 1.15, 95% CI 1.04-1.26), as were higher prescription frequency (aHR 1.26) and cumulative use of 6 months or more (aHR 1.45), while low-potency use and shorter exposure were associated with lower risk; overall TCS use was not associated with T2DM after adjustment.[^kim]
- **Evidence quality: the withdrawal literature is a real but low-quality evidence base; the metabolic-risk finding is from one large but observational cohort.** The withdrawal review's own limitations section flags "low quality of evidence, variability in the extent of data, and the lack of studies with rigorous steroid addiction methodology."[^hajar] The diabetes cohort is a single population-based study from South Korea with adjustment for confounders but the usual limits of observational data (residual confounding by indication, since sicker patients get stronger and longer steroid courses).[^kim]

## How It Works

Topical corticosteroids bind the glucocorticoid receptor in keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells, translocating to the nucleus to upregulate anti-inflammatory genes and repress pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as NF-kB and AP-1, which reduces cytokine production, vasoconstricts dermal vessels, and suppresses the recruitment and activity of inflammatory cells.[^hajar] Potency differs by molecule (the specific glucocorticoid, e.g. clobetasol propionate versus hydrocortisone), by vehicle (ointments generally increase potency and occlusion versus creams or lotions), and by concentration; the US seven-class system, built on vasoconstrictor assay data, ranks agents from class I super-potent (e.g. clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment) through high- and mid-potency agents like [[betamethasone-dipropionate]] (whose class shifts with vehicle, from potent ointments and augmented bases toward the milder cream), down to class VI to VII mild agents like low-strength hydrocortisone.[^kerkhof] The same receptor-mediated mechanism that suppresses inflammation also thins the epidermis and dermis with prolonged or high-potency use: chronic glucocorticoid signaling reduces fibroblast collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, which is the proposed basis for atrophy, striae, and telangiectasia, and tachyphylaxis (reduced response with continuous daily use) is thought to reflect receptor downregulation. Topical steroid withdrawal, by contrast, is a rebound phenomenon on stopping potent, long-duration facial or genital use rather than a direct effect of any single application.[^hajar]

## The Evidence

**Betamethasone dipropionate versus calcipotriol versus fixed combination, scalp psoriasis (topical, randomized double-blind).** An 8-week, multicentre, double-blind, parallel-group trial randomized 1,417 adults with scalp psoriasis involving more than 10% of the scalp to a once-daily two-compound formulation of calcipotriol 50 microg/g plus betamethasone dipropionate 0.5 mg/g (n=568), betamethasone dipropionate alone (n=563), or calcipotriol alone (n=286). "Absence of disease" or "very mild disease" at week 8 was reached by 68.4% on the combination, 61.0% on betamethasone dipropionate alone, and 43.4% on calcipotriol alone (combination vs. betamethasone dipropionate P=0.0079; combination vs. calcipotriol P<0.0001), and lesional/perilesional adverse events were lower in the corticosteroid-containing arms than the calcipotriol-only arm.[^kerkhof] *The corticosteroid alone outperformed the vitamin D analog alone by a wide margin, and adding the vitamin D analog to the corticosteroid gave a further, statistically significant but smaller improvement.*

**Twice-weekly maintenance fluticasone propionate versus emollient alone, atopic dermatitis relapse prevention (topical, randomized double-blind).** After stabilising 376 adults' moderate to severe atopic dermatitis flares with daily fluticasone propionate for 4 weeks, 295 patients continued into a 16-week randomized maintenance phase applying either fluticasone propionate (cream or ointment) or its emollient-only vehicle twice weekly to usually-affected sites, with daily emollient in both arms. After 16 weeks, disease remained under control in 133 of the 295 randomized patients: 87 of those using fluticasone propionate twice weekly (cream and ointment arms combined) versus 46 using emollient alone; patients using fluticasone propionate cream twice weekly were 5.8 times less likely to relapse than emollient alone (95% CI 3.1 to 10.8, P<0.001), and those using the ointment 1.9 times less likely (95% CI 1.2 to 3.2, P=0.010), with median time to relapse over 16 weeks on active treatment versus 6 weeks on emollient alone and no difference in adverse events between groups.[^berthjones] *A well-powered, multinational RCT found intermittent (twice-weekly, not daily) potent-corticosteroid use meaningfully delays eczema relapse once a flare is controlled, without an increase in reported adverse events over 16 weeks.*

**Topical corticosteroid withdrawal, systematic review (topical, safety).** A systematic review for the National Eczema Association Task Force screened 294 studies and included 34 meeting inclusion criteria on topical corticosteroid withdrawal ("steroid addiction"). Withdrawal was reported predominantly on the face and genital area (99.3% of cases) and in women (81.0%), "primarily in the setting of long-term inappropriate use of potent TCS"; burning and stinging were the most frequent symptoms (65.5%) and erythema the most common sign (92.3%), with the syndrome dividing into papulopustular and erythematoedematous subtypes. The authors concluded withdrawal "is likely a distinct clinical adverse effect of TCS misuse" while explicitly flagging low evidence quality and the lack of rigorous methodology across the included studies.[^hajar] *The best available systematic evidence supports topical steroid withdrawal as a real, if poorly quantified, risk concentrated in long-term, potent, facial or genital use, not routine short courses on the trunk or limbs.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Steroid-free" positioning on competing products implies topical corticosteroids are unsafe for routine use.** The RCT evidence for short, appropriately potency-matched courses is strong and the adverse-event rates in controlled trials like the Berth-Jones maintenance study were not elevated versus placebo/emollient over 16 weeks.[^berthjones] The real risk concentrates in high-potency, long-duration, facial, or genital use, which is a dosing and site problem, not a reason to avoid the class altogether.[^hajar]
- **"Clinically proven" is fair, but potency and site are doing the work, not the class label alone.** The scalp-psoriasis trial shows a roughly 18-point difference in response between a mid-to-high-potency corticosteroid and a vitamin D analog, and a 2026 cohort found diabetes risk scaled with potency and duration, not with use alone.[^kerkhof][^kim] "Corticosteroid" is not a single-strength claim; a 2.5% hydrocortisone class VII product and a class I clobetasol product are not interchangeable in either efficacy or risk.
