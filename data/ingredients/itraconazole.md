Itraconazole is an oral triazole antifungal, dispensed only by prescription and taken systemically rather than applied to the skin.[^dailymed] Its FDA-approved indications are systemic fungal infections (blastomycosis, histoplasmosis, aspergillosis) and onychomycosis of the toenail and fingernail caused by dermatophytes; using it for other dermatologic conditions, including Malassezia folliculitis ("fungal acne") and pityriasis versicolor, is common in the clinical literature but is not itself an FDA-labeled indication.[^dailymed][^saunte] For the skin and nail conditions it does treat, the evidence is real but narrow: moderate trial-level cure rates for onychomycosis, a credible reported effect for Malassezia folliculitis, and only a brief effectiveness claim for pityriasis versicolor, all set against a systemic drug carrying a boxed warning and a long list of dangerous drug interactions.[^dailymed][^saunte]

> **Bottom line:** Itraconazole clears a defined, narrow set of fungal skin and nail conditions when taken as a prescribed systemic course, most notably nail dermatophyte infection and Malassezia folliculitis that has not responded to topical treatment. It is not a topical skincare active, and its dermatologic use carries a real safety burden, a boxed warning against use in heart failure, a long list of contraindicated drug combinations, and rare but serious liver toxicity, that makes it a prescriber-managed decision rather than a self-directed one.[^dailymed]

## The Rubric

**Malassezia folliculitis ("fungal acne"), oral therapy for cases that do not clear topically.**
- **Effect size: notable, but reported as a range rather than a pooled trial statistic.** A 2020 review of Malassezia-associated skin diseases states that "systemic itraconazole 100-200 mg daily has been used for 1-4 weeks with a clinical treatment effect of 69-100%."[^saunte] That is a wide range spanning several small studies rather than one head-to-head trial figure, but the direction and magnitude are consistent with itraconazole being an effective step-up option once topical antifungal treatment, covered on [[fungal-acne]], has failed.
- **Evidence quality: thinner than the onychomycosis data.** The 69 to 100% figure comes from a narrative review synthesizing multiple studies, not a formal systematic review or meta-analysis with a pooled effect size and confidence interval. It is credible but not gold-standard.

**Pityriasis (tinea) versicolor.**
- **Effect size: reported as effective, but without a quantified cure rate.** The same review states that "oral itraconazole is also very effective in cases of pityriasis versicolor, 100 mg daily for 10 days," reserved for "extensive or recalcitrant cases" rather than as a routine first choice.[^saunte]
- **Evidence quality: thin.** This is a single descriptive line in a broader review, not a dedicated trial result. It supports itraconazole as a real option for resistant or widespread pityriasis versicolor, not a headline number.

**Onychomycosis (nail dermatophyte infection), the drug's actual FDA-approved dermatologic indication.**
- **Effect size: real but moderate.** The FDA label's own clinical trial data show continuous dosing of 200 mg once daily for 12 weeks in toenail onychomycosis produced mycologic cure (negative KOH plus negative culture) in 54% of patients, an "overall success" in 35%, and both mycologic and clinical cure in only 14% (based on the 110 patients given the drug across three pooled studies).[^dailymed] A pulse regimen for fingernail disease, 200 mg twice daily for one week, repeated once after a three-week gap, performed better: 61% mycologic cure, 56% overall success, and 47% with both mycologic and clinical cure (based on the 37 patients given the drug).[^dailymed] These are the most rigorous numbers behind any itraconazole dermatologic use, and they show a real but middling effect: roughly half of patients get a mycologically clean nail, and fewer than half get a nail that both tests clean and looks clinically cured.
- **Evidence quality: the strongest of the three uses.** This is the drug's own FDA-reviewed clinical trial data behind an approved indication, not a review's summary of scattered smaller studies.

Across all three uses, itraconazole is not a treatment for ordinary [[acne]]; it targets yeast (Malassezia) or dermatophyte fungi, not the bacterial, comedone-driven process behind acne vulgaris. Malassezia folliculitis and pityriasis versicolor both trace to the same yeast genus implicated in [[seborrheic-dermatitis]], and topical antifungal treatment, most evidenced with [[ketoconazole]], is the usual first step before a systemic course like itraconazole is considered.[^saunte]

## The Evidence

The onychomycosis numbers above come directly from the FDA label's clinical trial results for SPORANOX, the drug's approved dermatologic indication.[^dailymed] The Malassezia folliculitis and pityriasis versicolor figures come from a single 2020 review of Malassezia-associated skin diseases, which synthesizes the treatment literature for both conditions but does not itself report a pooled trial statistic the way the onychomycosis label data does.[^saunte] Taken together, the strongest evidence behind itraconazole's dermatologic use is its own FDA trial data for nail disease, showing a moderate, not high, cure rate; the evidence for its off-label use in Malassezia folliculitis and pityriasis versicolor is a credible but less rigorously quantified reported effect from the clinical literature.

## Safety

Itraconazole is a systemic drug, not a topical, and its risk profile reflects that. The FDA label carries a boxed warning stating it "should not be administered for the treatment of onychomycosis in patients with evidence of ventricular dysfunction such as congestive heart failure (CHF) or a history of CHF," and instructs that treatment be discontinued if signs of heart failure appear.[^dailymed] The same boxed warning lists drugs that must never be combined with itraconazole, including methadone, several heart-rhythm drugs (disopyramide, dofetilide, dronedarone, quinidine), ergot alkaloids, the statins lovastatin and simvastatin, and the sedatives triazolam and oral midazolam, because itraconazole and its major metabolite are "potent CYP3A4 inhibitors" capable of raising those drugs to dangerous levels.[^dailymed] Separately, the label warns of "rare cases of serious hepatotoxicity, including liver failure and death," and notes that some of these cases occurred in patients with "neither pre-existing liver disease nor a serious underlying medical condition," while some "developed within the first week of treatment" (two separate observations, not one combined group).[^dailymed] None of this rules out a prescriber-managed course of treatment, but it places itraconazole in a different risk category than any topical antifungal covered on this site.
