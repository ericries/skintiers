Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium, listed on cosmetic labels as Chrysanthemum Parthenium) is a Compositae-family plant long sold as an oral herbal remedy for migraine. The version used in skincare is a different, chemically altered preparation: parthenolide, the sesquiterpene lactone behind the plant's internal effects, is also "an important sensitizer in European Compositae-allergic patients," so a group of Johnson & Johnson cosmetic scientists developed a parthenolide-depleted extract (PD-feverfew) that removes the allergenic compound while keeping the plant's antioxidant flavonoids.[^paulsen][^sur] The extract appears on ingredient lists, including [Aveeno's Ultra-Calming Daily Facial Moisturizer SPF 30](https://incidecoder.com/products/aveeno-ultra-calming-daily-facial-moisturizer-spf-30), as Chrysanthemum Parthenium (Feverfew) Flower/Leaf/Stem Juice.

> Nearly all of the controlled evidence for a calming, anti-redness effect from PD-feverfew comes from the single Johnson & Johnson laboratory that developed it: small trials and models reported reduced erythema after UV exposure and after a chemical irritant, plus antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity in cells and animals.[^martin][^sur] An independent Danish patch-test study found that despite the depletion process, some feverfew-allergic patients still reacted to the finished creams.[^paulsen]

## The Rubric

**Topical redness and irritation, cosmetic soothing use (PD-feverfew)**
- Effect size: modest, in small manufacturer trials. In a clinical study run by the extract's own developers, topical PD-feverfew significantly reduced erythema versus placebo 24 hours after UV exposure.[^martin] A separate study from the same group reported reduced erythema in a methyl-nicotinate-induced vasodilation model, a standard test of whether a topical blunts a chemically triggered flush, and found PD-feverfew more potent than whole (parthenolide-containing) feverfew at reducing TPA-induced dermatitis in animal skin.[^sur] That is a small, single-source signal that a PD-feverfew formulation can blunt short-term, experimentally triggered redness; it is not evidence that it treats a chronic redness condition such as [[rosacea]].
- Evidence quality: preliminary and single-lab. The clinical and in vivo erythema data were produced and published by the Johnson & Johnson Skin Research Center group that also developed the extract, and none of it has been independently replicated.[^martin][^sur]

**UV and oxidative-stress protection (PD-feverfew, mechanistic)**
- Effect size: modest, and mostly cell-based. PD-feverfew scavenged a range of reactive oxygen species with greater activity than Vitamin C in a cell-free assay, and topical application reduced UV-induced epidermal hyperplasia, DNA damage and apoptosis in an animal model.[^martin] A later paper from the same group found PD-feverfew activates the Nrf2/antioxidant-response-element pathway and reduces UV-induced DNA damage in cultured human keratinocytes, but that work stopped at cell culture and was not tested on human skin.[^rodriguez]
- Evidence quality: preliminary, single-lab, and partly cell-culture only.[^rodriguez]

**Safety of the "parthenolide-depleted" claim**
Depletion does not guarantee tolerance for someone already allergic to feverfew. An independent patch-test study in Denmark tested seven patients with confirmed feverfew contact allergy against two commercial PD-feverfew creams: four of the seven reacted positively to one of the creams, and that reactivity tracked with simultaneous positive reactions to parthenolide itself. When the same cream was re-analyzed roughly two years later, no parthenolide was detectable, which the authors attributed to degradation of the compound over time.[^paulsen] A separate, independent review of whole feverfew extracts (not the depleted cosmetic form) found antioxidant activity varies sharply by plant part and concentration, with flower extract giving the strongest DPPH free-radical scavenging (84.09% versus 69.54% for a whole-herb extract) and the best effect on cultured skin cells below a 2.5% concentration, underlining how much a feverfew extract's activity depends on which plant fraction and dose are used.[^michalak]

## How It Works

Whole feverfew's internal effects, and its skin-sensitizing risk, run through parthenolide.[^paulsen] The cosmetic extract is processed to remove that compound and instead concentrates a pool of water-soluble antioxidant flavonoids. In vitro, PD-feverfew restored cigarette-smoke-depleted cellular thiols and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine release; the developers confirmed the extract was sufficiently depleted because, unlike whole feverfew, it did not inhibit TNF-alpha-induced NF-kappaB activity.[^martin][^sur] It also directly inhibited the pro-inflammatory enzymes 5-lipoxygenase, phosphodiesterase-3 and phosphodiesterase-4, and reduced release of nitric oxide, PGE2 and TNF-alpha from macrophages and of TNF-alpha, IL-2, IFN-gamma and IL-4 from human immune cells.[^sur] Separately, PD-feverfew was found to trigger nuclear translocation of the Nrf2 transcription factor and activate the antioxidant response element in a PI3-kinase-dependent, dose-dependent way in human keratinocytes, switching on cells' own antioxidant and DNA-repair genes.[^rodriguez]

## The Evidence

**Antioxidant and UV protection.** Martin and colleagues at the Johnson & Johnson Skin Research Center reported that PD-feverfew had free-radical scavenging activity "against a wide range of reactive oxygen species and with greater activity than Vitamin C." In an animal model, topical PD-feverfew reduced UV-induced epidermal hyperplasia, DNA damage and apoptosis, and in a clinical study the extract "significantly reduced erythema versus placebo 24 h post-UV exposure."[^martin]

**Anti-inflammatory activity and redness.** Sur and colleagues, from the same Johnson & Johnson group, confirmed the extract's parthenolide depletion pharmacologically, then showed it inhibited several pro-inflammatory enzymes and mediators in cell and immune-cell assays, and inhibited TPA-induced PGE2 release from human skin equivalents. In vivo, PD-feverfew "inhibited oxazolone-induced dermatitis, and was more potent than whole Feverfew in reducing TPA-induced dermatitis." The authors report the extract's efficacy was "confirmed clinically by a reduction in erythema in a methyl nicotinate-induced vasodilation model," a standard chemical-flush test rather than a trial in a diagnosed redness condition.[^sur]

**DNA repair pathway, cell culture only.** Rodriguez and colleagues, again from the Johnson & Johnson Skin Research Center, tested PD-feverfew in primary human keratinocytes and KB cells. The extract "induce[d] Nrf2 nuclear translocation and... increase[d] ARE activity in a dose dependent manner," an effect blocked by a PI3-kinase inhibitor, and it "reduced UV-induced DNA damage in a PI3K and Nrf2-dependent manner." This work was conducted entirely in cultured cells, not in human skin or human subjects.[^rodriguez]

**Persistent allergenicity in feverfew-allergic patients.** Paulsen and colleagues at Odense University Hospital, Denmark, an independent academic group with no stated connection to the extract's manufacturer, patch tested seven patients with confirmed feverfew contact allergy against two PD-feverfew-containing creams. Four of the seven patients tested positive to one of the creams, and that "reactivity was associated with simultaneous positive reactions to parthenolide." When the reactive cream was chemically re-analyzed about two years after the initial testing, no parthenolide was detected, "probably because of degradation of the compound." The authors concluded that "topical products containing parthenolide-depleted feverfew extracts may elicit positive patch test reactions in feverfew-sensitive patients," though "the reactivity is lost over time."[^paulsen]

**General feverfew antioxidant data, independent of the cosmetic manufacturer.** Michalak and colleagues compared hydroethanolic extracts of feverfew flower, leaf and whole herb (not the parthenolide-depleted cosmetic extract) for antioxidant and skin-cell activity. Flower extract gave the strongest DPPH radical-scavenging result (84.09% plus or minus 0.42%, versus 69.54% plus or minus 0.69% for herb extract and 64.73% plus or minus 0.44% for leaf extract) and the strongest pro-proliferative effect on cultured fibroblasts and keratinocytes, most notably at 1.0% and 2.5% concentrations; extracts below a 2.5% concentration showed the strongest antioxidant properties on skin cells. The review also notes parthenolide can comprise "up to 85% of the total sesquiterpene content" of the whole plant, and that the sesquiterpene-lactone fraction as a whole is "the most important for biological activity," which is why the cosmetic version is specifically depleted of parthenolide.[^michalak]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Soothes redness."** The only redness data are small, single-laboratory trials and models run by the extract's own developer, testing short-term UV- or chemically-triggered flushing, not a diagnosed redness condition such as rosacea.[^martin][^sur]
- **"Hypoallergenic" or "safe for sensitive, allergy-prone skin."** An independent patch-test study found feverfew-allergic patients still reacted to some finished PD-feverfew creams, tied to residual parthenolide that can vary batch to batch and degrade with age.[^paulsen]
- **"10 times the antioxidant power of Vitamin C."** The only comparison to Vitamin C in the peer-reviewed record is a cell-free radical-scavenging assay describing PD-feverfew as having "greater activity than Vitamin C," without a stated multiple.[^martin]
