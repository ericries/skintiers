Oral tablets, not something you put on your skin, and the first-line drug class for hives. **Bottom line:** this is one of the better-evidenced interventions on this site, with a labelled FDA indication and a 24-trial network meta-analysis behind it, and it still does not work for a large share of patients, which is why a whole guideline exists for the ones it fails. The condition is at [[chronic-spontaneous-urticaria]].

> **The label is more modest than the reputation.** One over-the-counter cetirizine product states its use as to "reduces hives and relieves itching due to hives (urticaria)", in the label's own grammar, and then says plainly: "This product will not prevent hives or an allergic skin reaction from occurring."[^otc] Prescription cetirizine oral solution is indicated "for the treatment of the uncomplicated skin manifestations of chronic idiopathic urticaria".[^rx] Uncomplicated, and treatment rather than prevention, are the operative words.

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: real but variable, and the ranking does not favour the familiar names.** A 2026 network meta-analysis pooled "24 randomized clinical trials (RCTs) involving 5,172 patients" of second-generation antihistamines in chronic spontaneous urticaria.[^nma] Ebastine ranked first on total symptom score, at a standardised mean difference of "-2.80 [95% confidence interval (CI):-5.12 to -0.47]", and olopatadine first on wheal score at "-0.84 [95% CI: -1.37 to -0.32]".[^nma] Neither is cetirizine, loratadine or fexofenadine, which are the ones most people actually take. The confidence interval on that first estimate spans an order of magnitude, so the ranking is softer than a league table implies.
- **Evidence quality: good, and better than almost anything else graded here.** A pooled body of 24 randomised trials, an FDA-labelled indication for the skin manifestations of chronic urticaria, and first-line status in the international guideline the meta-analysis cites.[^nma][^rx] That combination is rare on this site.
- **Safety: the class differences are smaller than marketing suggests.** The same analysis concludes "There are no meaningful differences in safety risks between different second-generation antihistamines", while noting bepotastine had significantly less somnolence than the others, at an odds ratio of "0.15 [95% CI: 0.03-0.69]".[^nma] The non-drowsy framing is a class property, not a reason to pick one brand over another.
- **Record finding: the failure case is common enough to have its own guideline.** A separate 2025 clinical practice guideline is titled for "H1 antihistamine-resistant Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria".[^resistant] A drug class does not get a dedicated guideline for its non-responders unless there are a lot of them. Anyone whose hives persist on a standard dose is in a recognised, documented group rather than an unusual one.
- **Route: oral, and that is the point.** Nothing here is topical. It is included because the condition it treats is a skin condition the site covers, and because people reach for a cream first when the evidence sits with a tablet.

## The Evidence

One network meta-analysis, read from its abstract, plus two FDA labels. The analysis searched PubMed, Embase and the Cochrane Central Register up to March 2025 and took change from baseline in total symptom score as its primary outcome, with pruritus and wheal scores secondary.[^nma]

This page does not cover dose escalation, which the guidelines discuss and which goes beyond what the labels above authorise. It also does not cover first-generation sedating antihistamines, which are a different safety proposition.

> **Bottom line.** Well evidenced, genuinely first line, and openly imperfect. Graded `good` rather than `best` because the pooled estimates are wide and a large documented minority do not respond.
