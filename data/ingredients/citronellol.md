A rose-scented terpene alcohol used as a fragrance ingredient, and one of the components of fragrance mix II.[^ox] It is in a formula to make it smell, not to do anything to skin, which is why the grade here is `weak`. **Bottom line:** as with [[geraniol]], what sensitises people is mostly the OXIDISED form, and the practical twist is that the standard patch test does not catch all of it.

> **Oxidising it multiplies the positive patch tests, and fragrance mix II misses some of them.** Across 658 dermatitis patients, "Purified citronellol detected positive reactions in 0.15%-0.31% of patients, while oxidized citronellol detected positive reactions in 0.61%-4.5%."[^ox] The authors conclude that "Oxidized citronellol detects more cases of contact allergy than purified citronellol, and these cases are not all detected using fragrance mix II."[^ox]

## The Rubric

**Fragrance ingredient, not a skin-health active**

- **Effect size on skin: none claimed.** Citronellol is there for scent. No efficacy evidence was sought, because no benefit is advertised for it.
- **Tolerability: a real allergen, but an uncommon one in absolute terms.** Even the oxidised form topped out at 4.5% of tested patients, and the purified form sat near 0.2%.[^ox] These were dermatitis patients being patch-tested, not the general public, so the true population rate is lower still.
- **Oxidation is the whole story, exactly as with [[geraniol]].** Terpenes autoxidise on contact with air. The oxidised form here tested positive several times as often as the purified one.[^ox] An old, frequently opened bottle is not the same chemical proposition as a fresh one.
- **It travels in a pack, and tightly.** Among patients reacting to oxidised citronellol, "75%-91%" also reacted to oxidised linalool or oxidised limonene.[^ox] That is a stronger co-reaction rate than this site has recorded for any other fragrance allergen pair. If one of the three is a problem, treat all three as suspect.
- **The diagnostic gap is the useful part.** Because fragrance mix II does not catch every case, a negative standard patch test does not fully rule citronellol out.[^ox] That is worth knowing before concluding a fragrance is safe for you.

## The Evidence

One well-matched primary study, and it answers the only question worth asking about a scent molecule: who reacts to it, and in what form.

**Hagvall and colleagues, 2020**, patch-tested 658 dermatitis patients with both purified and oxidised citronellol at several concentrations, and pre-tested for irritancy so that irritant reactions would not be mistaken for allergic ones.[^ox] The design is the reason the comparison is trustworthy: the same patients met both forms.

What the study does not establish: how often citronellol causes problems in people who are not already at a dermatitis clinic. No such figure is quoted here because none was found.

## Common Marketing Claims

There are none to quarantine, and that absence is itself the point. No brand advertises citronellol as a benefit; where it is named at all it appears in an ingredient or allergen-disclosure list rather than in marketing copy. A scent molecule with a measurable sensitisation rate and no claimed upside is judged entirely on tolerability.

## Who should care

Anyone who already reacts to fragrance, and particularly anyone who has tested positive to oxidised [[limonene]] or oxidised [[linalool]], given the co-reaction rates above. Everybody else can treat it as what it is: a smell.

Products here that declare it include [[thayers-rose-petal-facial-toner]], [[bio-oil-skincare-oil]], [[caudalie-vinopure-acne-serum]], [[lancome-advanced-genifique-serum]], [[necessaire-the-body-retinol]] and [[skin1004-madagascar-centella-air-fit-suncream]].
