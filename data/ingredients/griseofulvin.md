Griseofulvin is an oral antifungal, dispensed by prescription as a tablet or suspension and never as a topical or cosmetic ingredient. Its FDA-approved indications are dermatophyte ("ringworm") infections of the scalp, body, groin, feet, beard, and nails, and it has historically been the standard treatment for tinea capitis (scalp ringworm, most often in children).[^fda] It is a fungistatic drug derived from a species of *Penicillium* that is "deposited in the keratin precursor cells" so that newly grown hair, skin, and nail "becomes highly resistant to fungal invasions," which is why it must be taken for weeks while healthy keratin grows in.[^fda]

> Griseofulvin is a well-evidenced oral antifungal for dermatophyte (ringworm) infections, especially tinea capitis. A meta-analysis found it comparable overall to the newer terbinafine but better for Microsporum species, while terbinafine is better for Trichophyton.[^tey] It is a prescription systemic drug, not a skincare ingredient, and it does nothing for the Malassezia-yeast conditions a skincare audience usually asks about ([[fungal-acne]], seborrheic dermatitis), which need an azole such as [[ketoconazole]].

## The Rubric

**Dermatophyte ("ringworm") infection, especially tinea capitis (oral, prescription)**
- **Effect size: curative for susceptible dermatophyte infections.** In a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, griseofulvin's complete cure rate was comparable overall to oral [[terbinafine]], "odds ratio = 1.22 favoring terbinafine (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.785-1.919; P = .37)," but subgroup analysis found "terbinafine was more efficacious than griseofulvin in treating Trichophyton species (1.616; 95% CI = 1.274-2.051; P < .001) and griseofulvin was more efficacious than terbinafine in treating Microsporum species (0.408; 95% CI = 0.254-0.656; P < .001)."[^tey]
- **Evidence quality: good.** Seven randomized controlled trials involving 2163 subjects were pooled in the meta-analysis; the trials varied, and griseofulvin requires a longer course than terbinafine (mean 8 weeks, range 6 to 12, versus terbinafine's mean 4 weeks).[^tey]

## How It Works

Griseofulvin is a fungistatic antibiotic derived from a species of *Penicillium* that disrupts fungal mitosis and is "deposited in the keratin precursor cells" of hair, skin, and nails, where it has "a greater affinity for diseased tissue" and binds tightly to new keratin, which "becomes highly resistant to fungal invasions."[^fda] Because it works by making newly formed keratin resistant rather than killing fungus already present in old keratin, treatment must continue for weeks until infected hair or skin is replaced, and absorption is improved when taken with a fatty meal.[^fda]

## The Evidence

The best efficacy evidence is a meta-analysis of seven randomized controlled trials (2163 subjects) comparing oral griseofulvin and oral [[terbinafine]] for tinea capitis in immunocompetent patients.[^tey] Overall complete cure rates did not differ significantly between the two drugs, but the clinically useful finding is the split by dermatophyte: terbinafine was significantly more effective against Trichophyton species (the predominant cause of tinea capitis in much of North America and Europe), while griseofulvin was significantly more effective against Microsporum species.[^tey] That species dependence, plus griseofulvin's longer required course, is why terbinafine has largely replaced it as first-line for Trichophyton tinea capitis while griseofulvin remains preferred for Microsporum. Its FDA-labeled indications also cover tinea corporis, cruris, pedis, barbae, and unguium (nail) caused by susceptible dermatophytes, though for nail infections terbinafine is now the more effective oral option (see [[onychomycosis]]).[^fda]
