Centella asiatica is a tropical medicinal herb whose active pentacyclic triterpenes (asiaticoside, madecassoside, asiatic acid, and madecassic acid) are used topically for wound healing and, in modern cosmetics, in the soothing creams often labelled "cica."[^bylka]

The plant has a long traditional record for small wounds, burns, and scars, and a 2014 dermatology overview describes that literature as focused on "improvement of the healing process of small wounds, hypertrophic scars and burns."[^bylka] The best-characterized part of the story is mechanistic: its triterpenes stimulate collagen and connective-tissue synthesis in cultured skin cells, and the extract has anti-inflammatory activity in the lab.[^lu][^bonte][^whsr] The controlled clinical record is much thinner than the marketing implies. A systematic review of Centella for wound healing found only four clinical trials and could not pool them, the one human anti-aging trial paired it with vitamin C so its own effect cannot be isolated, and the current Cochrane review found no high-quality evidence that Centella-containing creams prevent stretch marks.[^whsr][^haftek][^cochrane12]

> Centella asiatica has a plausible, well-mapped mechanism for wound healing and collagen support, demonstrated mainly in cell and animal studies, but the human clinical evidence is limited to a handful of small trials for wounds and to confounded or null trials for anti-aging and stretch marks.[^whsr][^haftek][^cochrane12] It is generally well tolerated, though allergic contact dermatitis to the extract is documented.[^acd][^bylka]

## What It Is and How It Works

Centella asiatica (also called gotu kola) is a herbaceous plant whose topical activity is attributed to four triterpene compounds. The 2014 dermatology overview states that "the active compounds include pentacyclic triterpenes," naming asiaticoside, madecassoside, asiatic acid, and madecassic acid.[^bylka] Cosmetic extracts standardized to these triterpenes are the form used in most "cica" products.

The most reproducible mechanism is stimulation of dermal collagen. Using cultured human fibroblasts, one study reported that asiaticoside at 30 micrograms per mL significantly upregulated 54 genes tied to cell proliferation and extracellular-matrix synthesis, including those "encoding type I and type III collagen proteins," with matching increases at the messenger-RNA and protein level.[^lu] An earlier ELISA study of cultured human fibroblasts found that both asiaticoside and madecassoside raised type I collagen secretion by roughly 25 to 30%, and that only madecassoside significantly increased type III collagen (this study was run at a cosmetics-industry laboratory, LVMH Recherche).[^bonte] Synthesizing the preclinical literature, the 2022 wound-healing systematic review attributes the plant's effect to stimulation of "collagen I, Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF) and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF)" and to an anti-inflammatory action seen as reductions in IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, PGE2, and COX-2 in laboratory models.[^whsr] These are the health-relevant biological signals, but they are largely in vitro and animal findings, not human outcomes.

## The Evidence

Primary clinical evidence, by use, separated from the preclinical mechanism above.

**Wound healing.** A 2022 PRISMA systematic review searched four databases specifically for the effect of Centella asiatica on wound healing and reported that "four clinical trials met the inclusion criteria," covering wound contraction, granulation, healing and bleeding time, re-epithelialization, and wound appearance.[^whsr] The review concluded that C. asiatica "might enhance wound healing resulting from improved angiogenesis," but the trials were too few and too heterogeneous to combine, and the authors state that "more studies are needed in order to perform a meta-analysis."[^whsr] The traditional use for small wounds, hypertrophic scars, and burns is likewise reported at the level of an overview rather than pooled controlled data.[^bylka]

**Photoaging (cosmetic).** A randomized, double-blind study of 20 women applied a formula of 5% vitamin C plus 0.1% madecassoside to photoaged skin for six months and reported "significant improvement of the clinical score for deep and superficial wrinkles," suppleness, firmness, and hydration, with structural remodelling of the dermal elastic-fibre network on histology; "two-thirds of the subjects showed an improvement."[^haftek] Because vitamin C, a well-studied antioxidant with its own evidence for photoaging (see [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]), was in the same product, the madecassoside contribution cannot be separated out. The endpoints are cosmetic (wrinkle appearance and firmness), and prescription [[tretinoin]] and the broader [[retinoids]] class carry far stronger controlled anti-aging evidence.

**Stretch marks (striae gravidarum).** The 2012 Cochrane review of topical preparations for preventing stretch marks pooled trials of creams that included Centella-containing formulas and found no significant difference versus placebo or no treatment (average RR 0.74; 95% CI 0.53 to 1.03; five trials, 474 women), concluding there was "no high-quality evidence to support the use of any of the topical preparations."[^cochrane12] An earlier Cochrane review had included a single 100-woman trial of a cream containing Centella asiatica, alpha-tocopherol, and collagen-elastin hydrolysates that reduced stretch marks (OR 0.41; 95% CI 0.17 to 0.99), but only in women "who had previously suffered stretch marks in pregnancy," with no benefit for general use.[^cochrane00] A separate dermatology review of stretch-mark prevention likewise found only "limited evidence that centella" may help.[^korgavkar]

**Tolerability.** The plant is generally well tolerated topically, but it is not inert: a 2024 report documented allergic contact dermatitis to Centella asiatica extract in two patients, and the dermatology overview also notes potential adverse effects.[^acd][^bylka]

## The Rubric

Grades differ by use and are cosmetic where noted. Framing below is relative and names comparators.

**Wound healing and minor burns (health)**
- **Effect size: modest, on limited clinical evidence.** This is the traditional and best-studied use, and the mechanism (collagen synthesis, angiogenesis, anti-inflammatory action) is reproducible in cell and animal work.[^lu][^bonte][^whsr] But the controlled human record is thin: a dedicated systematic review found only four clinical trials and could not meta-analyze them.[^whsr] "Modest and preliminary. Plausible and mechanistically supported, but far below the evidence base of standard wound dressings, and unlike prescription topicals it is not a regulator-recognized wound drug in the US."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Consistent preclinical signals, few and small human trials, no pooled clinical estimate.[^whsr]

**Skin soothing and anti-inflammatory ("cica", cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: not clinically established.** The anti-inflammatory mechanism (reduced IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, PGE2, COX-2) is documented in laboratory models,[^whsr] which is the basis for "cica" positioning, but no controlled trial isolating Centella for redness or barrier soothing is cited here. "Mechanistically plausible but unproven clinically. [[niacinamide]] and [[colloidal-oatmeal]], both with their own human soothing and barrier evidence, are better-evidenced comparators."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** In vitro mechanism without an isolating human trial.[^whsr]

**Photoaging / wrinkles (cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: not established for Centella alone.** The one supporting RCT (n=20) delivered madecassoside together with 5% vitamin C, so the benefit cannot be attributed to the herb, and the endpoints are cosmetic.[^haftek] "Unresolved. Prescription [[tretinoin]] and the [[retinoids]] class have much stronger, isolated anti-aging evidence."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** A single small confounded trial.[^haftek]

**Stretch-mark prevention (striae gravidarum)**
- **Effect size: none demonstrated by high-quality evidence.** The current Cochrane review found no significant effect (RR 0.74; 95% CI 0.53 to 1.03) and no high-quality supporting evidence.[^cochrane12] "None demonstrated. One older single trial of a Centella-containing cream suggested benefit only in women with prior stretch marks (OR 0.41), not confirmed in the larger pooled analysis."[^cochrane00][^cochrane12]
- **Evidence quality: mixed, leaning negative.** A null pooled Cochrane result against one earlier positive combination-cream trial.[^cochrane12][^cochrane00]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Cica: heals and calms the skin."** The anti-inflammatory mechanism is real in laboratory models,[^whsr] and the wound-healing tradition is genuine, but the supporting clinical evidence for wounds is four small trials with no pooled estimate,[^whsr] and no cited controlled trial isolates Centella for facial redness or barrier soothing. The name "cica" derives from this wound (cicatrisation) heritage, not from a proven cosmetic soothing trial.
- **"Boosts collagen and reverses aging."** Asiaticoside and madecassoside do raise type I collagen synthesis in cultured fibroblasts by about 25 to 30%,[^bonte][^lu] but that is a cell-culture result. The only human anti-aging trial paired madecassoside with vitamin C and reported cosmetic endpoints, so it cannot show Centella reverses aging on its own.[^haftek]
- **"Prevents stretch marks."** The current Cochrane review found "no high-quality evidence to support the use of any of the topical preparations" for stretch marks, Centella-containing creams included.[^cochrane12]
- **"Natural, so gentle and hypoallergenic."** It is usually well tolerated, but allergic contact dermatitis to Centella asiatica extract is documented in the dermatology literature.[^acd][^bylka] Botanical origin is not a guarantee against sensitization.
