Beta-glucan is a polysaccharide built from repeating glucose units, drawn for skincare from yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*), oats, barley, or mushrooms; a 2018 review notes the class is "derived from a variety of sources including yeast, grain and fungus" and grouped as a biological response modifier.[^majtan] It is marketed as a hydrating, [[moisturizing]]-adjacent ingredient and as a soothing agent for irritated or slow-healing skin, but the human evidence behind either claim is thin: the cosmetic hydration case rests on a single old manufacturer study with no reported numbers, and the wound-healing case is strongest in medical ulcer trials, not ordinary skincare use.

> The best-controlled human trial found a beta-glucan gel sped healing of diabetic leg ulcers at 8 weeks (44% healed vs 17% on placebo, P=0.03), but the same trial's headline outcome, time to complete healing, was not statistically significant (36 vs 63 days, P=0.130).[^zykova] A separate open trial of a mushroom-derived beta-glucan cream improved itch and eczema-severity scores in atopic dermatitis as an add-on therapy, but it was not blinded.[^jesenak] The manufacturer study behind the common "improves skin hydration and barrier function" marketing claim reports no quantitative results in its published abstract.[^zulli]

## The Rubric

**Wound healing and ulcer care (topical, medical use)**
- **Effect size: modest and inconsistent.** In a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in diabetic leg ulcers, a soluble yeast beta-glucan gel beat placebo on healing at week 8 (44% vs 17%, P=0.03), but not on the trial's primary time-to-healing endpoint (36 vs 63 days, P=0.130) or on the proportion healed by 12 weeks (59% vs 37%, P=0.09).[^zykova] *Some weeks and endpoints favored the glucan gel over placebo, but the headline time-to-healing result did not reach significance.* This is a specific medical wound-gel formulation used on chronic ulcers under clinical supervision, not evidence for an everyday moisturizer or serum.
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One placebo-controlled human RCT (60 patients) with mixed, mostly non-significant results, plus an uncontrolled case report of venous-ulcer improvement and mechanistic/animal work.[^zykova][^medeiros][^majtan]

**Eczema and irritation, as an add-on (topical)**
- **Effect size: modest.** A multicenter, split-body trial of a mushroom-derived (pleuran) beta-glucan cream, added to standard care in 105 atopic-dermatitis patients, found lower itch (VAS 1.68 vs 1.95, P<0.001) and lower EASI severity scores (1.57 vs 1.85, P<0.001) on the treated side.[^jesenak] *A real numeric improvement in a diagnosed-eczema add-on trial, not evidence for general soothing of undiagnosed irritation.*
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One open-label, unblinded, split-body trial with no separate placebo cream; a design more prone to bias than a blinded RCT.[^jesenak]

**Skin hydration and barrier function ("skin function") claims (topical, cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: unquantified.** The manufacturer study most often cited for beta-glucan's cosmetic hydration and barrier claims reports that carboxymethylated yeast glucan promoted keratinocyte growth in cultured cells and, in placebo-controlled work in healthy volunteers, protected against UV-A and detergent-induced damage, but its published abstract gives no percentages, sample size, or statistics.[^zulli] *The claim traces to one old industry study whose own abstract contains no reportable numbers.*
- **Evidence quality: preliminary and manufacturer-run.** A single 1998 study from Mibelle AG Cosmetics; no independent controlled human trial measuring hydration, transepidermal water loss, or elasticity as its own endpoint was found.[^zulli]

## The Evidence

**Wound healing and ulcer care.** A double-blind, placebo-controlled trial randomized 60 patients with type 1 or 2 diabetes and lower-extremity ulcers (Wagner grade 1-2) to a soluble yeast beta-1,3/1,6-glucan (SBG) gel or a methylcellulose placebo, applied three times weekly for up to 12 weeks alongside conventional wound management. Median time to complete healing was 36 days with SBG versus 63 days with placebo (P=0.130); ulcers healed by week 12 were 59% (SBG) versus 37% (placebo, P=0.09); but weekly percentage reduction in ulcer size significantly favored SBG at weeks 1-2, 3-4, and 5-6 (P<0.05 each), and healing at week 8 was 44% versus 17% (P=0.03). The authors concluded the preparation "shows promising potential as a treatment accelerating cutaneous healing," short of a definitive result on the trial's own primary endpoint.[^zykova] A smaller, uncontrolled report applied purified yeast (1→3)-beta-glucan to venous leg ulcers; one wound that had not healed for over 15 years shrank 67.8% in area during treatment, but the study had no placebo arm and reported no statistics.[^medeiros] A 2018 review of the ingredient class describes beta-glucans acting on macrophages, keratinocytes, and fibroblasts to drive "tissue granulation, collagen deposition and reepithelialization," a mechanism built mostly on in vitro and animal work.[^majtan] A separate cell-culture study found barley beta-glucan caused "a short-transitory proliferation arrest" in human dermal fibroblasts while increasing their migration at 24 hours, and the authors linked this to faster wound closure they observed in mouse skin, again not a human clinical result.[^fuste]

**Eczema and irritation.** A multicenter, open (unblinded) split-body trial enrolled 105 patients with mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis, applying a beta-glucan cream (pleuran, from the oyster mushroom *Pleurotus ostreatus*) to one side of the body alongside standard care; 80 patients (76.2%) completed the study. Itch fell more on the treated side (VAS 1.68 vs 1.95, P<0.001) and EASI severity scores were lower (1.57 vs 1.85, P<0.001), with the cream reported as well tolerated. The authors called it "the first study evaluating and confirming the potential use of beta-glucan-based cream as a supportive complementary therapy of atopic dermatitis."[^jesenak]

**Skin hydration and barrier claims.** The most-cited cosmetic-industry source is a 1998 study from Mibelle AG Cosmetics on carboxymethylated yeast glucan (CM-glucan), reporting in placebo-controlled work with cultured skin cells and healthy volunteers that it promoted keratinocyte growth, protected against UV-A and detergent-induced damage, and increased stratum-corneum renewal; the published abstract states no percentages, sample sizes, or statistical results behind those findings, stating only that "the use of non-specific immune-stimulators, such as glucan, is a new approach for improving the function of stressed skin."[^zulli] No independent, controlled human trial measuring beta-glucan's effect on skin hydration, transepidermal water loss, or elasticity as its own endpoint was found.

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Deeply hydrating" / "boosts the moisture barrier."** The industry study behind this framing reports no quantitative hydration, TEWL, or barrier results in its published abstract, and it is manufacturer-run.[^zulli] No independent controlled human hydration trial was found.
- **"Heals and repairs skin" on a serum or cream.** The positive numeric wound-healing results come from a specific medical glucan gel tested on diabetic and venous ulcers under clinical supervision, not from a cosmetic moisturizer or serum, and the trial's own primary time-to-healing endpoint was not statistically significant.[^zykova]
- **"Soothes irritated and sensitive skin."** The controlled evidence is for diagnosed atopic dermatitis as an add-on to standard treatment in an unblinded trial, not for general skin sensitivity or irritation in the absence of a diagnosed condition.[^jesenak]
