Methyl estradiolpropanoate (INCI name MEP) is a synthetic estradiol derivative developed and marketed exclusively as the active in Biopelle/Ferndale Pharma Group's Emepelle line, sold as a "non-hormonal" way to treat what its developers call "estrogen deficient skin" (EDS) in postmenopausal women.[^draelos] Its central claim, that it activates skin estrogen receptors but is "metabolized in the skin to an inactive compound avoiding estrogen side effects," comes from the manufacturer's own pilot trial, and all three published human studies of MEP to date test its manufacturer's branded products, so the evidence for its signature "soft estrogen" mechanism has never been examined by an independent lab.[^draelos][^cohen2019][^cohen2022] A 2024 independent systematic review of anti-aging cosmeceuticals graded MEP a C, its second-lowest tier, behind retinol and vitamin C's Grade A.[^lau]

> The one double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial of MEP reported statistically significant improvement over vehicle at 14 weeks in dryness (P<0.001), laxity (P=0.001), atrophy (P=0.003), and dullness (P<0.001), but only 4 of the 9 subjects in the biopsy substudy showed any increase in fibroblast estrogen-receptor staining, and the trial did not report its total enrollment in the published abstract.[^draelos]

## The Rubric

**Estrogen-deficient skin: dryness, laxity, atrophy, dullness (topical, postmenopausal women)**
- **Effect size: modest per the sponsor's own pilot, unquantified elsewhere.** Draelos reported statistically significant improvement from baseline at week 14 in dryness, laxity, atrophy, and dullness versus vehicle, but the abstract gives no effect-size numbers (percent change, score deltas) for these endpoints, only p-values, so the magnitude of benefit cannot be assessed from the published record.[^draelos] The two Cohen open-label studies report GAIS and hydration-scale improvements (up to 93% to 100% of subjects noting improvement on subjective, unblinded scales) but had no vehicle or placebo arm, so nothing separates a genuine MEP effect from the moisturizing base, regular skincare, or expectation bias.[^cohen2019][^cohen2022]
- **Evidence quality: weak. One small, industry-authored, vehicle-controlled pilot plus two open-label case series, all testing the same manufacturer's branded product, with no independent replication.** The only controlled trial is Draelos's "pilot study," whose own title flags it as preliminary; the biopsy substudy (n=9) found the proposed mechanism, increased fibroblast estrogen-receptor expression, in fewer than half of subjects.[^draelos] The Cohen 2019 study enrolled 14 women in an open, uncontrolled single-site design, and the Cohen/Downie 2022 eye-cream study enrolled 31 women, also open-label with no comparator.[^cohen2019][^cohen2022] No study has been conducted by researchers outside the commercial relationship with Emepelle's manufacturer, and an independent 2024 systematic review assigned MEP a Grade C recommendation, its own conclusion that the evidence, while not disqualifying, remains thin.[^lau]

## How It Works

MEP is positioned as a "non-hormonal estrogen receptor activator": Draelos describes it as metabolized in skin to an inactive compound, which is the manufacturer's rationale for claiming estrogen-like effects on dermal fibroblasts and epidermal keratinocytes without systemic hormonal exposure.[^draelos] The stated biological premise, that estrogen receptors on dermal fibroblasts and keratinocytes decline with menopause and that reactivating them could restore collagen, hydration, and elasticity, is a plausible extension of known estrogen-skin biology, but the specific claim that MEP itself binds and activates those receptors, and is then locally deactivated before reaching meaningful systemic exposure, rests on the manufacturer's own pharmacology rather than an independently published mechanistic study.[^draelos] The one attempt to biopsy-confirm receptor engagement found increased fibroblast estrogen-receptor staining in only 4 of 9 subjects, a result the published abstract does not contextualize against a vehicle-treated biopsy comparison.[^draelos] PubChem carries MEP as CID 171390764 (molecular formula C22H30O4, molecular weight 358.5), a 2-methylpropanoate (isobutyrate) ester of estradiol, which confirms its chemical identity as a synthetic estradiol derivative but says nothing about the "soft estrogen" local-deactivation story, which is a pharmacology claim rather than a structural one.

## The Evidence

**Facial estrogen-deficient skin, double-blind, vehicle-controlled pilot (topical).** Draelos ran a "double-blind randomized pilot study" applying topical MEP to the face twice daily for 14 weeks in postmenopausal women who had never taken hormone replacement therapy, comparing it against vehicle. The investigator noted "statistically significant improvement from baseline at week 14 in dryness (P<0.001), laxity (P=0.001), atrophy (P=0.003), and dullness (P<0.001) as compared to vehicle." In a biopsy substudy, "four of nine subjects" showed increased fibroblast estrogen-receptor staining. The published abstract does not state total study enrollment, dropout rate, or effect-size magnitudes beyond p-values.[^draelos] *This is the only controlled human trial of MEP and it found a statistically significant benefit over vehicle on several subjective aging measures, but it is explicitly labeled a pilot, provides no size-of-effect numbers, and its own mechanistic substudy confirmed the estrogen-receptor story in fewer than half the biopsied subjects.*

**Emepelle Serum and Night Cream regimen, open-label (topical, combination).** Cohen enrolled 14 women aged 53 to 68, amenorrheic 1 to 10 years, with at least moderate wrinkling and elastosis, in a single-site, uncontrolled study of Emepelle Serum (morning) and Night Cream (evening) over 20 weeks. By week 20, "100% of study participants... showed at least one-grade improvement" in a facial hydration scale, and investigator clinical assessment scored a 53% improvement in texture, 21% in keratoses, and 15% in laxity on the Alexiades-Armenakas grading scale.[^cohen2019] *An uncontrolled, unblinded case series in 14 women reported large subjective and investigator-rated improvements, but with no vehicle or untreated comparator, none of this can be attributed specifically to MEP rather than to moisturization, sun protection counseling, or rater and patient expectation.*

**Emepelle Eye Cream, open-label periorbital study (topical).** Cohen and Downie tested Emepelle Eye Cream in 31 women who had completed the study, using clinician- and self-rated Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale (GAIS) scores. "The averaged clinical GAIS improvement for all subjects who completed the study improved by 21%," and subjects reported satisfaction with periorbital appearance improved by 47%; the product was well tolerated with no significant side effects reported.[^cohen2022] *Another open-label, uncontrolled study of a branded MEP product reporting modest-to-moderate subjective improvement; useful as a tolerability signal but not as efficacy evidence given the lack of a comparator arm.*

**Independent systematic review, Grade C (context).** A 2024 systematic review of cosmeceuticals for antiaging, using Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine grading and searching PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane, included MEP among 32 studies covering multiple ingredients. Retinol and vitamin C "received a Grade A for recommendation," while "methyl estradiolpropanoate, bakuchiol, tetrahydrojasmonic acid, and growth factors received a recommendation grade of C."[^lau] *An outside academic review, not funded by or authored with Emepelle's manufacturer, placed MEP in the same middling evidence tier as bakuchiol and topical growth factors, well below retinol and vitamin C, which is a reasonable independent check on the manufacturer-funded trial data above.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"A non-hormonal way to treat estrogen-deficient skin."** The "non-hormonal" framing rests entirely on the manufacturer's own claim that MEP is metabolized locally to an inactive compound before reaching systemic circulation; no independent pharmacokinetic or systemic-hormone-level study supporting that claim was found in the published record reviewed here, so "non-hormonal" should be read as an unverified manufacturer safety claim rather than an independently confirmed fact.[^draelos]
- **"Clinically proven to reverse estrogen deficient skin."** Overstated. The single controlled trial is a small pilot with unstated enrollment and no effect-size figures beyond p-values, its own biopsy substudy confirmed the proposed receptor mechanism in a minority of subjects, and the two supporting studies are open-label case series of the manufacturer's own products with no comparator arm.[^draelos][^cohen2019][^cohen2022]
