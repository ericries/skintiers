"Sunscreen" is a **category**, not an ingredient. It is a delivery vehicle for one or more UV filters (mineral filters such as zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, and organic filters such as avobenzone, octocrylene, and octinoxate). Each of those filters has its own absorption profile, stability, and safety questions. The question the category-level randomized evidence can actually answer is **does regular sunscreen use work?** What SPF does and does not measure, and why the filter set matters, follows from there. The honest answer on efficacy is that regular use is one of the very few skincare practices with randomized-trial support against hard clinical endpoints (photoaging and skin cancer), but that support traces largely to a *single* long-running Australian field trial, and an independent systematic review is markedly more cautious.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The Rubric

Grades are for **regular, real-world use of a broad-spectrum sunscreen**, not for any specific product's SPF label or marketing. Framing is relative and names comparators.

**Photoaging prevention (fine lines, texture, pigment mottling)**
- **Effect size: strong.** In the one randomized field trial to test it, adults assigned to daily sunscreen showed 24% less skin aging over 4.5 years than those using it at their discretion (relative odds 0.76; 95% CI 0.59 to 0.98; n=903), and the daily group "showed no detectable increase in skin aging" at all.[^1] "Strong. The single best-supported anti-aging step in skincare: the only intervention demonstrated by a randomized trial with a blinded, multi-year aging endpoint. Tretinoin has RCT support too, but from shorter vehicle-controlled wrinkle trials, not a years-long field trial of actual daily use."
- **Evidence quality: gold-standard (with one big caveat).** Randomized, controlled, with an adjudicated skin-aging endpoint, the rarest evidence tier in this field. The caveat: it rests substantially on a single cohort (the Nambour trial, southeast Queensland).[^1]

**Skin cancer prevention: melanoma**
- **Effect size: notable but imprecise.** In post-trial follow-up, the daily-sunscreen group had 11 new primary melanomas versus 22 in the discretionary group (HR 0.50; 95% CI 0.24 to 1.02; P=.051, a halving that just misses significance), with a stronger signal for invasive melanoma (3 vs 11; HR 0.27; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.97).[^2] "Notable. One of only a handful of skincare interventions ever tested against a hard cancer endpoint in an RCT, but the confidence intervals are wide and the invasive-melanoma count is tiny."
- **Evidence quality: gold-standard design, moderate certainty.** An RCT with a long-term cancer endpoint is exceptional evidence for skincare; the limitation is a single cohort and imprecise estimates.[^2]

**Skin cancer prevention: keratinocyte cancers (squamous- and basal-cell)**
- **Effect size: modest to notable for SCC, none demonstrated for BCC.** Over 4.5 years, squamous-cell *tumor burden* fell in the daily group (28 tumors in 22 people vs 46 tumors in 25 people; tumor rate ratio 0.61; 95% CI 0.46 to 0.81).[^3] But when an independent Cochrane review counted *people* who developed at least one cSCC, the difference was not significant (RR 0.88; 95% CI 0.50 to 1.54), and basal-cell carcinoma was unaffected in every analysis (RR ~1.03).[^3][^4] "Modest. A real reduction in SCC tumor burden; no demonstrated BCC benefit."
- **Evidence quality: solid for SCC tumor counts, low overall.** The Cochrane review graded the keratinocyte-cancer evidence "low quality" and concluded it was "unable to demonstrate … whether sunscreen was effective for the prevention of basal cell carcinoma (BCC) or cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)."[^4]

## What We Actually Know

Regular use of a broad-spectrum sunscreen has genuine randomized-trial support for **slowing photoaging** and **reducing melanoma and squamous-cell tumor burden**, a level of evidence almost nothing else in skincare can claim, because these are among the only skincare RCTs with hard clinical endpoints rather than short-term cosmetic scores.[^1][^2][^3] Two honest qualifiers belong on every one of these claims. First, the landmark trial evidence comes overwhelmingly from **one cohort** (the Nambour trial in Australia); Hughes 2013, Green 2011, and Green 1999 are all the same ~1,600-person population analyzed for different endpoints, so this is one trial reporting three outcomes rather than three independent trials.[^1][^2][^3] Second, when the evidence is synthesized *independently*, it looks weaker: the 2016 Cochrane review found "no difference" at the person level for either keratinocyte cancer and rated the evidence low-quality.[^4] The strongest single claim, photoaging prevention, is also the best-supported, because it is a within-trial randomized comparison rather than a rare-event count. The benefit is for **regular, adequately applied** use; it is not a property of any SPF number on a label, and real-world application is typically far below the amount used to earn that number.[^5]

## How It Works

UV filters reduce the ultraviolet dose reaching living skin. Mineral filters (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) largely absorb and scatter UV; organic filters absorb UV and re-emit it as heat. Ultraviolet B (roughly 290 to 320 nm) is the main driver of sunburn and of the DNA photodamage (pyrimidine dimers) implicated in keratinocyte cancers; ultraviolet A (roughly 320 to 400 nm) penetrates deeper into the dermis and is the principal driver of photoaging. "Broad spectrum" means a product provides meaningful protection across both UVA and UVB rather than UVB alone, which matters because SPF, a sunburn (UVB) metric, does not by itself guarantee UVA coverage. The mechanism by which sunscreen prevents the graded outcomes is therefore straightforward and non-controversial: it lowers the cumulative UV dose that causes photoaging and UV-induced skin cancers. Critically, the protection actually delivered depends on how much product is applied: SPF is measured at a laboratory thickness of 2 mg/cm², whereas people apply far less in practice.[^5]

## What SPF measures, and what "broad spectrum" certifies

SPF is a UVB number. "SPF, or sun protective factor, is a relative measure of how long a product protects one from UVB only; it makes no reference to protection from other light wavelengths," per a 2020 review in the Journal of Drugs in Dermatology.[^6] A dermatology review in the Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology makes the same point: "UVB protection is measured by a product's SPF."[^7] Sunburn, and the DNA photodamage that drives keratinocyte cancers, are mainly UVB effects. UVA is a separate axis. It penetrates deeper and is the principal driver of photoaging: UVA "up regulates the formation of matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs), enzymes that degrade the matrix protein's elastin and collagen, which, if not prevented, can result in marked reduction in skin elasticity and increased wrinkling," and it is also a contributor to skin cancer.[^7]

Because SPF ignores UVA, a separate test governs the "Broad Spectrum" claim. Under the US FDA sunscreen monograph (21 CFR 201.327), a product earns "Broad Spectrum" through one in vitro measurement: "A mean critical wavelength of 370 nm or greater is classified as broad spectrum protection."[^8] That is a single pass/fail threshold rather than a graded UVA rating, and it is a low bar. Nearly half of US products that clear it would fail a stricter European UVA standard: the 2020 review reports that "49% of US beach and body sunscreens could pass the FDA broad spectrum test but not the European UVA test," and that "US sunscreens allow three times as much UVA penetration as do European sunscreens."[^6] A "Broad Spectrum SPF 60" on a US label therefore certifies strong UVB protection and only a floor of UVA protection, not a specific UVA strength.

## The global filter landscape

How strong a sunscreen's UVA protection actually is depends on which filters it contains, and the approved menu differs by country. The US regulates sunscreens as OTC drugs, and for years its monograph offered a shorter list than the EU, so many US products rely on an older set of filters (avobenzone, homosalate, octocrylene, octisalate). Avobenzone is the workhorse UVA filter in that set. The 2020 review states "avobenzone is the sole chemical approved by the FDA to afford UVA protection," and calls it "highly unstable," which is why formulators add stabilizers such as octocrylene "in an attempt to retard its degradation."[^6]

The filters standard elsewhere are newer. Ecamsule (Mexoryl SX), drometrizole trisiloxane (Mexoryl XL), bemotrizinol (Tinosorb S), and bisoctrizole (Tinosorb M) are all organic UVA filters,[^7] and the 2020 review reports they "offer better protection from UVA and free radicals than avobenzone and are frequently found in Asian, European, and South American sunscreens."[^6] Until recently none was permitted in a US monograph sunscreen. That began to change on June 10, 2026, when the FDA issued a final administrative order adding bemotrizinol to the US OTC sunscreen monograph (M020) at concentrations up to 6% as "generally recognized as safe and effective."[^9] Bisoctrizole and the two Mexoryl filters remain outside the US monograph, and older-filter products already on the shelf are unchanged. The review's own summary of the gap: "American sunscreens remain effective at minimizing sunburn but are more limited in their protection against UVA mediated skin disorders such as photoaging and non-melanoma skin cancers compared to certain international products."[^6]

## The Evidence

All of the randomized clinical-endpoint evidence below comes from the Nambour Skin Cancer Prevention Trial, a community-based RCT of ~1,621 residents of Nambour, southeast Queensland, run 1992 to 1996 with long-term follow-up. It is reported here as one trial with several published endpoints, not as independent replications.

**Photoaging.** Hughes et al. (Annals of Internal Medicine, 2013) randomized 903 adults younger than 55 to daily versus discretionary sunscreen (and, in a factorial design, to β-carotene or placebo). After 4.5 years, "skin aging … was 24% less in the daily sunscreen group than in the discretionary sunscreen group (relative odds, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.59 to 0.98])," and "the daily sunscreen group showed no detectable increase in skin aging after 4.5 years." β-carotene supplementation "had no overall effect on skin aging."[^1]

**Melanoma.** Green et al. (Journal of Clinical Oncology, 2011) followed the full 1,621-person cohort to 2006, roughly a decade after the intervention period. The daily-sunscreen group had 11 new primary melanomas versus 22 in the discretionary group (HR 0.50; 95% CI 0.24 to 1.02; P=.051). The reduction was clearer for invasive melanoma (3 vs 11; HR 0.27; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.97) than for preinvasive disease (HR 0.73; 95% CI 0.29 to 1.81). The authors concluded "melanoma may be preventable by regular sunscreen use in adults."[^2]

**Keratinocyte cancers (SCC/BCC).** Green et al. (Lancet, 1999;354:723-729) reported the 4.5-year primary results: 22 people in the daily-sunscreen groups had 28 incident squamous-cell tumors versus 25 people with 46 tumors in the no-daily-sunscreen groups (tumor rate ratio 0.61; 95% CI 0.46 to 0.81), while basal-cell carcinoma was not reduced (rate ratio ~1.03; 95% CI 0.73 to 1.46). β-carotene had no effect on either cancer.[^3] The independent 2016 Cochrane review re-analyzed this same trial at the person level and "found no difference in terms of the number of participants developing BCC (n=1621; RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.43) or cSCC (n=1621; RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.54)," rated the evidence low-quality, and stated it was "unable to demonstrate … whether sunscreen was effective for the prevention of" either cancer.[^4] The tension between the two, a clear reduction in SCC *tumor burden* but no significant reduction in the *proportion of people* affected, is the honest state of the keratinocyte-cancer evidence.

**Independent synthesis (noted separately from the trial itself).** That the only Cochrane review on the question rests on this single RCT, and reads it cautiously, is itself a finding: the practice is well-founded but under-replicated.[^4]

**Real-world application (the practical ceiling).** SPF is established at 2 mg/cm². Petersen and Wulf (Photodermatology, Photoimmunology & Photomedicine, 2014), reviewing the application literature, note that "sunscreen under natural conditions is applied insufficiently with amounts about 0.39 to 1.0 mg/cm², which decreases the protection factor considerably."[^5] The randomized benefits above were obtained under a trial protocol of daily, supervised, reapplied use, closer to the tested condition than typical consumer behavior.

## Uses

Well-supported: **daily broad-spectrum sunscreen as a photoaging-prevention step**, where the randomized evidence is the strongest in skincare, and as **one component of skin-cancer risk reduction**, particularly for melanoma and squamous-cell tumor burden in high-UV settings. Because this is a category profile, the graded evidence above concerns the practice of regular, adequate application, not any single product's filter set or label claim. Which filters a product uses still matters for real UVA coverage, as the mechanism and global-landscape sections above explain.

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Blue-light / HEV-light protection."** The randomized clinical-endpoint evidence graded here is entirely about ultraviolet radiation. No RCT here shows a photoaging or skin-cancer benefit from filtering visible/blue light, so it is unproven for the outcomes this profile grades.
- **"Reef-safe" (as an efficacy signal).** It is an unregulated environmental term, not an efficacy claim, and carries no information about the product's photoprotection.
- **"SPF 100 protects twice as much as SPF 50."** SPF is a diminishing-returns ratio of blocked erythemal UVB, not a linear one, so SPF 100 does not block twice the UV of SPF 50; the increment at the top of the scale is small. Real-world under-application (0.39 to 1.0 vs the tested 2 mg/cm²) erodes the delivered SPF far more than the gap between high SPF numbers.[^5] SPF is also a UVB-only number and says nothing about UVA coverage.[^6]
- **"All-day protection" / "apply once in the morning" / "waterproof."** Contradicted by the applied-dose and wear reality: protection degrades with time, sweat, water, and rubbing, and SPF is measured at a fixed dose that consumers rarely re-establish. The trial benefits came from *daily, reapplied* use, not a single morning application.[^5] No topical is truly waterproof or all-day.
