Adapalene is a synthetic third-generation [[retinoids|retinoid]] and the active ingredient in Differin, first approved by the FDA as a prescription acne drug in 1996 and later sold over the counter. Skincare retinoids sit on a conversion pathway: a retinyl ester is converted to [[retinol]], retinol to retinaldehyde, and retinaldehyde to retinoic acid, the only form that binds the skin's retinoic acid receptors directly. Adapalene sits off that chain. Like [[tretinoin]] (which is retinoic acid itself) and the other synthetic prescription retinoids [[tazarotene]] and [[trifarotene]], it acts on the receptors directly rather than being converted from retinol, and the FDA label describes it as "a naphthoic acid derivative with retinoid-like properties" that "binds to specific retinoic acid nuclear receptors."[^epiduo] Two secondary reviews describe that receptor activity as selective for the beta and gamma retinoic acid receptors rather than binding all three subtypes broadly.[^statpearls][^cosio] Its controlled evidence is for acne, so this profile grades that use and treats photoaging as an off-label, class-level extrapolation.

## The Rubric

Grades differ by use. Framing below is relative and names comparators.

**Acne vulgaris (mild to moderate, facial)**
- **Effect size: modest to notable.** Adapalene is one of the standard FDA-approved topical retinoids for acne, and where it has been tested head to head it lands level with the other first-line topical actives rather than above them. A Cochrane review of topical benzoyl peroxide pooled 5 RCTs (1,472 participants, 11 to 12 weeks) and found "there may be little to no difference between BPO and adapalene" for participant-reported improvement (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.10, low-certainty evidence).[^cochrane-bpo] A meta-analysis of 5 randomized trials (900 patients) reported adapalene 0.1% gel about equal to [[tretinoin]] 0.025% gel in reducing lesion counts, with a faster early response and better local tolerability.[^cunliffe] The vehicle-adjusted effect is modest and typical of the retinoid class, the same band as [[tazarotene]] and [[trifarotene]]; the practical draw is that it matches [[benzoyl-peroxide]] and tretinoin on efficacy while being better tolerated than tretinoin, and it sits alongside [[azelaic-acid]] as a topical acne option. "Modest to notable: level with benzoyl peroxide and tretinoin in head-to-head trials, better tolerated than tretinoin, with a vehicle-adjusted effect that is class-typical rather than class-beating."
- **Evidence quality: solid.** A Cochrane systematic review, a 2022 network meta-analysis of the topical acne field that includes the retinoid class, a pooled analysis of 5 comparative RCTs, and FDA approval across prescription and over-the-counter labels all converge.[^cochrane-bpo][^mavranezouli][^cunliffe][^differin-otc][^epiduo] Short of gold-standard because the benzoyl-peroxide comparison is graded low-certainty and the tretinoin comparison rests on a single pooled analysis of manufacturer-era trials.

**Photoaging / anti-aging (off-label)**
- **Effect size: not established for adapalene specifically.** Adapalene's FDA indication is acne; it is not approved for photoaging, and this profile cites no adapalene-specific controlled anti-aging trial. The general retinoid anti-aging evidence, strongest for prescription [[tretinoin]], lives on [[retinoids]]. As a receptor-acting retinoid adapalene shares that pathway, but it is milder than prescription tretinoin, and any anti-aging use is extrapolation from the class rather than from adapalene's own trials cited here. "Off-label and class-level: milder than prescription tretinoin, with the anti-aging case resting on the retinoid class, not on adapalene-specific evidence."

## What We Actually Know

Adapalene is a synthetic retinoid with FDA-approved evidence for acne. In head-to-head data it holds the retinoid class line rather than beating it: a Cochrane review found little to no difference between benzoyl peroxide and adapalene for participant-reported improvement (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.10),[^cochrane-bpo] and a pooled analysis of 5 comparative RCTs found it about equal to [[tretinoin]] 0.025% gel on lesion reduction, faster in the first week, and better tolerated locally.[^cunliffe] A 2022 network meta-analysis of the topical acne field places the topical retinoid class, adapalene among it, among the effective topical options, though it grouped agents into classes and did not isolate adapalene.[^mavranezouli] Adapalene is also stable enough to be co-formulated with benzoyl peroxide; the fixed combination Epiduo (adapalene 0.1% / benzoyl peroxide 2.5%) is "indicated for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris in patients 12 years of age and older."[^epiduo] One honest limit sits beside this. The benzoyl-peroxide comparison is low-certainty by Cochrane's GRADE assessment.[^cochrane-bpo]

## How It Works

Adapalene is a receptor agonist that needs no metabolic conversion, not a form the skin must oxidize from retinol. The FDA label for the Epiduo combination states that "adapalene binds to specific retinoic acid nuclear receptors but does not bind to cytosolic receptor protein," and, as the label is careful to add, "the significance of these findings with regard to the mechanism of action of adapalene for the treatment of acne is unknown."[^epiduo] The label identifies it as a synthetic retinoid and "a naphthoic acid derivative with retinoid-like properties."[^epiduo] Where the FDA label describes receptor binding in general terms, two secondary reviews describe the selectivity more precisely: StatPearls states that "adapalene and tazarotene have higher specificity for RAR-gamma and RAR-beta and do not interact with RXRs," and the Cosio review likewise describes adapalene as selective for the beta and gamma RARs over the alpha isoform.[^statpearls][^cosio] This is the same receptor-selective design used by [[tazarotene]], and it differs from [[tretinoin]], which is the active acid itself and binds the receptor family without that beta/gamma selectivity. StatPearls also notes adapalene "is an active metabolite and therefore requires no metabolic conversion," which places it off the retinol-to-retinaldehyde-to-acid chain that the [[retinoids]] hub describes, and reports that it is more photostable than tretinoin, which is part of why it can be co-formulated with benzoyl peroxide.[^statpearls]

## The Evidence

Primary clinical evidence:

**Acne, versus benzoyl peroxide.** The 2020 Cochrane review of topical benzoyl peroxide for acne pooled 5 randomized trials (1,472 participants, 11 to 12 weeks) comparing benzoyl peroxide with adapalene and reported that "there may be little to no difference between BPO and adapalene" on participant-reported improvement, with a risk ratio of 0.99 (95% CI 0.90 to 1.10), rated low-certainty evidence.[^cochrane-bpo]

**Acne, versus tretinoin.** A meta-analysis of 5 randomized trials (900 patients, 450 per arm) compared adapalene 0.1% gel with [[tretinoin]] 0.025% gel. It found the two about equal in reducing total lesion counts, adapalene faster in the first week for inflammatory and total lesions, and adapalene better tolerated at all evaluation points.[^cunliffe] This is one pooled analysis of comparative trials from the drug's development era rather than an independent replication, which is why the acne grade above is solid but short of gold-standard.

**Acne, class-level.** Mavranezouli and colleagues' 2022 network meta-analysis of pharmacological, physical, and combined acne treatments covers the topical retinoid class, adapalene included, among the topical options and ranks topical retinoids and benzoyl peroxide as effective over placebo. It grouped treatments into broad classes rather than reporting an adapalene-specific estimate, so it supports the class placement rather than an adapalene-only effect size.[^mavranezouli]

**Regulatory status and combination.** Adapalene 0.1% gel carries an FDA over-the-counter Drug Facts label for acne, listing the active ingredient as "adapalene, USP 0.1% (retinoid)," purpose "acne treatment," and use "for the treatment of acne."[^differin-otc] The fixed combination with benzoyl peroxide is approved as Epiduo (adapalene 0.1% / benzoyl peroxide 2.5%), "indicated for the topical treatment of acne vulgaris in patients 12 years of age and older," where the label lists benzoyl peroxide as "an oxidizing agent with bacteriocidal and keratolytic effects."[^epiduo] Adapalene was first approved as a prescription drug in 1996; StatPearls reports that in 2016 the FDA approved adapalene 0.1% gel "as an over-the-counter acne treatment in patients 12 and older."[^statpearls]

## Uses

Well-supported: mild-to-moderate facial acne vulgaris, where adapalene is one of the FDA-approved topical retinoids and, in head-to-head data, lands level with [[benzoyl-peroxide]] and with [[tretinoin]] on efficacy while being better tolerated than tretinoin.[^cochrane-bpo][^cunliffe] Well-supported as a combination: the fixed adapalene plus benzoyl peroxide gel (Epiduo), approved for acne from age 12, where adapalene's photostability lets the two actives sit in one formulation.[^epiduo] Available over the counter: adapalene 0.1% gel is sold OTC for acne, the same molecule and strength as the original prescription gel.[^differin-otc] As with all retinoids, local irritation (dryness, redness, scaling, stinging) is the main practical limit.[^epiduo]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Gentler than tretinoin, so it is the retinoid for sensitive skin."** Partly grounded. The pooled comparative meta-analysis did find adapalene better tolerated than tretinoin at every evaluation point while matching it on lesion reduction.[^cunliffe] But "gentle" is relative to another retinoid, not absolute: the label still lists dryness, erythema, scaling, and stinging as expected local reactions.[^epiduo] It is a better-tolerated retinoid, not a non-irritating one.
- **"Now over the counter, so it is weaker than prescription retinoids."** The OTC adapalene 0.1% gel is the same molecule at the same strength as the original prescription gel; over-the-counter is a regulatory status, not a lower potency.[^differin-otc][^statpearls] The claim inverts to overreach in the other direction too: OTC adapalene 0.1% is not equivalent to higher-strength prescription formulations or to tretinoin's separate anti-aging evidence.
- **"Clears acne better than benzoyl peroxide."** Not supported. The Cochrane review found "little to no difference between BPO and adapalene" for participant-reported improvement (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.10, low-certainty).[^cochrane-bpo] The evidence points to equivalence, not superiority.
