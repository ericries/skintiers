Olive oil is the pressed fruit oil of *Olea europaea*, used in cosmetics as an emollient and as the oil phase of cleansing oils. It is the rare case where the best controlled trial of an ingredient found it made skin worse: the one forearm-controlled study that isolates olive oil on adult skin reported "a significant reduction in stratum corneum integrity" and mild erythema, while sunflower seed oil on the other arm of the same volunteers held up fine.[^danby]

> **The reason is its fatty acid profile, and it is not fixable by buying a better bottle.** Olive oil is oleic-acid-dominant: USDA reference data put it at 71.3 g oleic and 9.76 g linoleic per 100 g, where linoleic-type sunflower oil is 19.5 g oleic and 65.7 g linoleic.[^usda] A review of plant oils for barrier repair states the rule directly: "Oils with a higher linoleic acid to oleic acid ratio have better barrier repair potential, whereas oils with higher amounts of irritating oleic acid may be detrimental to skin-barrier function."[^vaughn] Cold-pressed, extra-virgin and organic all describe the same oleic-heavy lipid.

## The Rubric

**Barrier repair**
Graded **no benefit on preliminary evidence**, and the direction of the one isolating trial is negative. Danby and colleagues randomised 19 adult volunteers in forearm-controlled mechanistic studies, applying six drops twice daily; after four weeks olive oil "caused a significant reduction in stratum corneum integrity and induced mild erythema in volunteers with and without a history of atopic dermatitis", while "Sunflower seed oil preserved stratum corneum integrity, did not cause erythema, and improved hydration in the same volunteers".[^danby] Nineteen people is small, and the within-person design is the strongest feature it has: each volunteer served as their own control. For what the barrier actually responds to, see [[skin-barrier-repair]] and [[ceramides]].

**Immediate softening and hydration**
Graded **modest on preliminary evidence**. The OBSeRvE pilot randomised 115 healthy full-term neonates to olive oil, sunflower oil or no oil twice daily for four weeks and found "Both oil groups had significantly improved hydration but significantly less improvement in lipid lamellae structure compared to the no oil group".[^cooke] So the oil does hydrate, and it did so while the untreated skin was building better lipid structure than either oil group. Any occlusive film does the first thing; [[petrolatum]] and [[glycerin]] do it with no barrier penalty attached.

**Makeup and sunscreen removal as a cleansing oil**
Graded **notable on anecdotal evidence**, and the evidence class is deliberate. Dissolving oily soil is solvency rather than pharmacology, it is not in dispute, and no trial measures it. Worth separating from everything above: the trials that found harm applied olive oil as a leave-on, twice daily, for four to five weeks.[^danby][^cooke] A cleansing oil sits on skin for under a minute and is emulsified and rinsed, which is a reason to expect less exposure, not a published finding. [[dhc-deep-cleansing-oil]] is built on it and leads with it.

**Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity from polyphenols**
Graded **no demonstrated benefit on anecdotal evidence**. Olive fruit does contain polyphenols, and reviews list anti-inflammatory potential among plant oils generally, but no controlled human trial isolates a topical polyphenol effect for olive oil on skin.[^vaughn]

## The Evidence

**The neonatal literature is not an olive oil literature.** A 2022 PRISMA systematic review of topical oils on neonatal skin pooled 14 randomised trials in 5,683 neonates and concluded that "Although current evidence indicates a potential benefit, more studies with a high level of evidence on the subject are required".[^aksucu] Read the materials before borrowing that sentence for olive oil: the review records that the included studies used "blended sunflower (n = 8), coconut (n = 5), almond (n = 2), olive (n = 1), mustard (n = 1), and vegetable (n = 1) oils".[^aksucu] One of fourteen. The reassuring headline is carried almost entirely by other oils.

**What the authors themselves recommend.** Danby and colleagues do not hedge: "The use of olive oil for the treatment of dry skin and infant massage should therefore be discouraged. These findings challenge the unfounded belief that all natural oils are beneficial for the skin".[^danby] The OBSeRvE authors reach a softer version from their own null and mixed results, that "caution should be exercised when recommending oils for neonatal skin", and state plainly that their trial "was not powered for clinical significance".[^cooke] Both recommendations concern infant care and dry-skin treatment. Neither was written about cleansing oils, and neither tested one.

**Three different materials share the olive name.** Olea Europaea Fruit Oil is the triglyceride oil everything above is about. Olive *leaf* extract is a separate material from a different part of the plant, and [[murad-resurgence-retinal-resculpt-overnight-treatment]] declares the leaf extract rather than the fruit oil. Olive-derived [[squalane]] is a hydrogenated hydrocarbon, not a triglyceride, and it carries no oleic acid at all, so the barrier finding on this page does not transfer to it. Checking which one a label names is the whole of telling them apart.

**Where it turns up on this site.** Three products here declare the fruit oil: [[dhc-deep-cleansing-oil]], where it is the first ingredient; [[byoma-moisturizing-rich-cream]], where it sits at the tail of the list between hydroxyacetophenone and [[bakuchiol]]; and [[isntree-hyaluronic-acid-watery-sun-gel-spf-50]], where it falls among the plant extracts well down the list. Position matters here more than usual, since the trials dosed six drops of neat oil twice daily rather than a fractional-percentage inclusion in a finished cream.

**Who should be most careful.** The Danby result held in volunteers with and without a history of atopic dermatitis, so a clear patch test history is not protection.[^danby] Anyone managing [[atopic-dermatitis]] or [[dry-skin]] has better-supported options and a specific reason to skip this one.

## Common Marketing Claims

**Natural, cold-pressed, extra virgin.** All three are accurate descriptions of the oil and none of them changes the fatty acid ratio that drives the barrier finding. The review that recommends cold pressing as the preferred extraction method is the same review that flags high-oleic oils as potentially detrimental.[^vaughn]

**Used for thousands of years.** True, and the trial authors addressed exactly this framing when they wrote of challenging "the unfounded belief that all natural oils are beneficial for the skin".[^danby]

**Rich in antioxidants and vitamin E.** Olive oil does contain tocopherols. No controlled trial on this page measures a skin outcome from them.
