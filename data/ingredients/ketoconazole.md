Ketoconazole is an azole antifungal, sold in medicated shampoos and creams over the counter at 1% and by prescription at 2%, whose best-evidenced use is clearing the flaking, scaling, and itch of dandruff and seborrhoeic dermatitis by suppressing the scalp yeast Malassezia.[^cochrane][^label]

## The Rubric

**Seborrhoeic dermatitis and dandruff (Malassezia-driven flaking)**
- **Effect size: notable.** In the Cochrane review of topical antifungals, ketoconazole 2% showed "a 31% lower risk of failed clearance of rashes compared with placebo (risk ratio (RR) 0.69, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.59 to 0.81, eight studies)" at four weeks, against a median placebo failure rate of 69%.[^cochrane] It matched topical steroids for remission (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.44) while side effects were "44% lower in the ketoconazole group than in the steroid group" (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.96).[^cochrane] The 2% strength beats the over-the-counter 1%: an open randomized trial in 66 patients found "KET 2% was significantly superior over KET 1% (p < 0.001)" for reducing flakiness and Malassezia density.[^pierard] "Notable: clears most cases and rivals a topical steroid with fewer side effects."
- **Evidence quality: solid in volume, formally rated low.** The case rests on 12 ketoconazole randomized trials (N = 3,253) pooled in a Cochrane review, plus decades of use and FDA approval as both an over-the-counter and a prescription drug.[^cochrane][^label] But Cochrane graded the placebo comparison "low-quality evidence," and noted that "24 trials [of the 51 reviewed] had some form of conflict of interest, such as funding by pharmaceutical companies."[^cochrane] So the direction of benefit is well established; its precise size is less certain.

**Other uses (scalp Malassezia folliculitis, hair loss)**
- **Effect size: not established.** Ketoconazole shampoo is also used off-label for Malassezia folliculitis ("fungal acne") and as an adjunct in androgenetic alopecia, but the controlled evidence for those uses is far thinner than for dandruff and seborrhoeic dermatitis.

## How It Works

Ketoconazole inhibits fungal ergosterol synthesis, which suppresses Malassezia, the scalp yeast whose overgrowth drives the inflammation and flaking of dandruff and seborrhoeic dermatitis.[^cochrane] It is a medicine used for a defined course, not a daily cosmetic active: the over-the-counter label directs use "every 3-4 days for up to 8 weeks."[^label]

## The Evidence

The anchor is the 2015 Cochrane systematic review of topical antifungals for seborrhoeic dermatitis, which pooled 51 randomized trials (9,052 participants), among them 12 ketoconazole-versus-placebo trials (N = 3,253).[^cochrane] For clearance, ketoconazole 2% cut the risk of failed clearance by 31% versus placebo (RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.81, eight studies) at four weeks.[^cochrane] Head to head, it matched topical corticosteroids for remission while cutting side effects by 44%, and roughly tied the other first-line antifungal ciclopirox (RR 1.09, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.26).[^cochrane] The one dedicated strength comparison, an open randomized trial in 66 patients, found 2% significantly better than 1% (p < 0.001).[^pierard] Cochrane's main caveat is trial quality: it rated the placebo comparison "low-quality evidence" and flagged industry conflicts of interest in "24 trials" of the 51.[^cochrane]

## Common Marketing Claims

Over-the-counter ketoconazole shampoos lean on dandruff-control and "clinically proven" language, and the leading brand adds strong comparative claims about being far more effective than other anti-dandruff products. The clinical-proof framing is broadly consistent with the trial evidence above, but any comparative-effectiveness multiplier is the brand's own marketing, not a figure from the Cochrane review.
