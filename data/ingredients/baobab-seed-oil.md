Baobab seed oil is a vegetable oil pressed from the seeds of the baobab tree (Adansonia digitata), native to sub-Saharan Africa and Madagascar, and used in cosmetics as a plant-derived emollient. Fatty-acid composition analyses of the crude seed oil find it dominated by oleic, linolenic, and palmitic acid triglycerides, the general category of components that places vegetable seed oils among occlusive moisturizers, alongside cyclopropenoid fatty acids that are a documented characteristic of the wider Adansonia genus.[^msalilwa][^ralaimanarivo][^statpearls]

> Baobab seed oil's evidence is compositional, not clinical: chemistry analyses agree it is a fatty-acid-rich vegetable oil in the same broad category as other plant emollients, and the Adansonia genus is notable for cyclopropenoid fatty acids that food scientists recommend reducing by refining before the oil is eaten, but no study applying baobab oil itself to human skin was found, so its moisturizing role rests on the general evidence for fatty-acid vegetable oils as emollients rather than on baobab-specific testing.[^msalilwa][^ralaimanarivo][^statpearls]

## The Rubric

**Emollience and moisturizing (topical, cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: not independently quantified; category-level only.** Msalilwa and colleagues found Tanzanian baobab crude seed oil to be roughly a quarter oleic acid (21.20 to 24.86%), 12.16 to 26.14% linolenic acid, 14.99 to 20.48% palmitic acid, and smaller shares of linoleic (3.64 to 5.48%) and stearic acid (1.15 to 2.16%).[^msalilwa] That triglyceride profile puts it in the same general chemical class as the vegetable seed oils StatPearls' review of moisturizers groups among occlusive agents that reduce transepidermal water loss, though StatPearls names other seed oils (coconut, castor, olive, grape seed, soybean) rather than baobab specifically.[^statpearls] The general mechanism for how fatty-acid oils moisturize is covered on [[moisturizing]]; no trial applying baobab oil to human skin was found in the sources checked for this page.
- **Evidence quality: category-level, not ingredient-specific.** The only data located are oil-chemistry analyses of the crude seed oil itself (gas-liquid chromatography of fatty acids, physicochemical indices), not a study of the oil applied to skin.[^msalilwa][^ralaimanarivo]

## The Evidence

**Fatty-acid composition.** Msalilwa and colleagues Soxhlet-extracted crude seed oil from baobab fruit collected across Tanzania's semiarid regions and profiled it by gas-liquid chromatography, reporting that "the A. digitata crude seed oil was found to contain mainly twelve essential fatty acids and two different CPFAs [cyclopropenoid fatty acids]."[^msalilwa] Alongside the oleic, linolenic, and palmitic acids that dominate the profile, they measured the cyclopropenoid acids sterculic acid (0.97 to 1.45%) and dehydrosterculic acid (0.80 to 1.29%); malvalic acid was below the detection limit in their samples. Because CPFAs are a food-safety concern in edible oils, the authors recommend refining the oil at "temperature ranging from 200°C to 250°C" to reduce them toward the level considered safe for consumption, a finding about oral/food use of the crude oil rather than a cosmetic-safety finding.[^msalilwa]

**Genus-wide confirmation of cyclopropenoid fatty acids.** An earlier gas-liquid chromatography/mass-spectrometry survey of oil from six Adansonia species from Madagascar and Africa, including A. digitata, found the oils "give a positive response to the Halphen test" (a classic indicator of cyclopropenoid fatty acids) and detected malvalic acid ranging from 3 to 28%, sterculic acid from 1 to 8%, and dihydrosterculic acid from 1.5 to 5.1% across the species and samples tested, with total oil content across species ranging from 8 to 46%.[^ralaimanarivo] The paper does not break these figures out for A. digitata alone, but it confirms cyclopropenoid fatty acids are a genus-wide characteristic rather than an artifact of one regional sample.

**Traditional dermatologic use, in context.** A 2022 review of oils used in dermatologic practice in Nigeria included baobab oil among seven plant oils and fats examined, alongside shea butter, coconut oil, palm kernel oil, palm oil, soy oil, and python oil, describing the group collectively as containing "free fatty acids, triglycerides, ceramides, phospholipids, vitamins and antioxidants" while cautioning that such oils are "not without adverse effects, which may be mainly due to processing and storage hygiene."[^ayanlowo] The review does not report findings specific to baobab oil beyond its inclusion in that list.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Cold-pressed and unrefined for maximum benefit."** Cosmetic baobab oil is often marketed as cold-pressed and unrefined. Chemistry analysis of the crude, unrefined seed oil finds it retains cyclopropenoid fatty acids, compounds that food scientists recommend reducing through heat refining at 200 to 250 degrees Celsius before the oil is eaten.[^msalilwa]
