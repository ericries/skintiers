Glycolic acid is the lowest-molecular-weight alpha-hydroxy acid (AHA), a small organic acid originally derived from sugar cane and used to loosen and shed the outer layer of skin in both leave-on cosmetics and higher-strength chemical peels.[^kornhauser]

AHAs are a family of water-soluble acids that includes glycolic acid (from sugar cane), lactic acid, citric, malic, tartaric, and mandelic acid; glycolic is the smallest of them and the one most used in skincare.[^kornhauser] At low, single-digit strengths they act mainly at the surface, weakening the bonds between dead surface cells so the outer layer sheds, and the controlled trials behind them tested creams at around 8% to 10%.[^stiller][^kaidbey] At the higher strengths used for in-office peels (20% to 30% in one melasma trial) the effect reaches deeper.[^hurley] In the United States, AHAs in cosmetics are regulated as cosmetics rather than drugs, so no FDA efficacy review sits behind them; the best-documented health effect of glycolic acid is a downside, that it can make skin more sensitive to ultraviolet light.[^kaidbey][^kornhauser]

> Glycolic acid and the other AHAs are chemical exfoliants with modest evidence. A small pilot study found long-term topical AHA increased skin thickness and dermal collagen, and a vehicle-controlled trial found 8% glycolic and lactic creams "modestly useful" against photodamage.[^ditre][^stiller] As peels they perform about as well as other superficial peels for acne and added nothing to hydroquinone for melasma.[^cochrane][^hurley] The clearest health finding is unfavorable: in a randomized double-blind study, 10% glycolic acid increased the skin's sensitivity to UV light, and the effect reversed within a week of stopping.[^kaidbey]

## The Rubric

Grades differ by use, and are framed relative to named comparators. Sun sensitivity is a separate safety axis, graded on its own rather than folded into an efficacy grade.

**Photoaging and skin texture (topical, low strength)**
- **Effect size: modest.** The health-level (skin-biology) signal comes from one small pilot: patients applied a 25% AHA lotion to one forearm and placebo to the other for about six months, and the treated arm showed "increased density of collagen," more dermal acid mucopolysaccharides, and "an approximate 25% increase in skin thickness."[^ditre] The controlled clinical evidence is more modest and largely about appearance: in a vehicle-controlled trial, 8% glycolic and 8% lactic creams beat vehicle on overall photodamage and sallowness, which the authors judged "modestly useful."[^stiller] Topical retinoids, the best-evidenced topicals for photoaging, rest on larger and more replicated trials; see [[retinoids]]. "Modest, and on a smaller and less-replicated evidence base than topical retinoids."
- **Evidence quality: mixed.** One RCT (n=74) supports a modest anti-photodamage effect at 8%, and the dermal-remodeling findings come from a small pilot that reported no p-values for its thickness measure.[^ditre][^stiller] Several of the improved endpoints (sallowness, roughness, mottled pigmentation) are cosmetic appearance measures rather than skin biology.[^stiller]

**Acne (chemical peels)**
- **Effect size: modest, and level with other superficial peels.** Where glycolic peels have been compared head to head, they land alongside the alternatives rather than ahead of them: a Cochrane review found a glycolic peel about equal to a salicylic-mandelic peel on participants' global self-assessment (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.26; 40 participants, 1 study).[^cochrane] Salicylic acid, a comparable superficial peel and OTC acne active, is covered on [[salicylic-acid]], and guidelines rank benzoyl peroxide and topical retinoids above superficial acids; see [[benzoyl-peroxide]] and [[retinoids]]. "Modest, roughly interchangeable with other superficial acid peels, and below the first-line acne topicals."
- **Evidence quality: weak.** The peel comparison rests on a single small study that Cochrane rated low quality, and the review concluded that outside its azelaic-acid comparisons "the quality of evidence was low or very low."[^cochrane]

**Melasma and hyperpigmentation (glycolic peels added to hydroquinone)**
- **Effect size: none demonstrated as an add-on.** In a split-face RCT of 21 women, everyone used 4% hydroquinone and daily sunscreen, and one side also received 20% to 30% glycolic peels every two weeks; both sides improved versus baseline (P<.001), but adding the peels made "no significant difference" over hydroquinone alone (P=.75).[^hurley] Hydroquinone, the comparator that carried the effect here, is a well-studied skin-lightening agent covered on [[hydroquinone]]. "No demonstrated benefit beyond hydroquinone; the pigment improvement in the trial came from hydroquinone, not the peels."
- **Evidence quality: weak.** A single small split-face RCT (n=21) for the add-on question.[^hurley]

**Sun sensitivity (a safety axis, not an efficacy grade)**
- A randomized, double-blind study treated the backs of 29 people once daily, six days a week, with 10% glycolic acid (pH 3.5) or placebo for four weeks, then exposed the sites to UV.[^kaidbey] Glycolic acid "caused enhanced sensitivity to UV light measured as increased SBC [sunburn cell] induction and lowered MEDs [minimal erythema doses]"; UV-induced DNA damage (cyclobutyl pyrimidine dimers) was "elevated but not to a statistically significant level."[^kaidbey] The authors concluded that glycolic acid "sensitizes the skin to the damaging effects of UV light," and that the effect "is reversed within a week of terminating treatments."[^kaidbey] A review by former FDA scientists notes that "A number of clinical studies have reported that topical application of glycolic acid can increase sensitivity of the skin to solar-simulated radiation (SSR)."[^kornhauser] This is a health effect (increased UV-induced skin damage), it is transient, and it is why AHA products carry sun-sensitivity cautions.[^kaidbey][^kornhauser]

## What We Actually Know

Glycolic acid is a chemical exfoliant with modest and mostly appearance-level efficacy evidence. A small pilot study found that six months of topical AHA increased collagen density and produced "an approximate 25% increase in skin thickness," and a vehicle-controlled RCT found 8% glycolic and lactic creams "modestly useful" against the signs of photodamage.[^ditre][^stiller] Used as an in-office peel, glycolic acid performs about the same as other superficial peels for acne and, in the one relevant RCT, added nothing to hydroquinone for melasma.[^cochrane][^hurley] The best-documented health effect is unfavorable and transient: a randomized double-blind study showed that 10% glycolic acid increases the skin's sensitivity to UV light, reversing within a week of stopping.[^kaidbey] In the United States AHAs in cosmetics are regulated as cosmetics, not drugs, so there is no FDA efficacy approval behind these uses.[^kornhauser]

## How It Works

Glycolic acid is the smallest of the alpha-hydroxy acids, a two-carbon acid (hydroxyacetic acid), which is a large part of why it is the AHA most used on skin.[^kornhauser] At low pH it weakens the cohesion between corneocytes, the flattened dead cells of the outermost skin layer, so that layer loosens and sheds; this desquamating action is the basis for AHAs being used as exfoliants and superficial peels.[^kornhauser] With prolonged use the effect is not confined to the surface. In the pilot study of 25% AHA, biopsies of the treated forearm showed a thicker epidermis and papillary dermis with "increased density of collagen," more acid mucopolysaccharides (a glycosaminoglycan measure), and improved elastic-fiber quality, with "no inflammation," which the authors read as "significant reversal of epidermal and dermal markers of photoaging."[^ditre] The mechanism that drives the sun-sensitivity finding is the flip side of exfoliation: thinning or removing the outer dead-cell layer leaves the living skin below less shielded, and a study of 10% glycolic acid measured this directly as increased sunburn-cell formation and a lower minimal erythema dose after UV exposure.[^kaidbey]

## The Evidence

Primary clinical evidence, by use.

**Photoaging.** A pilot clinical, histologic, and ultrastructural study had patients apply a lotion containing "25% glycolic, lactic, or citric acid" to one forearm and a placebo lotion to the other for an average of six months.[^ditre] Treatment "caused an approximate 25% increase in skin thickness," with a thicker epidermis and papillary dermal changes including "increased density of collagen," increased acid mucopolysaccharides, and improved elastic fibers, and the authors concluded it "produced significant reversal of epidermal and dermal markers of photoaging."[^ditre] The stronger controlled evidence is a single-center, 22-week, double-blind, vehicle-controlled RCT in 74 women aged 40 to 70 with photodamaged skin.[^stiller] On the face, the share achieving at least one grade of improvement in overall photodamage severity was "significantly greater than with the vehicle cream (76% glycolic acid, 71% lactic acid, and 40% vehicle; P < .05)," and on the forearms glycolic acid beat vehicle for overall severity and sallowness while lactic acid also improved mottled hyperpigmentation and roughness (each P<.05).[^stiller] The authors' verdict was measured: the creams are "modestly useful in ameliorating some of the signs of chronic cutaneous photodamage."[^stiller]

**Acne.** A Cochrane systematic review that pooled topical acne actives, including alpha-hydroxy (fruit) acid, found only one relevant head-to-head for glycolic acid: against a salicylic-mandelic acid peel, glycolic peels made little or no difference to participants' global self-assessment (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.26; 40 participants, 1 study), rated low quality.[^cochrane] The review's overall caution applies here: outside its azelaic-acid comparisons, "the quality of evidence was low or very low."[^cochrane]

**Melasma.** A split-face RCT enrolled 21 Hispanic women with epidermal and mixed melasma; all applied 4% hydroquinone cream and an SPF-25 sunscreen daily to the whole face, and one side additionally received 20% to 30% glycolic acid peels every two weeks for eight weeks.[^hurley] Both hydroquinone alone and hydroquinone plus peels reduced pigmentation versus baseline (P<.001), but "no significant difference was found using combination therapy compared with hydroquinone alone (P =.75)," and the authors concluded the added peels "did not enhance the hypopigmenting effect of hydroquinone treatment alone."[^hurley]

**Sun sensitivity.** A randomized, double-blind study treated the backs of 29 Caucasian subjects once daily, six days per week, with 10% glycolic acid (pH 3.5) or placebo for four weeks before a UV challenge; sunburn cells were counted in one subgroup (n=16) and DNA cyclobutyl pyrimidine dimers in another (n=13).[^kaidbey] Glycolic acid "caused enhanced sensitivity to UV light measured as increased SBC induction and lowered MEDs," while the DNA dimers were "elevated but not to a statistically significant level," and no difference remained one week after stopping.[^kaidbey] The authors concluded that "short-term application of 10% glycolic acid sensitizes the skin to the damaging effects of UV light" and that "this photosensitivity is reversed within a week of terminating treatments."[^kaidbey]

**Regulatory and safety context.** A review by former FDA scientists places AHAs among widely used cosmetic and therapeutic agents and gives "special emphasis" to "the effects of their prolonged use on sun-exposed skin," noting that "A number of clinical studies have reported that topical application of glycolic acid can increase sensitivity of the skin to solar-simulated radiation (SSR)."[^kornhauser] In US cosmetics these acids are marketed as cosmetics rather than approved drugs.[^kornhauser]

## Uses

Supported but modest: improving the look of photodamaged, rough, or sallow skin, where an 8% glycolic or lactic cream beat vehicle in a controlled trial and a small pilot showed dermal collagen and thickness gains, though on a smaller evidence base than topical [[retinoids]].[^ditre][^stiller] Comparable to peers, not better: as a superficial in-office peel for acne, where glycolic peels are about level with a salicylic-mandelic peel and sit below the first-line acne topicals.[^cochrane] Not supported as an add-on: layering glycolic peels onto hydroquinone for melasma gave no benefit over hydroquinone alone in the one RCT to test it.[^hurley] The main documented health consideration is that glycolic acid increases the skin's sensitivity to UV light while it is being used, an effect that clears within a week of stopping.[^kaidbey]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Boosts collagen and reverses aging."** The collagen and skin-thickness findings come from one small pilot study without reported statistics, and the controlled RCT that followed judged the same class of creams only "modestly useful" against photodamage.[^ditre][^stiller] Topical retinoids carry larger and more replicated anti-aging evidence; see [[retinoids]].
- **"Fades dark spots and melasma."** The one RCT of glycolic peels for melasma added them to hydroquinone and found they "did not enhance the hypopigmenting effect of hydroquinone treatment alone" (P=.75); the pigment improvement came from the hydroquinone.[^hurley]
- **"A gentle daily exfoliant for glowing skin."** Glow and radiance are cosmetic appearance claims, not the endpoints the trials measured, and the best-documented health effect of daily use is increased UV sensitivity: 10% glycolic acid raised sunburn-cell formation and lowered the minimal erythema dose in a randomized study.[^kaidbey][^stiller]
- **"Natural fruit acid, so it is mild and sun-safe."** The opposite is documented for the sun: a randomized double-blind study found glycolic acid "sensitizes the skin to the damaging effects of UV light," reversing within a week of stopping.[^kaidbey]
