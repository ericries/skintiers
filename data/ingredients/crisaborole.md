Crisaborole is a prescription, nonsteroidal topical drug (brand name Eucrisa, 2% ointment) that inhibits phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4), an enzyme that breaks down the inflammation-regulating messenger cAMP; the FDA-approved label states it is "indicated for topical treatment of mild to moderate atopic dermatitis in adult and pediatric patients 3 months of age and older."[^dailymed] It works by a different mechanism than topical corticosteroids and is one of the prescription anti-inflammatory options for [[atopic-dermatitis]] alongside the topical calcineurin inhibitor [[tacrolimus-topical|tacrolimus]].

> In the two identically designed phase 3 trials that supported FDA approval, crisaborole cleared or nearly cleared skin in about half of patients after four weeks versus roughly a third to two-fifths on the inert vehicle ointment alone, a real but modest incremental benefit, and about 4% of crisaborole-treated patients reported application-site pain (including burning or stinging) versus 1% on vehicle.[^paller2016][^dailymed]

## The Rubric

**Mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis (topical, ages 3 months and up)**
- **Effect size: modest.** Crisaborole's approval rests on two identically designed, vehicle-controlled, double-blind phase 3 trials (AD-301 and AD-302) in patients age 2 and older with mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis, treated twice daily for 28 days.[^paller2016] The gap over vehicle on the primary clearance endpoint was real and statistically significant in both trials but a matter of roughly 7 to 13 percentage points, not a dramatic separation, and close to a third to two-fifths of vehicle-only patients also improved; see [The Evidence](#the-evidence) for the exact figures. "Modest: a real, statistically significant edge over an inert vehicle ointment in two identical phase 3 trials, on the order of single-digit-to-teens percentage points, not a large separation."
- **Evidence quality: solid for short-term efficacy.** Two adequately sized, identically designed, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled phase 3 trials that were the basis for FDA approval, run to a prespecified primary endpoint.[^paller2016] The trials are 28 days and vehicle-controlled, so they establish superiority to an inert ointment rather than a head-to-head comparison against topical corticosteroids or [[tacrolimus-topical|tacrolimus]].

## The Evidence

The phase 3, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trials AD-301 and AD-302 (NCT02118766, NCT02118792) randomized patients age 2 and older with mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis (affecting 5% to 95% body surface area) to crisaborole 2% ointment or vehicle ointment, applied twice daily for 28 days, in a roughly 2:1 ratio.[^paller2016] On the primary endpoint, ISGA success (clear or almost clear with a 2-grade-or-greater improvement from baseline) at day 29, crisaborole beat vehicle in both trials: 32.8% versus 25.4% (P=.038) in AD-301, and 31.4% versus 18.0% (P<.001) in AD-302.[^paller2016] On the secondary clear/almost-clear outcome, 51.7% versus 40.6% (P=.005) in AD-301 and 48.5% versus 29.7% (P<.001) in AD-302 reached that status.[^paller2016] Patients on crisaborole also reached treatment success and improvement in pruritus (itch) sooner than those on vehicle in both trials (P≤.001).[^paller2016] The trials compare crisaborole only against an inert vehicle ointment, not against a topical corticosteroid or [[tacrolimus-topical|tacrolimus]], so they establish that crisaborole outperforms doing nothing but do not establish where it ranks against other prescription anti-inflammatory options.

## Tolerability

The most common treatment-related adverse event in the FDA label's pooled trial data is application-site pain, occurring in 4% (45 of 1,012) of crisaborole-treated patients versus 1% (6 of 499) on vehicle, described in the label as including sensations of burning or stinging.[^dailymed] Contact urticaria occurred at under 1% in crisaborole-treated subjects, and allergic contact dermatitis has been identified in postmarketing experience.[^dailymed] The labeled dosing is a thin layer applied twice daily to affected areas, with the label noting that once a clinical effect is achieved, reducing to once daily may be considered.[^dailymed]

## How It Works

Crisaborole is a benzoxaborole, a boron-containing small molecule; its developers describe the boron atom's configuration as enabling "selective targeting and inhibition of PDE4" while keeping the molecule small (251 daltons), which they say facilitates penetration through skin.[^jarnagin2016] PDE4 normally breaks down cyclic AMP (cAMP), a second messenger; by inhibiting PDE4, crisaborole raises intracellular cAMP, and the FDA label states this "results in increased intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels."[^dailymed] Its developers report that crisaborole "inhibits cytokine production from peripheral blood mononuclear cells in a pattern similar to other PDE4 inhibitors and distinct from corticosteroids," positioning it as a different anti-inflammatory route than a topical steroid rather than a steroid substitute proven equally potent.[^jarnagin2016]

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Steroid-free" framed as gentler or side-effect-free.** Crisaborole is genuinely nonsteroidal, acting through PDE4 inhibition rather than the corticosteroid pathway, and its developers describe its cytokine-suppression pattern as "distinct from corticosteroids."[^jarnagin2016] But nonsteroidal is not the same as side-effect-free: application-site pain (including burning or stinging) occurred in about 4% of crisaborole-treated patients versus 1% on vehicle in the approval trials, those trials only ran 28 days, and they were never compared head to head against a topical corticosteroid.[^dailymed][^paller2016]
