Tasmannia lanceolata extract (TLE) is a leaf extract of the Tasmanian pepperberry, an Australian mountain-pepper plant, used in cosmetics and marketed for soothing and anti-redness benefits. Its main constituent is polygodial, a compound with anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory models.[^gaillard] Despite the soothing marketing, the one controlled human trial of the extract tested a different endpoint entirely: the appearance of stretch marks.[^gaillard] The extract also has in-vitro activity against skin fungi, a traditional use rather than a demonstrated cosmetic benefit.[^noe]

> Tasmannia lanceolata extract's best human evidence is a single small (29 women), manufacturer-run, placebo-controlled trial reporting improved stretch-mark appearance over 8 weeks, with no effect-size numbers reported and no independent replication.[^gaillard] The anti-redness and soothing claims rest on polygodial's laboratory anti-inflammatory reputation rather than on a clinical redness trial. It is a thinly evidenced botanical.

## The Rubric

The grade is for the extract's one clinically tested skin benefit, stretch-mark appearance. The soothing/anti-redness marketing is a separate claim with no controlled human trial, addressed below.

**Stretch-mark appearance**
- **Effect size: uncertain and unquantified.** In the only controlled trial, a double-blind randomized study in 29 women with stabilized stretch marks, 8 weeks of topical TLE "significantly reduced" skin roughness and "significantly increased" firmness (from week 4) versus placebo, with significantly higher dermal density and thickness than placebo by ultrasound.[^gaillard] The abstract reports statistical significance but no percentages or absolute magnitudes, so how large the visible improvement is cannot be stated. Stretch marks are notoriously resistant to topical treatment, which makes any positive signal notable but also warrants caution about a single result.
- **Evidence quality: weak. One small, sponsor-run trial, no replication.** The study was conducted by authors affiliated with the Robertet Group, which produces the extract, alongside a testing lab, so it is an industry study of the maker's own ingredient; n was 29 and there is no independent replication.[^gaillard] Its ex-vivo arm found higher pro-collagen I, elastin, and TGF-beta in TLE-treated stretch-mark skin explants than in controls, a plausible mechanism, but from the same study and not an independent line of evidence.[^gaillard]

**Soothing / anti-redness (the marketing claim, not clinically tested)**
- No controlled human trial of the extract for redness, irritation, or sensitive-skin endpoints was located. The claim is an extrapolation from polygodial's anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory models, cited as background in the same trial.[^gaillard] Treat it as a mechanistic hypothesis, not a demonstrated result.

## What We Actually Know

Tasmannia lanceolata extract is a polygodial-containing leaf extract with one controlled human trial behind it, and that trial was for stretch marks, not the soothing use it is usually sold for.[^gaillard] The trial (29 women, 8 weeks, double-blind, placebo-controlled) reported significant improvements in stretch-mark roughness, firmness, and dermal density, but published no effect sizes, was run by the ingredient's manufacturer, and has not been independently replicated.[^gaillard] Separately, the extract inhibits dermatophyte fungi in vitro, consistent with its traditional use against fungal infections, but that is a laboratory finding, not a cosmetic skin benefit.[^noe] The anti-redness marketing rests on polygodial's lab anti-inflammatory reputation rather than on any clinical redness endpoint.

## The Evidence

The single controlled human study is Gaillard and colleagues' 2021 trial: double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, 29 women aged 25 to 60 with stabilized stretch marks, topical TLE versus placebo daily for 8 weeks, with photographic, ultrasound, and clinical-scoring endpoints plus an ex-vivo human skin explant arm.[^gaillard] It reported significant reductions in stretch-mark roughness and significant increases in firmness, dermal density, and thickness versus placebo, and higher pro-collagen I, elastin, and TGF-beta in treated explants, concluding the extract "could help improve the aspect of stabilized stretch marks."[^gaillard] The limits are the small size, the manufacturer affiliation, the absence of reported effect magnitudes, and no replication. The other dermatology-adjacent evidence is in vitro: TLE inhibited the growth of human dermatophytic (skin) fungi among Australian plants traditionally used against fungal infections.[^noe]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Soothing" / "anti-redness" / "calming."** Not tested in a controlled human trial of this extract; an extrapolation from polygodial's laboratory anti-inflammatory activity.[^gaillard]
- **"Reduces stretch marks."** Supported only by a single small manufacturer-run trial that reported significance without effect sizes and has not been independently replicated.[^gaillard]
