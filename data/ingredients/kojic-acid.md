Kojic acid is a tyrosinase-inhibiting compound originally isolated from the fungus *Aspergillus oryzae* during fermentation, used in leave-on skincare mainly for melasma and hyperpigmentation, typically at 1% to 2%.[^pubchem] It is rarely sold alone: most of the controlled evidence tests it mixed into a hydroquinone- or glycolic-acid-based formula, it is a recognized cause of allergic contact dermatitis, with high sensitization frequency among prior users who present with suspected cosmetic-related reactions, and it degrades in some formulations unless stabilized.

> Split-face trials against hydroquinone found kojic acid roughly comparable but "more irritating," and adding 2% kojic acid to a hydroquinone/glycolic-acid gel outperformed the gel alone; a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of melasma treatments put kojic acid's effect size below hydroquinone, azelaic acid, tranexamic acid, and cysteamine while giving it one of the lowest irritation rates of the agents compared.[^garcia][^lim][^chang] A 220-patient patch-test series found a "comparatively high frequency of contact sensitivity" among prior kojic acid users, and a US cosmetic-ingredient safety panel concluded sensitization and skin-lightening effects were not seen below a 1% use concentration.[^nakagawa][^cir]

## The Rubric

**Hyperpigmentation and melasma, combined with hydroquinone or glycolic acid (topical)**
- **Effect size: modest, and best documented as an add-on rather than alone.** Adding 2% kojic acid to a hydroquinone/glycolic-acid gel outperformed the gel without it, and a kojic acid/hydroquinone combination outperformed kojic acid alone, but a direct split-face comparison against hydroquinone alone found kojic acid roughly comparable in effect and more irritating.[^lim][^deo][^garcia] "Modest: adds a real but incremental benefit on top of hydroquinone or glycolic acid rather than outperforming them alone, and comes with more irritation than hydroquinone in a head-to-head split-face comparison."
- **Evidence quality: mixed.** Two split-face RCTs from the 1990s (39 and 40 patients) and one single-blind combination RCT (80 patients, 20 per arm), all comparing formulations rather than isolating kojic acid against a vehicle or placebo control.[^garcia][^lim][^deo]

**Melasma, ranked against other topical depigmenting agents (topical)**
- **Effect size: modest, below several established comparators.** A 2023 meta-analysis of topical melasma treatments ranked kojic acid's pooled effect size below cysteamine, tranexamic acid, azelaic acid, and hydroquinone-based formulations, while giving it one of the lowest irritation rates among the agents compared; the well-evidenced tyrosinase inhibitor [[hydroquinone]] and the differently-mechanism [[azelaic-acid]] both outranked it on efficacy.[^chang] "Modest and comparatively better-tolerated: ranks below hydroquinone, azelaic acid, tranexamic acid, and cysteamine on pooled effect size, but with one of the lowest irritation rates in the same pooled comparison."
- **Evidence quality: solid as a pooled comparison, though built on a heterogeneous mix of underlying trials.** One network-style systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizing dozens of melasma trials across agents.[^chang]

## The Evidence

**Kojic acid versus hydroquinone, split-face.** Garcia and Fulton treated 39 melasma patients with kojic acid on one side of the face and hydroquinone in a matched vehicle on the other, assessed clinically and by Wood's-light ultraviolet photography. Fifty-one percent of patients responded equally to both agents, 28% had a more dramatic reduction in pigment with kojic acid, and 21% did better with hydroquinone, differences the authors did not find statistically significant; however, "the kojic acid preparation was more irritating."[^garcia] *Kojic acid performed about as well as hydroquinone on pigment clearance but caused more irritation.*

**Kojic acid added to a hydroquinone/glycolic-acid gel, split-face.** Lim randomized 40 Chinese women with epidermal melasma to a gel of 10% glycolic acid and 2% hydroquinone on one side of the face, with 2% kojic acid added to the same gel on the other side, for 12 weeks. More than half the melasma cleared in 24 of 40 patients (60%) on the kojic acid side versus 19 of 40 (47.5%) on the side without it, with complete clearance of melasma in 2 patients overall; redness, stinging, and exfoliation appeared on both sides and resolved by week three.[^lim] *Adding kojic acid to an existing hydroquinone/glycolic-acid gel improved clearance rates further, at the cost of transient irritation shared by both sides of the formula.*

**Kojic acid combinations, single-blind RCT.** Deo and colleagues randomized 80 melasma patients to four arms of 20: kojic acid 1% alone, kojic acid 1% plus hydroquinone 2%, kojic acid 1% plus betamethasone valerate 0.1%, or all three combined, for 12 weeks with biweekly evaluation by Melasma Area and Severity Index (MASI). Clinical efficacy was highest in the kojic acid plus hydroquinone group, followed closely by the three-agent combination and kojic acid alone, with the kojic acid plus betamethasone group lowest; the authors concluded kojic acid "in synergy with hydroquinone is a superior depigmenting agent as compared with other combinations."[^deo] *Pairing kojic acid with hydroquinone beat pairing it with a topical steroid or using it alone.*

**Pooled comparison across melasma treatments.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of 45 studies (2,359 patients) on topical melasma treatments reported a standardized mean difference of -0.9 (95% CI -1.3 to -0.5) for kojic acid's MASI/modified-MASI improvement, smaller than cysteamine (-1.6), tranexamic acid (-1.5), azelaic acid (-1.3), and hydroquinone-based formulations (-1.3 to -1.4), and reported skin irritation in 5.3% of kojic acid users, among the lowest rates of the agents it compared.[^chang] *Across a large pooled comparison, kojic acid clears melasma less than several alternatives but irritates fewer users than most of them.*

## Sensitization and stability

Kojic acid is a recognized cause of allergic and pigmented contact dermatitis. In a one-year patch-test series of 220 female patients with suspected cosmetic-related dermatitis, 8 had used kojic acid-containing products and 5 of those 8 reacted positively to 1% kojic acid on patch testing, described as "a comparatively high frequency of contact sensitivity"; none of the 212 patients without prior kojic acid exposure reacted.[^nakagawa] A separate case report described pigmented contact dermatitis caused by kojic acid itself, terming it "a paradoxical side effect of a skin lightener."[^garciagavin] The US Cosmetic Ingredient Review (CIR) Expert Panel's safety assessment noted kojic acid "depigmented black guinea pig skin at a concentration of 4%, but this effect was not seen at 1%," concluded that "the 2 end points of concern, dermal sensitization and skin lightening, would not be seen at use concentrations below 1%," and on that basis found the ingredient "safe for use in cosmetic products up to that level."[^cir]

Kojic acid is also chemically unstable in some vehicles. A formulation study comparing 1% kojic acid in an emulgel versus a simple gel, each with an antioxidant stabilizer, found the emulgel with 0.25% sodium metabisulfite retained 86% of its starting kojic acid content under stress conditions versus 76% for the simple gel with 0.1% antioxidant (P<0.0001), and concluded refrigerated storage was the better condition for both forms.[^khadivi]

## How It Works

Kojic acid was first isolated from *Aspergillus oryzae*, a fermentation fungus, and PubChem's compound record classifies it structurally as a pyranone and lists it as a tyrosinase inhibitor, the copper-containing enzyme that catalyzes the rate-limiting steps of melanin synthesis.[^pubchem] A 2023 review of naturally occurring tyrosinase inhibitors classifies kojic acid among the "active site copper iron-binding inhibitors," meaning it works by chelating the copper at the enzyme's catalytic center rather than by a separate route such as blocking pigment transfer.[^kim] This copper-chelation mechanism is shared conceptually with [[arbutin]]'s target enzyme, though the two compounds are structurally unrelated.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"A natural alternative to hydroquinone."** Head-to-head split-face trials found kojic acid roughly comparable to hydroquinone in effect but more irritating in one comparison, and a pooled meta-analysis ranked its effect size below hydroquinone.[^garcia][^chang] It is not shown to be a stronger or safer-on-efficacy substitute; its main documented tolerability edge is a lower irritation rate in the pooled comparison, not superior clearance.[^chang]
- **"Gentle and non-sensitizing."** A dedicated patch-test series found a majority of prior kojic acid users who reacted at all reacted specifically to kojic acid, and a regulatory panel capped its concluded-safe concentration at below 1% partly because of sensitization risk at higher levels.[^nakagawa][^cir]
