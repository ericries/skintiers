Lactic acid is an alpha-hydroxy acid (AHA) used in leave-on skincare at concentrations that have been tested from 5% to 12%; its l-form is the one a review whose lead author is a retired FDA scientist describes as carrying the "optimal biologic activity."[^kornhauser] It shares its evidence base with the smaller AHA [[glycolic-acid]]: the trial most often cited for leave-on AHA creams tested an 8% lactic acid cream head to head against an 8% glycolic acid cream, not lactic acid alone, and the two performed about the same.[^stiller]

> An 8% lactic acid cream and an 8% glycolic acid cream produced near-identical, modest improvement against vehicle in the lead 22-week trial behind leave-on AHA creams (71% and 76% of faces improved by at least one photodamage grade, versus 40% on vehicle, P<.05).[^stiller] Whether lactic acid is actually gentler than glycolic acid is unsettled: one comparative study found it less irritating at higher concentrations and tied to fewer consumer complaints, while a separate controlled trial measuring transepidermal water loss and redness found no significant difference between 8% glycolic and 8% lactic acid.[^smith-ijcs][^berardesca] Lactic acid's own dose-response data show a real gap between concentrations: 12% produced measurable dermal changes that 5% did not.[^smith-jaad]

## The Rubric

Grades differ by use and by concentration.

**Photoaging appearance (topical, leave-on, tested at 8%)**
- Effect size: modest, and shared with glycolic acid rather than distinct to lactic acid. In the lead 22-week, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial, 8% lactic acid cream improved overall photodamage severity in 71% of faces by at least one grade, against 76% for 8% glycolic acid and 40% for vehicle (P<.05); on the forearms, lactic acid was reported as significantly superior to vehicle for overall severity, mottled hyperpigmentation, sallowness, and roughness.[^stiller] These are investigator appearance grades, not histology or collagen measurement. "Modest; matched glycolic acid at the same 8% concentration in the one lead trial, on appearance grading rather than skin biology."
- Evidence quality: solid for a cosmetic trial, but singular. One double-blind, vehicle-controlled RCT (74 women aged 40 to 70, 67 completing); one participant withdrew for skin irritation, and the authors concluded both acids were "modestly useful in ameliorating some of the signs of chronic cutaneous photodamage."[^stiller]

**Skin smoothness, firmness, and fine lines (topical, dose-dependent)**
- Effect size: modest, and dependent on concentration. In a 3-month trial, 12% lactic acid applied twice daily increased epidermal and dermal firmness and thickness alongside clinical improvement in skin smoothness and the appearance of lines and wrinkles; 5% lactic acid produced similar clinical and epidermal change but no measured dermal change.[^smith-jaad] "Modest; the dermal effect only showed up at 12%, not 5%, in the one dose-comparison trial, though both concentrations changed the epidermis and surface appearance."
- Evidence quality: preliminary. A single-author trial reporting instrumented skin thickness and firmness alongside clinical scoring; the published abstract does not describe a vehicle-controlled arm.[^smith-jaad]

**Mildness and hydration relative to glycolic acid**
- Effect size: mixed, depending on what is measured. A comparative study across several AHAs found l(+) lactic acid produced fewer consumer complaints than glycolic acid over six weeks of use and was less irritating than glycolic acid at concentrations above 1.5 mol/L, while both acids gave "comparative improvements in skin hydration and lines and wrinkle improvement" at equimolar concentrations.[^smith-ijcs] A separate controlled study measuring transepidermal water loss and redness found no significant difference between 8% glycolic acid and 8% lactic acid after four weeks, and after a standardized sodium lauryl sulfate irritant challenge it was gluconolactone and tartaric acid, not lactic acid, that gave significantly lower water loss than glycolic acid.[^berardesca] "Mixed; fewer complaints and less stinging in one comparative study, but no measurable barrier or irritation advantage over glycolic acid in a separate controlled trial, and no clear hydration edge over glycolic acid either."
- Evidence quality: preliminary and inconsistent. Two small controlled studies point in different directions depending on whether the outcome is subjective stinging and complaints or instrumented water loss and erythema.[^smith-ijcs][^berardesca]

## What We Actually Know

The best-controlled data on lactic acid do not test it alone: the lead 22-week trial behind leave-on AHA marketing ran 8% lactic acid and 8% glycolic acid side by side against vehicle, and the two acids produced statistically similar, modest improvement in photodamage appearance (71% vs 76% of faces improved by at least one grade, versus 40% on vehicle, P<.05).[^stiller] The popular claim that lactic acid is gentler and more hydrating than glycolic acid has real but split support: a comparative study of several AHAs found lactic acid's l(+) form less irritating at higher concentrations and better tolerated by consumers over six weeks, with hydration and wrinkle improvement comparable, not clearly superior, to glycolic acid,[^smith-ijcs] while a separate controlled trial measuring transepidermal water loss and skin redness found no significant difference between 8% glycolic and 8% lactic acid, and found gluconolactone and tartaric acid, not lactic acid, gave better protection than glycolic acid after an irritant challenge.[^berardesca] Lactic acid's effects also depend on concentration: a 3-month trial found 12% produced measurable increases in epidermal and dermal thickness and firmness, while 5% changed the epidermis and surface appearance but not the dermis.[^smith-jaad]

## How It Works

Lactic acid belongs to the alpha-hydroxy acid family, water-soluble acids that weaken the bonds holding dead corneocytes together at the skin's surface so the outer layer sheds faster, the same mechanism as [[glycolic-acid]].[^kornhauser] The Kornhauser review notes lactic acid "is also used in various topical formulations to exfoliate the skin and also to provide antiaging properties," and cites work finding that topical creams containing lactic acid at 1.5%, 3%, or 5% produced "a concentration-dependent increase in apoptotic cells" compared with vehicle, consistent with a dose-dependent turnover effect on the epidermis.[^kornhauser] The dermal changes seen at higher concentrations, increased thickness and firmness at 12% but not 5% in the Smith dose-comparison trial, point to an effect that reaches beyond simple surface desquamation once concentration is high enough.[^smith-jaad]

## The Evidence

**Photoaging (8%, head to head with glycolic acid).** The single-center, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial randomized 74 women aged 40 to 70 with moderate to severe photodamage to twice-daily 8% glycolic acid cream, 8% L-lactic acid cream, or vehicle on the face and forearms for 22 weeks; 67 completed. On the face, 76% of glycolic acid users and 71% of lactic acid users achieved at least one grade of improvement in overall photodamage severity, versus 40% on vehicle (P<.05). On the forearms at week 22, lactic acid was reported as significantly superior to vehicle for overall photodamage severity, mottled hyperpigmentation, sallowness, and roughness. One participant withdrew for skin irritation. The authors concluded the two acids "are modestly useful in ameliorating some of the signs of chronic cutaneous photodamage" and are "well tolerated."[^stiller]

**Comparative effectiveness and irritation across AHAs.** A study evaluated glycolic acid, lactic acid (both l(+) and d(-) forms), citric, hydroxybutyric, and malic acids at 0.5 to 1.15 mol/L for stinging potential, cell renewal, moisture content, and lines and wrinkles over six weeks. "Results indicated that lactic l(+) and glycolic were the most effective acids when tested at equimolar concentrations," and both were "less irritating than the d(-) lactic acid form and the other acids tested." With long-term use, "both d(-) and l(+) lactic acid and glycolic acid produced comparative improvements in skin hydration and lines and wrinkle improvement," though l(+) lactic acid did so "with fewer consumer complaints than any of the other acids tested." When concentration rose above 1.5 mol/L, "a greater difference in irritation potential was observed with respect to stinging, with the l(+) form of lactic acid being less irritating than glycolic acid and the d(-) form." The paper concludes "lactic acid has a slight technical advantage over glycolic acid" on this stinging measure.[^smith-ijcs]

**Barrier function and irritant challenge.** Eleven healthy subjects had glycolic acid, lactic acid, tartaric acid, and gluconolactone, each at 8% in a base cream, applied twice daily for four weeks to test sites on the arm and forearm, alongside vehicle and untreated controls. "No significant differences in transepidermal water loss (TEWL) and erythema were observed between the four AHAs at week 4." After a standardized 5% sodium lauryl sulfate irritant challenge, sites pretreated with gluconolactone and tartaric acid showed significantly lower TEWL than glycolic acid (P<0.05) and than vehicle and untreated skin (P<0.01) at 24 and 48 hours; the same two acids, not lactic acid, also showed significantly reduced redness after irritation. The authors concluded AHAs "can modulate stratum corneum barrier function and prevent skin irritation" but "the effect is not equal for all AHAs."[^berardesca]

**Concentration-dependent skin changes.** In a 3-month trial, participants applied either 5% or 12% lactic acid twice daily. "Treatment with 12% lactic acid resulted in increased epidermal and dermal firmness and thickness and clinical improvement in skin smoothness and in the appearance of lines and wrinkles." "No dermal changes were observed after treatment with 5% lactic acid; however, similar clinical and epidermal changes were noted." The author concluded that "cosmetic benefits from the use of alpha-hydroxy acids are caused by modification of the skin surface, the epidermis and the dermis," with 5% acting on the surface and epidermis and 12% reaching the dermis as well.[^smith-jaad]

## Uses

Supported on controlled evidence, shared with glycolic acid: modest improvement in the appearance of photodamaged skin at 8%, tested head to head against glycolic acid rather than in isolation.[^stiller] Supported on smaller or single-study evidence: concentration-dependent improvement in skin smoothness and the appearance of lines and wrinkles, with a measurable dermal effect only at 12% and not 5%.[^smith-jaad] Mixed and study-dependent: the claim that lactic acid is gentler or more hydrating than glycolic acid, which held up on subjective stinging and consumer complaints in one comparative study but not on instrumented water loss and redness in another.[^smith-ijcs][^berardesca] General moisturizing and barrier evidence for other ingredient classes is covered on [[moisturizing]].

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Gentler than glycolic acid."** True in one comparative study on subjective stinging and consumer complaints, especially at higher concentrations,[^smith-ijcs] but a separate controlled trial measuring transepidermal water loss and erythema found no significant difference between 8% glycolic and 8% lactic acid, and rated two other AHAs (gluconolactone, tartaric acid) as giving better measured barrier protection than glycolic acid, not lactic acid.[^berardesca]
- **"More hydrating than glycolic acid."** The comparative trial that tested both found "comparative," not superior, hydration improvement for lactic acid versus glycolic acid at matched concentrations.[^smith-ijcs]
- **"Clinically proven to reduce photodamage."** The controlled evidence is for an 8% cream tested alongside an equal-strength glycolic acid cream, both modestly effective and statistically similar to each other, not a unique lactic acid result.[^stiller]
