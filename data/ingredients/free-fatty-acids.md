Free fatty acids are one of the three lipid classes, alongside [[ceramides]] and cholesterol, that pack the spaces between the flattened dead cells of the outermost skin layer (the stratum corneum) and form the barrier that limits water loss. They are rarely sold as a standalone active; their evidence is almost entirely about what happens when they are present, absent, or mixed with the other two barrier lipids in the right proportion, not about applying fatty acids alone.

> The barrier lipids work as a proportioned mixture rather than as separate ingredients: an experimental study found that applying only one or two of ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids to damaged skin delayed recovery, while "only equimolar mixtures allow normal recovery," with further acceleration as the ratio of any one lipid rose up to three-fold.[^man] A separate line of older animal evidence found that a diet or topical deficiency in one specific free fatty acid, linoleic acid, breaks the barrier and that supplying it topically restores water-loss measurements to normal.[^prottey]

## The Rubric

**Permeability barrier repair, as part of the ceramide-cholesterol-fatty acid triad**
- **Effect size: real in the physiologic-mixture experiments, but a component effect, not a free-fatty-acid effect on its own.** The stratum corneum's intercellular lipids sit in "the approximately 1:1:1 molar ratio of ceramides (Cer), cholesterol, and nonessential fatty acids," and Elias's review, from the same research group as the Man study, describes all three as required "to form the lamellar structures that regulate barrier function."[^elias] Man and colleagues tested this directly on experimentally stripped skin: ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids "are required for permeability barrier homeostasis," applying only one or two of the three "delays barrier recovery," and only an equimolar mixture of all three restored normal repair, measured by transepidermal water loss.[^man] Isolated free fatty acids applied alone are therefore not shown to help, and in this model can slow recovery relative to no treatment at all. "Real in the complete equimolar mixture; not shown to work as a fatty acid applied by itself."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, mostly in an animal model, and largely from one lab.** The equimolar-mixture findings come from experimentally stripped mouse skin, with the authors reporting "similar preliminary results" in damaged human skin rather than a full human trial.[^man] Three of the four primary sources cited on this page (Man 1996, whose authors include Feingold and Elias, plus Elias 2012 and Feingold 2007) come out of the same UCSF Elias/Feingold research group, so the mixture/structural case rests largely on a single lab rather than independent corroboration; Prottey 1976, below, is the one genuinely independent primary source. Feingold's 2007 review, from that same group, adds context on the epidermis's own fatty-acid metabolism, noting that "the elongation and desaturation of fatty acids is also required for barrier homeostasis," a synthesis pathway rather than a topical-application claim.[^feingold]

**Essential fatty acid (linoleic acid) deficiency and barrier restoration**
- **Effect size: strong within an animal deficiency model, not established in humans.** In rats made essential-fatty-acid deficient, epidermal barrier function was "manifested by a greatly increased rate of transepidermal water loss"; topical sunflower seed oil, rich in linoleic acid, "rapidly restored to normal" the elevated water loss, with linoleic acid measurably incorporated into epidermal lipids, while olive oil, low in linoleic acid, "did not influence epidermal barrier function."[^prottey] "Strong and specific to linoleic acid in deficient rats; a different fatty acid (oleic acid) did not work."
- **Evidence quality: a single 1976 rodent study.** The Prottey experiment used chemically or dietarily induced deficiency in rats, not a controlled human trial, and is nearly fifty years old.[^prottey]

## What We Actually Know

Free fatty acids are one leg of a three-part structural mixture, not an ingredient with an efficacy trial of its own. The stratum corneum's water barrier depends on ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids sitting in roughly equal (1:1:1) molar proportion,[^elias] and an experimental test of that mixture on damaged skin found that supplying only one or two of the three lipids delayed barrier recovery while the complete equimolar combination restored it, with further gains as any one component was increased up to three-fold.[^man] Separately, an older rat study tied one specific free fatty acid, linoleic acid, to barrier integrity: deficient animals had elevated water loss that topical linoleic acid corrected, while a fatty acid lacking linoleic acid (oleic acid, via olive oil) did not.[^prottey] Neither line of evidence supports applying free fatty acids in isolation as a treatment; the case is for the complete physiologic lipid mixture, covered from the ceramide side on [[ceramides]] and at the moisturizer-category level on [[moisturizing]].

## How It Works

"Free" fatty acids are the non-esterified fatty acids of the stratum corneum's intercellular lipid lamellae, distinct from the fatty acids esterified into triglycerides, phospholipids, or the acylceramides. Elias's structural account places them alongside cholesterol "lying alongside the N-acyl group of Cer" in the lamellar sheets between corneocytes, with the three lipid classes together, roughly 1:1:1 by mole, forming the "lamellar structures that regulate barrier function."[^elias] He specifies these as "nonessential fatty acids," distinguishing the bulk free-fatty-acid pool from linoleic acid, the essential fatty acid that instead esterifies onto certain ceramide species (acylceramides) as a separate structural role.[^elias] The epidermis makes much of its own free-fatty-acid supply: Feingold's review notes that "the elongation and desaturation of fatty acids is also required for barrier homeostasis," describing a de novo synthetic pathway in keratinocytes rather than a supply drawn only from diet or surface oils.[^feingold] Linoleic acid is the exception that a cell cannot make itself; Prottey's deficiency experiments showed that when it is missing, water loss rises, and replacing it topically, but not with a non-essential fatty acid like oleic acid, corrects the defect.[^prottey]

## The Evidence

**Equimolar physiologic-lipid mixtures (rodent, with preliminary human data).** Man, Feingold, Thornfeldt, and Elias applied combinations of ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids (linoleic, palmitic, or stearic acid) to experimentally perturbed mouse skin. "Application of one or two of these lipids to perturbed skin delays barrier recovery; only equimolar mixtures allow normal recovery," measured by transepidermal water loss, and "further acceleration of barrier repair occurs as the ratio of any of these ingredients is increased up to 3-fold." The authors reported "similar preliminary results" in damaged human skin.[^man]

**Structural basis (review).** Elias's 2012 structural review describes the stratum corneum extracellular matrix as built on "the approximately 1:1:1 molar ratio of ceramides (Cer), cholesterol, and nonessential fatty acids," with cholesterol and free fatty acids positioned alongside ceramide's acyl chain to form the lamellae that "regulate barrier function," and separately notes that linoleic acid's role is to esterify certain ceramide species (acylCer) rather than to sit in the bulk free-fatty-acid pool.[^elias]

**Epidermal fatty-acid metabolism and deficiency (review).** Feingold's 2007 review states the extracellular lipid matrix is "50% ceramides, 25% cholesterol, and 15% free fatty acids," that "essential fatty acid deficiency results in abnormalities in stratum corneum structure function," and that fatty-acid elongation and desaturation in the epidermis itself is required for barrier homeostasis.[^feingold]

**Linoleic acid deficiency and repair (rat model).** Prottey and colleagues induced impaired epidermal barrier function in rats by essential-fatty-acid deficiency and, separately, by a cutaneous irritant. Barrier impairment was "manifested by a greatly increased rate of transepidermal water loss." Topical sunflower seed oil "rapidly restored to normal" the elevated water loss in both models, with linoleic acid from the oil incorporated into epidermal lipids, while olive oil, low in linoleic acid, "did not influence epidermal barrier function," leading the authors to conclude "a close relationship of barrier function and essential fatty acids is indicated."[^prottey]

## Uses

Supported only as part of the complete physiologic lipid mixture, not as an isolated ingredient: barrier repair after experimental skin damage required ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids together in roughly equimolar proportion, with one or two of the three lipids alone delaying rather than helping recovery.[^man][^elias] Supported for one specific free fatty acid, linoleic acid, in an animal deficiency model: restoring it topically corrected an essential-fatty-acid-deficient barrier, while a non-essential fatty acid (oleic acid) did not.[^prottey] This is the mechanistic basis for ceramide-cholesterol-fatty acid moisturizer formulations generally; the product-level clinical evidence for that combination sits on [[ceramides]], and the broader case for emollients and eczema on [[moisturizing]].

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Free fatty acids repair the skin barrier."** The controlled evidence is for the complete equimolar ceramide-cholesterol-fatty acid mixture, not for a fatty acid applied alone; an incomplete mixture that includes only free fatty acids (or only one or two of the three lipids) delayed recovery in the Man experiments rather than helping it.[^man]
- **A product listing "free fatty acids" or a specific fatty acid (such as linoleic or oleic acid) as an active.** Most of the direct evidence concerns the epidermis's own lipid balance and a complete physiologic mixture, not a topical fatty acid ingredient tested in isolation; the one fatty acid with dedicated deficiency-repair evidence, linoleic acid, was tested in essential-fatty-acid-deficient rats, not in a general skincare context.[^prottey]
