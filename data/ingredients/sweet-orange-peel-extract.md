A citrus peel extract with exactly one study behind it, run without a control group. **Bottom line:** underarm pigment fell over eight weeks in the 32 women who used it, and nobody measured what would have happened without it.

## The Rubric

**Lightening underarm hyperpigmentation (cosmetic)**
Graded unknown on preliminary evidence. The study is real and its numbers are real: a melanin index of "349.04 ± 109.84 AU which decreased to 253.06 ± 96.36 AU after 8 weeks of treatment. (p < 0.001)".[^review] What it lacks is anyone to compare that against. In a single-arm study the measured change includes everything else that happened over eight weeks, from the moisturising base to the simple act of stopping whatever was irritating the skin.

**Any other pigmentation, anywhere else (cosmetic)**
Graded unknown on anecdotal evidence. The axillae are the only site studied. [[hyperpigmentation]] covers the ingredients with evidence behind them for pigment generally.

## The Evidence

**One study, and its design is the finding.** A 2025 systematic review of axillary hyperpigmentation treatments found ten studies in total, of which one covers this ingredient: Oei et al. 2024, described in the review's own table as "Observational", in "32 females, 18-25", who "applied one fingertip of sweet orange peels (Citrus sinensis L.) 0.1% extract cream twice daily for 8 weeks".[^review] No randomisation, no placebo, no comparator.

**What it reported.** Besides the melanin index change, "68.8% of patients showed mild improvement (26%-50%) and 25% experienced good improvement (51%-75%)" on a physician global assessment, with adverse events recorded in the table as "None."[^review] A physician global assessment in an unblinded single-arm study is the weakest kind of outcome measure, because the assessor knows everyone was treated.

**The review's own ranking does not put it near the top.** Its abstract says topical agents including this one "demonstrated varying degrees of pigmentation reduction", and then names the two it considers to have shown "significant effectiveness and minimal side effects": desonide and Cyperus rotundus oil.[^review] Sweet orange extract is not among them. The review's authors declare no conflicts of interest.[^review]

**The mechanism offered is plausible and untested here.** The review states that sweet orange "contains polyphenols, vitamin A, vitamin E, and ascorbic acid, and its peel exhibits an anti-tyrosinase effect", tyrosinase being the enzyme melanin synthesis depends on.[^review] An anti-tyrosinase effect in a dish is a reason to run a trial, not a result from one, and the trial that was run could not isolate it.

**One thing worth raising that the study does not address.** Expressed citrus peel preparations are a classic cause of plant-triggered photosensitivity, which is why [[phytophotodermatitis]] exists as a condition. Nothing in the material read here reports the furocoumarin content of the 0.1% extract cream studied, or tests it under sun exposure[^review], so this page can neither raise nor dismiss that concern. It is a gap in the evidence rather than a finding either way.

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven unless it also appears in The Evidence above. No brand page was fetched for this ingredient, so these are the claims the evidence itself invites rather than wording quoted from a seller.

- **That it is clinically proven to fade dark underarms.** One uncontrolled study in 32 women is not a clinical proof of anything, whatever its p value.[^review]
- **That it inhibits tyrosinase, therefore it lightens skin.** The first half is the review's own description of the peel; the second half is a leap no study here makes.[^review]
- **That a citrus extract is gentler because it is natural.** Citrus peel preparations are the standard cause of plant-triggered photosensitivity, which is a reason for more care rather than less; see [[phytophotodermatitis]].
