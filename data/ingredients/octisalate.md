Octisalate (INCI ethylhexyl salicylate, also called octyl salicylate or 2-ethylhexyl salicylate) is an organic ultraviolet-B sunscreen filter permitted in United States over-the-counter sunscreens at up to 5%, where it works as a weak, secondary UVB absorber rather than a product's main filter.[^cir][^fda2019] A safety review by the Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel classifies it plainly, calling salicylic acid and ethylhexyl salicylate "low-level photoprotective agents," and a photophysical study groups it among "UV-B sunscreens."[^cir][^krishnan] UVB (roughly 290 to 320 nm) is the sunburn and DNA-damage band; UVA (320 to 400 nm) is the deeper photoaging band, and octisalate covers essentially none of it, so it is combined with a dedicated UVA filter such as avobenzone, the main UVA filter in US sunscreens, covered on [[avobenzone]].[^latha][^cir] Regular use of a broad-spectrum sunscreen has randomized-trial support for slowing photoaging and reducing skin-cancer burden, and that health benefit belongs to the practice and the whole formula, not to octisalate as a single filter; it is documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^latha] Two questions shape how it is graded: how much protection a weak UVB co-filter actually delivers, and what its measured systemic absorption means.

> Octisalate is a weak organic UVB filter used as a co-filter rather than a main one, contributing modestly to a product's SPF and nothing to UVA coverage; at its 5% US maximum the FDA lists it "Category III," meaning the safety record is insufficient for a positive determination, and two independent studies measure it entering the body through skin, an absorption that has not been linked to any harm.[^cir][^fda2019][^matta2020][^hiller][^krishnan]

## The Rubric

The grade below is for octisalate's job in a sunscreen, ultraviolet-B absorption, framed relative to the other filters it sits alongside. Systemic absorption and safety are a separate axis and are graded separately, not folded into the photoprotection grade.

**Ultraviolet-B photoprotection (as a secondary filter)**
- **Effect size: minimal on its own.** Octisalate is a weak UVB absorber. The Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel places it among "low-level photoprotective agents," and a photophysical characterization treats octyl salicylate as one of a set of "UV-B sunscreens."[^cir][^krishnan] It absorbs in the UVB band (about 290 to 320 nm) and contributes little to a product's SPF, a UVB metric; it provides essentially no UVA (320 to 400 nm) coverage, so it does not stand in for a UVA filter and is formulated alongside one such as [[avobenzone]].[^latha][^cir] "Minimal as a standalone filter. It is a co-filter that adds some UVB absorbance and no meaningful UVA protection, well short of the primary UVB and UVA filters it is combined with."
- **Evidence quality: solid for the characterization; the health payoff is inherited, not filter-specific.** That octisalate is a low-level UVB absorber is documented in an expert-panel safety assessment and a photophysical study.[^cir][^krishnan] What is not demonstrated for octisalate on its own is any clinical-endpoint benefit; the randomized evidence that regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use slows photoaging and lowers skin-cancer burden is for the practice and the finished product, covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], and octisalate is one minor contributor to the UVB half of that protection.[^latha]

**Systemic absorption and safety (a separate axis, not an efficacy grade)**
- Two independent studies show octisalate is absorbed through skin into the body. In an FDA maximal-use randomized trial, octisalate reached geometric mean maximum plasma concentrations of 4.6 to 5.8 ng/mL across three spray products, above the 0.5 ng/mL threshold at which the agency asks for further nonclinical safety testing.[^matta2020] A separate real-life dermal-exposure study recovered the parent compound and its metabolites in urine, with a long terminal elimination half-life.[^hiller] In its 2019 proposed sunscreen rule the FDA placed octisalate, with eleven other organic filters, in "Category III," because the record lacked "sufficient data to support positive GRASE determinations," and requested more data.[^fda2019] No health harm from this absorption has been demonstrated, and the authors of the plasma trial wrote that the results "do not indicate that individuals should refrain from the use of sunscreen."[^matta2020] Allergic contact dermatitis to octisalate is documented but uncommon.[^shaw] The absorption itself is well established; its clinical significance is unresolved.

## What We Actually Know

Octisalate is a weak organic UVB filter used as a secondary ingredient rather than a product's main filter. The Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel calls salicylates "low-level photoprotective agents," and photophysical work classes octyl salicylate among the "UV-B sunscreens."[^cir][^krishnan] It absorbs in the UVB band (about 290 to 320 nm), adds modestly to SPF, and covers essentially no UVA, so a broad-spectrum formula pairs it with a dedicated UVA filter such as avobenzone.[^latha][^cir] Its health value is the value of using a broad-spectrum sunscreen regularly, slowing photoaging and lowering skin-cancer burden, which is a property of the practice and the whole formula documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], not of octisalate in isolation.[^latha] On safety, two independent studies show measurable systemic uptake through skin, an FDA maximal-use plasma trial and a real-life dermal-exposure biomonitoring study, which is one reason the FDA lists it Category III and has asked for more data; no harm has been shown.[^matta2020][^hiller][^fda2019] The filter is also detected in surface waters internationally.[^schneiderlim]

## How It Works

Octisalate is the 2-ethylhexyl ester of salicylic acid, an organic (chemical) filter that absorbs a UV photon and re-emits the energy as heat rather than scattering light the way mineral filters do. Its absorbance sits in the ultraviolet-B region (about 290 to 320 nm), the wavelengths that cause sunburn and the pyrimidine-dimer DNA damage implicated in keratinocyte cancers; it does not meaningfully absorb ultraviolet-A (320 to 400 nm), the deeper photoaging band.[^latha][^cir] Because it is a weak absorber, its practical role is as a co-filter that adds some UVB coverage and helps raise a product's SPF, not as the ingredient carrying the protection; the Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel describes it as a "low-level photoprotective agent."[^cir] A fluorescence study of its excited-state behavior found that, among the UVB filters tested, "octyl salicylate emits from a single excited state," a simpler photophysical profile than the multi-state behavior of octinoxate and padimate O measured in the same work.[^krishnan] It supplies no UVA protection, which is why broad-spectrum products combine it with a UVA filter such as avobenzone, the main UVA filter in US over-the-counter sunscreens, covered on [[avobenzone]].[^cir][^latha]

## The Evidence

Primary evidence, by question.

**Spectral coverage and filter strength.** Octisalate is characterized as a weak UVB absorber. The Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel's safety assessment states that "Ethylhexyl Salicylate (formerly known as Octyl Salicylate) is used as a fragrance ingredient, sunscreen agent, and UV light absorber," and that "Salicylic Acid and Ethylhexyl Salicylate are low-level photoprotective agents."[^cir] A fluorescence-dynamics study of "three UV-B sunscreens" treats octyl salicylate as one of them and reports that it "emits from a single excited state."[^krishnan] The UVB band it absorbs is defined in a dermatology review as "UVB (290-320)" nm, distinct from "UVA (320-400nm)," which octisalate does not meaningfully cover.[^latha]

**Systemic absorption.** Two independent studies measured how much octisalate enters the body after topical use. In an FDA maximal-use randomized trial (48 participants, sunscreen applied at 2 mg/cm2 to 75% of body surface, a single application on day 1 then four times daily on days 2 to 4), octisalate reached geometric mean maximum plasma concentrations of "5.1 ng/mL (CV, 81.6%) for aerosol spray, 5.8 ng/mL (CV, 77.4%) for nonaerosol spray, and 4.6 ng/mL (CV, 97.6%) for pump spray," all above the 0.5 ng/mL level below which the FDA may waive further nonclinical safety testing.[^matta2020] In a separate real-life dermal-exposure study, twenty volunteers were exposed to a commercial sunscreen for nine hours ("2 mg/cm2 body surface area; double re-application; corresponding to 3.8 g EHS"), and the parent compound and its oxidized metabolite were recovered in urine, with cumulative 24-hour excretion up to 334 micrograms of ethylhexyl salicylate and 124 micrograms of the metabolite 5OH-EHS, which peaked at 213 micrograms per gram creatinine and cleared with a terminal half-life of about 87 hours.[^hiller] The recovered amounts are a small fraction of the 3.8 grams applied, but they establish measurable systemic uptake through skin.

**Regulatory status.** Octisalate is a permitted US OTC sunscreen active at a maximum concentration of 5%.[^fda2019] In the FDA's 2019 proposed sunscreen rule the agency proposed that octisalate and eleven other organic filters be classified "Category III," stating "the public record does not currently contain sufficient data to support positive GRASE determinations for cinoxate, dioxybenzone, ensulizole, homosalate, meradimate, octinoxate, octisalate, octocrylene, padimate O, sulisobenzone, oxybenzone, or avobenzone, we are proposing that these ingredients are Category III," and requested additional safety data.[^fda2019] Ethylhexyl salicylate is also a UV filter in widespread cosmetic use in the European Union, where the German Human Biomonitoring Commission derived reference values for its urinary metabolites.[^hbm] It therefore remains available and widely used while long-term safety data are still being sought.

**Contact allergy and environmental presence.** Allergic contact dermatitis to octisalate is documented but uncommon; a case report describes a patient who "developed allergic contact dermatitis from sunscreens containing octisalate (octyl salicylate, 2-ethylhexyl salicylate)."[^shaw] Separately, a review of the environmental effects of sunscreen actives reports that ethylhexyl salicylate is among the UV filters detected in water sources worldwide.[^schneiderlim]

## Uses

Well-supported: as a secondary ultraviolet-B filter in a broad-spectrum sunscreen, where octisalate adds some UVB absorbance and contributes to a product's SPF alongside stronger UVB filters and a dedicated UVA filter such as avobenzone.[^cir][^latha] The health benefit of using such a sunscreen regularly, slowing photoaging and lowering skin-cancer burden, is a property of the practice and the whole formula documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], not of octisalate on its own.[^latha] Its practical limits are that it is a weak absorber that cannot carry a product's protection by itself and that it covers no UVA, so it is not used as a standalone filter.[^cir] It is also absorbed systemically above the FDA's testing threshold, which is one reason the agency has requested more safety data.[^matta2020][^hiller][^fda2019]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Broad-spectrum protection" (attributed to octisalate).** Octisalate is a UVB-only filter and provides essentially no UVA coverage.[^cir][^latha] Any broad-spectrum performance of a product containing it comes from its other filters, particularly a UVA filter such as avobenzone; that landscape is covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^latha]
- **"Gentle / non-irritating sunscreen filter."** Octisalate is generally well tolerated, but allergic contact dermatitis to it is documented in case reports, so it is not free of sensitization risk.[^shaw]
- **"Chemical filters are toxic / hormone disruptors."** The demonstrated fact is measurable systemic absorption, shown in an FDA plasma trial and a real-life dermal-exposure study.[^matta2020][^hiller] That absorption triggers the FDA's Category III request for more data, not a finding of harm, and the plasma-trial authors stated the results "do not indicate that individuals should refrain from the use of sunscreen."[^fda2019][^matta2020] No health harm from octisalate at label use has been demonstrated.
