Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, the active ingredient in household bleach) is used in dermatology as a highly diluted "bleach bath": a small amount of household bleach added to a tub of bathwater, soaked in for several minutes and rinsed off.[^pubchem] In the trials below the regimen was a diluted-bleach soak for 10 minutes, twice weekly.[^wong] It is recommended as an adjunctive measure for moderate-to-severe [[atopic-dermatitis]] to reduce *Staphylococcus aureus* skin colonization, which is elevated in eczematous skin and thought to drive flares, but the best available systematic review found bleach baths were not significantly more effective than plain water baths at reducing eczema severity.[^chopra]

> A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis of 5 studies found "no significant differences observed between bleach vs water baths at 4 weeks vs baseline for the Eczema Area and Severity Index (I2 = 98%; random effect regression model, P = .16) or body surface area (I2 = 96%; P = .36)."[^chopra]

## The Rubric

**Adjunctive therapy for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis with secondary bacterial signs**
- **Effect size: modest at best, and not clearly distinguishable from bathing in plain water.** Individual trials report improvement from baseline: a Malaysian randomized, placebo-controlled trial found bleach baths produced "significant reductions in EASI scores" over 2 months, with *S. aureus* skin density falling 41.9% from baseline at 1 month and 53.3% at 2 months.[^wong] But when the 2017 meta-analysis pooled trials against a plain-water comparator, the difference disappeared (P = .16 for EASI, P = .36 for body surface area), and of 4 studies that directly compared bleach with water, "only 2 found significantly greater decreases in AD severity with bleach baths, 1 found greater decreases with water baths, and 1 found no significant differences."[^chopra]
- **Evidence quality: thin and heterogeneous.** Only 5 studies exist in the pooled review, with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 96 to 98%), small sample sizes, and inconsistent bleach concentrations and bathing schedules across trials.[^chopra] The most-cited positive trial (Huang 2009) combined bleach baths with intranasal mupirocin ointment and compared that combination against plain-water baths with petrolatum ointment, so it cannot isolate the bleach bath's own contribution; it also found no benefit at the head and neck, sites not submerged in the bath.[^huang] Bathing itself (hydration, scale removal, ritual moisturization afterward) is a plausible confounder that dilute bleach may not add much to.

## How It Works

Sodium hypochlorite is a chlorine-based oxidizing antimicrobial (molecular formula ClNaO) with broad-spectrum activity against bacteria including *S. aureus*, the organism recovered from a large majority of atopic dermatitis lesions and implicated in exacerbating skin inflammation and barrier disruption.[^pubchem][^huang] The rationale for bleach baths is that regular, low-concentration exposure of the skin surface suppresses this bacterial colonization, which in turn should reduce the inflammatory drive of eczema flares; the Malaysia trial documented this mechanism directly, with quantitative *S. aureus* skin cultures dropping substantially over 2 months of use.[^wong] Whether that documented antibacterial effect translates into a skin-severity benefit beyond what water alone provides is exactly the point the 2017 meta-analysis could not confirm.[^chopra]

## The Evidence

**Wong 2013, randomized, placebo-controlled (bleach vs distilled water baths).** Patients aged 2 to 30 with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis in Malaysia soaked in diluted bleach or distilled water baths for 10 minutes, twice weekly for 2 months. The bleach group showed "significant reductions in EASI scores," and *S. aureus* density fell "41.9% reduction... from baseline... at 1 month further reducing to 53.3% at 2 months"; side effects were mild and equally frequent in both arms, with no withdrawals for intolerance.[^wong] *A clean bleach-vs-water design with quantified bacterial reduction and clinical improvement, though the abstract does not report the between-group EASI comparison numerically.*

**Huang 2009, randomized, investigator-blinded, placebo-controlled (bleach bath + mupirocin vs water bath + petrolatum).** Thirty-one patients aged 6 months to 17 years with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis and signs of secondary bacterial infection all received 14 days of oral cephalexin, then were randomized to dilute sodium hypochlorite baths plus intranasal mupirocin, or plain water baths plus intranasal petrolatum, for 3 months. The treatment group "showed significantly greater mean reductions from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index scores, compared with the placebo group, at the 1-month and 3-month visits," but "the mean Eczema Area and Severity Index scores for the head and neck did not decrease," since those sites were not submerged in the bath.[^huang] *This is the trial most often cited for bleach baths, but the intervention bundled bleach with intranasal mupirocin against a water-plus-petrolatum control, so the reported benefit cannot be attributed to the bleach bath alone.*

**Chopra 2017, systematic review and meta-analysis (5 studies).** Pooling studies that compared bleach against water baths, "four studies reported significantly decreased AD severity in patients treated with bleach on at least 1 time point," but of the 4 studies with a water-bath comparator, results were split: 2 favored bleach, 1 favored water, and 1 found no difference. In pooled random-effects analysis there was "no significant differences observed between bleach vs water baths at 4 weeks vs baseline for the Eczema Area and Severity Index (I2 = 98%; random effect regression model, P = .16) or body surface area (I2 = 96%; P = .36)."[^chopra] *The authors' own conclusion is blunt: bleach baths decrease AD severity from baseline, but "do not appear to be more effective than water baths alone," and they call for larger, better-designed trials.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Bleach baths kill the staph bacteria driving your eczema."** The antimicrobial mechanism is real and documented: dilute sodium hypochlorite measurably reduces *S. aureus* skin density in trials.[^wong] What is not established is that this translates into a skin outcome meaningfully better than soaking in plain water, per the pooled meta-analysis.[^chopra]
- **"A proven, guideline-backed eczema treatment."** Dilute bleach baths are a commonly recommended adjunct in atopic dermatitis management and do improve severity scores from baseline in individual trials, but "proven" overstates the comparative evidence; the best synthesis available found no statistically significant edge over water baths, with the field's most-cited positive trial confounded by concurrent mupirocin use.[^chopra][^huang]
