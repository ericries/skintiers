Lanolin is the waxy secretion of sheep's wool, refined into an emollient. It is one of the few cosmetic-sounding ingredients that is also a recognised over-the-counter drug active, and it carries a reputation as a major allergen that the published frequency does not support.

## The Rubric

- **Effect size: modest, as an occlusive emollient.** Lanolin appears as a declared active ingredient in over-the-counter skin protectant drugs, almost always alongside [[petrolatum]]. In a query of openFDA's drug listing directory for products declaring LANOLIN as an active, 50 of 51 returned records carry marketing category OTC MONOGRAPH DRUG, and the common pairing is lanolin at 15.5 g/100 g with petrolatum at 53.4 g/100 g.[^ndc] That is regulatory recognition of the protectant role, not a measurement of how much it helps.
- **Evidence quality: mixed.** The one efficacy synthesis read for this page is a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of lanolin, aloe vera and peppermint for nipple pain and trauma in lactating mothers: 11 studies, 1,495 participants, average age 29 plus or minus 5 years, which concludes that "the application of lanolin significantly reduced nipple pain and nipple trauma in lactating mothers".[^nipple] Its abstract gives no effect size, no confidence interval and no p-value for that result, so the direction is supported and the magnitude is not. The grade on this page is `mid` for exactly that reason.
- **Tolerability: better than the reputation, worse for some groups.** A 2023 review in *Dermatitis* describes lanolin as "a weak sensitizer" and reports that "the frequency of contact allergy to it in the European population reportedly is 0.4%".[^review] It also names the groups where that number does not apply: "stasis dermatitis, leg ulcers, perianal/genital dermatitis, and atopic dermatitis", plus children and the elderly.[^review]
- **A testing caveat worth knowing before you avoid it.** The same review states that detecting lanolin-induced dermatitis "in diseased skin by patch testing on normal skin may lead to false negative results", and, in the other direction, that "patients with a positive patch test to lanolin may tolerate use of lanolin on normal skin".[^review] A patch-test result in either direction is therefore weaker evidence about your own tolerance than it sounds.

## What We Actually Know

It occludes and it is cheap, its protectant role is recognised in the over-the-counter monograph system, and the one pooled analysis found here supports its use on damaged nipple skin without saying by how much. Everything else about it is an argument about allergy that has, per the review above, been running since the 1920s.

## How It Works

As an occlusive and emollient. The 2023 review describes it as "a complex mixture of high molecular weight esters, aliphatic alcohols, sterols, fatty acids, and hydrocarbons" used "for centuries for its emollient properties", refined since 1882 into waxes and derivatives including modern highly purified anhydrous grades.[^review] Nothing read for this page attributes an active biological mechanism to it beyond barrier occlusion; the general case for occlusion is on [[petrolatum]].

## The Evidence

The nipple-trauma meta-analysis is the strongest efficacy evidence located, and its abstract withholds every number behind its conclusion, which is a recurring failure mode catalogued on [[study-pages-recorded-as-unusable]]. Treat it as supporting the direction only.

On allergy, the 0.4% European figure is the useful anchor: it makes lanolin a weak sensitizer at population level, while the named high-risk groups are precisely the people most likely to be reaching for an occlusive in the first place, since leg ulcers, stasis dermatitis and [[atopic-dermatitis]] all involve broken or inflamed skin. The review also reports a demographic difference in the United States, that "non-Hispanic white patients are more likely than their non-Hispanic black counterparts to be lanolin allergic".[^review] No mechanism for that difference is given in the abstract and none is proposed here.

## Uses

Skin protectant and emollient in over-the-counter drug products, usually with petrolatum; nipple care during breastfeeding; lip and dry-skin products, including [[aquaphor-healing-ointment]], which contains it. General dry-skin context is on [[dry-skin]] and lip-specific context on [[lip-care]].

## Common Marketing Claims

> Quoted phrasing below is characteristic of the category rather than taken from one brand, and nothing here is endorsed.

- "Hypoallergenic" on a lanolin-containing product. The term has no agreed regulatory definition, and lanolin's own 0.4% population figure neither justifies nor refutes it for any individual product.[^review]
- "Natural" or "from wool". True and irrelevant to tolerance: the allergenicity debate concerns the refined fraction, and purification grade is what the literature argues about.[^review]
- Implications that a lanolin allergy can be ruled in or out by a patch test alone. The 2023 review says otherwise in both directions.[^review]
