Gluconolactone is a polyhydroxy acid (PHA), a larger cousin of [[glycolic-acid]] and the other AHAs, marketed on the premise that its bigger molecule penetrates skin more slowly and so exfoliates with less irritation while also acting as a humectant.[^grimes] The best controlled evidence for the irritation claim is one small barrier study that pitted gluconolactone directly against glycolic acid; the hydration and UV-related claims rest on fewer or more recent studies, some from the same research group that has also studied gluconolactone chemical peels.

> Against a sodium lauryl sulphate irritation challenge, a controlled trial found gluconolactone-pretreated skin held up better than glycolic-acid-pretreated skin on measured water loss, and a review of PHAs reports they "do not cause the sensory irritation responses that can limit the use of classical AHAs" while adding humectant properties AHAs lack.[^berardesca][^grimes] Separate split-face trials of 10% and 30% gluconolactone found increased skin hydration and reduced transepidermal water loss, with no added benefit from the higher concentration.[^corneometric][^tewl]

## The Rubric

**Barrier tolerance versus glycolic acid (topical, controlled irritation challenge)**
- **Effect size: modest, and better than glycolic acid on this one measure.** Eleven healthy subjects applied four different 8% acids, including glycolic acid and gluconolactone, twice daily for four weeks, then underwent a 5% sodium lauryl sulphate patch test under occlusion for six hours.[^berardesca] Gluconolactone-treated sites showed "significantly lower TEWL compared with VE, UNT (P < 0.01) and GA (P < 0.05) both at 24 and 48 h" after the challenge, with erythema following the same pattern.[^berardesca] The authors concluded that AHAs "can modulate stratum corneum barrier function and prevent skin irritation," but "the effect is not equal for all AHAs, being more marked for the molecules characterized by antioxidant properties."[^berardesca] This is a single small trial (n=11) measuring how skin responds to a harsh detergent after weeks of pretreatment, not a head-to-head trial of the acids' own irritation on first use. "Modest; in one small controlled challenge, pretreatment with gluconolactone left skin more resistant to a detergent irritant than pretreatment with glycolic acid, at matched 8% strength."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One randomized trial with 11 subjects, comparing four acids at a single concentration (8%).[^berardesca]

**Hydration and skin barrier measures (topical, 10% and 30% solutions)**
- **Effect size: modest.** A split-face trial in 16 women measured skin hydration by corneometry after gluconolactone applications at 10% and 30%, finding "a significant increase in facial skin hydration" at both strengths, with "no significant differences... observed between the application of 10% and 30% solution," and concluding gluconolactone "is a moisturizing substance which works well in dry skin care."[^corneometric] A related split-face trial by the same group, using gluconolactone as a chemical peel at the same two concentrations, found transepidermal water loss "significantly lower" at several facial sites after a series of treatments and pH reduced at all sites, again with comparable results between 10% and 30%.[^tewl] This overlaps the general evidence that hydrating ingredients support the barrier; see [[moisturizing]]. "Modest; two split-face trials from one research group found gluconolactone raised hydration and lowered water loss at 10% and 30%, with no extra benefit from tripling the concentration."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Both trials are from the same group, 16 subjects each, unblinded split-face design, and use gluconolactone as an in-office peel/application rather than a leave-on cosmetic formula.[^corneometric][^tewl]

**UV-related protection (in vitro / ex vivo)**
- **Effect size: unclear for real-world use.** In an in vitro transgenic model of photoaging plus human skin samples, gluconolactone "provided up to 50% protection against UV radiation, as measured in our in vitro system, and did not significantly increase sunburn cells in human skin."[^bernstein] This measures UV-induced elastin-promoter activation in a lab model and a proxy irritation measure (sunburn cell counts) after UV exposure, not sunscreen-grade UV protection or a clinical photoaging outcome, and it does not substitute for [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]. "Unclear for real-world use; an in vitro/ex vivo signal, not a measured SPF or a clinical photoaging trial."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** A single in vitro/ex vivo study.[^bernstein]

## What We Actually Know

Gluconolactone is a polyhydroxy acid used as a gentler alternative to AHAs like [[glycolic-acid]]. The strongest controlled evidence for that gentleness claim is one small trial (11 subjects) in which pretreatment with 8% gluconolactone left skin significantly more resistant to a detergent irritation challenge than pretreatment with 8% glycolic acid, measured by transepidermal water loss and erythema.[^berardesca] Split-face trials at 10% and 30% found gluconolactone raised skin hydration and lowered water loss, with the two concentrations performing about the same.[^corneometric][^tewl] A review of PHAs describes them as providing "similar effects as AHAs" while adding "humectant and moisturization properties" and avoiding the "sensory irritation responses" that limit classical AHAs.[^grimes] A UV-related claim rests on a single in vitro/ex vivo study showing partial protection against UV-induced elastin-promoter activation in a lab model, not a measured sunscreen effect.[^bernstein]

## How It Works

Gluconolactone is an aldonic acid derived from gluconic acid, larger and more hydrophilic than the classic AHAs, which the PHA literature credits for slower skin penetration and, by extension, less irritation than smaller acids like glycolic acid.[^grimes] Its multiple hydroxyl groups also make it a humectant, which the Berardesca trial's authors linked to its comparatively strong protective effect against a detergent challenge, alongside tartaric acid, attributing the advantage to "molecules characterized by antioxidant properties."[^berardesca][^grimes]

## The Evidence

Primary clinical evidence, by use.

**Barrier tolerance versus glycolic acid.** Eleven healthy subjects (mean age 28±6) had four different 8% AHA formulations, plus a vehicle and an untreated control, applied to six sites on the volar arm and forearm twice a day for four weeks.[^berardesca] At week four, a 5% sodium lauryl sulphate challenge patch was applied under occlusion for six hours to test how each pretreated site handled a standardized irritant.[^berardesca] "GLU- and TA-treated sites resulted in significantly lower TEWL compared with VE, UNT (P < 0.01) and GA (P < 0.05) both at 24 and 48 h," and erythema scores followed the same pattern.[^berardesca] The authors concluded AHAs "can modulate stratum corneum barrier function and prevent skin irritation," but "the effect is not equal for all AHAs, being more marked for the molecules characterized by antioxidant properties" (gluconolactone and tartaric acid outperformed glycolic and lactic acid on this measure).[^berardesca]

**Hydration and TEWL at 10% and 30%.** A split-face trial in 16 women applied 10% gluconolactone solution to one side of the face and 30% to the other across three sessions, measuring hydration by corneometry at the forehead, periocular area, and cheek before treatment and one week after the last session.[^corneometric] Both concentrations produced "a significant increase in facial skin hydration," with "no significant differences... observed between the application of 10% and 30% solution," leading the authors to conclude gluconolactone "is a moisturizing substance which works well in dry skin care."[^corneometric] A related split-face trial by the same group used gluconolactone as a chemical peel series at the same two concentrations in 16 women, measuring sebum, pH, and TEWL at the forehead, periocular area, cheek, and nose wing.[^tewl] TEWL was "significantly lower" around the eyes, on the left forehead, and on the right cheek after the treatment series, pH fell at all measured sites, and outcomes were again comparable between the 10% and 30% strengths.[^tewl]

**UV-related protection (in vitro/ex vivo).** Using a transgenic model of cutaneous photoaging to measure UV-induced elastin promoter activation, plus sunburn-cell counts in human skin samples after UV exposure, researchers found gluconolactone "provided up to 50% protection against UV radiation, as measured in our in vitro system, and did not significantly increase sunburn cells in human skin."[^bernstein] This is a lab measure of a photoaging-relevant molecular pathway and an irritation proxy, not a clinical trial of sunburn prevention or a measured SPF value.[^bernstein]

## Uses

Modestly supported as a milder-feeling exfoliant than glycolic acid: in one small controlled trial, gluconolactone outperformed glycolic acid on measured skin-barrier recovery after an irritation challenge.[^berardesca] Modestly supported as a hydrating topical at 10% to 30%, with corneometry and TEWL improvements and no added benefit from the higher strength.[^corneometric][^tewl] Its combined exfoliation-plus-hydration profile overlaps with the general evidence for barrier-supportive moisturizing; see [[moisturizing]].[^corneometric][^tewl]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"As effective as AHAs but with zero irritation."** The controlled evidence is narrower: one small trial found gluconolactone left skin more resistant to a detergent challenge than glycolic acid at matched concentration, not that it causes no irritation at all.[^berardesca]
- **"Provides real sun protection."** The UV-related finding is an in vitro/ex vivo signal in a lab photoaging model, not a measured SPF value or a clinical sunburn-prevention trial; it does not substitute for [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^bernstein]
