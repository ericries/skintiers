Melasyl is L'Oreal's trade name for 2-mercaptonicotinoyl glycine (2-MNG), a synthetic small molecule discovered and developed inside the company's own research division; L'Oreal Research and Innovation scientists first described the compound and its mechanism in a 2024 paper in Pigment Cell Melanoma Research.[^sextius] It is marketed for facial hyperpigmentation, including in La Roche-Posay's Mela B3 range and [[la-roche-posay-mela-b3-serum]].

A substantial body of peer-reviewed literature on the molecule now exists. But every human study located for this page traces back to the manufacturer: the authors are La Roche-Posay or L'Oreal employees, the contract research organization L'Oreal hired to run the trial, or outside dermatologists who disclose honoraria or consulting fees from L'Oreal.[^sextius][^dedormael][^passeron][^demessant][^dumbuya][^muller] No trial independent of L'Oreal, financially or otherwise, was located.

> L'Oreal has published a real mechanism paper and several human trials on Melasyl, including a 109-woman randomized trial reporting that its finished Mela B3 serum performed statistically non-inferior to hydroquinone 4% for melasma, with fewer early skin reactions.[^passeron] That trial tested Melasyl combined with 10% niacinamide, not Melasyl alone, and every located study, without exception, was authored, funded, or run by the company that makes and sells Melasyl, or by its paid consultants. No independently conducted trial has confirmed any of these findings.

## The Rubric

Grading below covers Melasyl's one marketed use, topical correction of hyperpigmentation. All available evidence originates with the manufacturer, so this grade describes a preliminary, unreplicated record rather than a confirmed effect.

**Facial hyperpigmentation (melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, general dyschromia), topical**
- **Effect size: preliminary, and not cleanly isolated from other actives.** The only randomized trial in an existing-melasma population tested Melasyl as part of the finished Mela B3 serum, combined with 10% niacinamide, which itself has independent, if modest, hyperpigmentation evidence (see [[niacinamide]]); the combination was statistically non-inferior to hydroquinone 4% and produced fewer local skin reactions early in treatment, but the result cannot be attributed to Melasyl by itself.[^passeron] The clearest isolated-compound human data measure a different endpoint, prevention of new UV-induced darkening rather than lightening of existing spots: a 33-subject trial found 0.5% and 1% 2-MNG alone reduced UV-induced darkening and new melanin production more than a vehicle control and more than a reference brightening ingredient.[^dedormael] Two further open-label studies, neither with a vehicle or comparator arm, reported improvement from baseline in post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and in general dyschromia in skin of color; the skin-of-color study's own authors state it "did not specifically address the effectiveness of individual ingredients, nor include a comparison to a gold standard treatment."[^demessant][^dumbuya]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary, despite the size of the published record.** Every located human study was authored by La Roche-Posay or L'Oreal employees, run through the CRO L'Oreal contracted, or included outside dermatologists with disclosed L'Oreal honoraria or consulting ties.[^sextius][^dedormael][^passeron][^demessant][^dumbuya][^muller] Two of the five human studies had no vehicle or active comparator, and the one trial with a hydroquinone comparator tested Melasyl only inside a niacinamide combination. No trial independent of the manufacturer was located.

## What We Actually Know

2-MNG works through a mechanism its discoverers describe as distinct from most existing pigment correctors, demonstrated so far in reconstructed skin and skin-explant models rather than in a direct measurement in living human skin.[^sextius] Human trials in melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and general dyschromia in skin of color report real percentage improvements, and one 109-woman randomized trial found the finished Mela B3 serum statistically non-inferior to hydroquinone 4% with fewer early skin reactions.[^passeron][^demessant][^dumbuya] Every one of those trials, however, was designed, run, funded, or authored by La Roche-Posay, L'Oreal, or paid L'Oreal consultants, and only one isolates the molecule against a vehicle control in a population with existing hyperpigmentation-adjacent skin (the UV-darkening prevention study); the flagship hydroquinone comparison tested Melasyl combined with niacinamide, not alone.[^dedormael][^passeron] An academic review of melasma treatments published in 2026, by authors with no apparent L'Oreal affiliation, lists Melasyl among "non-hydroquinone brighteners," a passing mention that confirms the compound is recognized in the dermatology literature without adding independent efficacy data of its own.[^liao]

## How It Works

Most established topical pigment correctors, including [[hydroquinone]], [[azelaic-acid]], and kojic acid, work chiefly by inhibiting tyrosinase, the enzyme that starts melanin synthesis. Sextius and colleagues, all affiliated with L'Oreal Research and Innovation (with two co-authors at an academic melanin-chemistry institute in Japan), report that 2-MNG works differently: "unlike most melanogenesis inhibitors on the market, this molecule is not a tyrosinase inhibitor. 2-MNG binds to certain melanin precursors, preventing their integration into growing melanin and leading to inhibition of eumelanin and pheomelanin synthesis, without compromising the integrity of melanocytes."[^sextius] The paper states this effectiveness "was confirmed by topical application on pigmented reconstructed epidermis and human skin explants," a laboratory and tissue-culture demonstration rather than a measurement in living human skin.[^sextius]

## The Evidence

**Mechanism (in vitro and ex vivo).** Sextius and colleagues used high-throughput screening and computational methods to design 2-MNG, then tested it on reconstructed pigmented epidermis and human skin explants, reporting it inhibits both eumelanin and pheomelanin synthesis through a precursor-binding mechanism rather than tyrosinase inhibition, "without compromising the integrity of melanocytes."[^sextius] All authors are affiliated with L'Oreal Research and Innovation or, for two co-authors, an academic institute studying melanin chemistry.

**UV-induced darkening prevention (randomized, human, isolated compound).** de Dormael and colleagues, all L'Oreal Research and Innovation employees, ran a randomized study in 33 subjects with melanin-rich skin, comparing 0.5% and 1% 2-MNG alone, a combination of 0.5% 2-MNG with lipohydroxy acid and the UV filter Mexoryl-SX, a vehicle control, and 4-n-butylresorcinol as a reference ingredient. 2-MNG alone reduced immediate darkening and new melanin production more than vehicle, with the 1% concentration performing better than 0.5%, and "the association of 2-MNG with LHA plus MSX showed the highest efficacy on melanin-rich skin with pigmentation induced by UV exposure."[^dedormael] This is the one located trial that isolates 2-MNG's own effect against a vehicle control, but its endpoint is prevention of new UV-triggered darkening, not treatment of existing melasma or dark spots.

**Melasma versus hydroquinone 4% (randomized, human, combination product).** Passeron and colleagues (a mix of La Roche-Posay/L'Oreal employees in France and Brazil, an academic dermatologist in Nice who discloses honoraria from L'Oreal among other companies, and a New York academic dermatologist who discloses honoraria from numerous companies) ran a randomized, investigator-blind, non-inferiority trial in 109 women with facial melasma, comparing the finished Mela B3 serum (2-MNG plus 10% niacinamide, applied twice daily) against hydroquinone 4% (once daily), both with SPF 50+ sunscreen, over 12 weeks. "At D84, the estimated difference in mMASI score between MB3 and hydroquinone 4% was 0.46," meeting the trial's non-inferiority threshold, and both groups improved significantly from baseline by day 28. The Mela B3 arm "showed better tolerability versus hydroquinone 4% at D28 with fewer local skin reactions (6.0% versus 21.4%, respectively; p = 0.0286)."[^passeron] The paper frames hydroquinone 4% itself as "the gold standard of care for melasma." Because the tested product combines Melasyl with niacinamide, which has its own independent, if modest, hyperpigmentation evidence (see [[niacinamide]]), this result does not isolate Melasyl's individual contribution.
> Bottom line: in a company-run trial, a niacinamide-plus-Melasyl serum matched hydroquinone 4% on a standard melasma severity score and caused fewer early skin reactions. It does not show what Melasyl alone contributes.

**Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (open-label, uncontrolled).** Demessant-Flavigny and colleagues, all but two of them L'Oreal employees (the remaining two work for the contract research organization that conducted the study), applied a Melasyl-containing serum twice daily for three months to 32 subjects in Mauritius with phototype IV-VI skin and acne-related hyperpigmentation, with no sunscreen and no vehicle or comparator arm. They reported "a significant decrease of 15.8% (p < 0.05) in PAHPI score" by day 84, a "25.1% decrease" in lesion darkness, and "a 60% reduction in spot color intensity" by instrumental measurement (p < 0.05).[^demessant] With no control arm, the finding cannot rule out improvement from sun avoidance, study participation, or measurement regression alone.

**Facial dyschromia in skin of color (open-label, uncontrolled).** Dumbuya and colleagues (two L'Oreal USA employees and four outside dermatologists who disclose being researchers or consultants for L'Oreal) ran a 12-week open-label study of a 2-MNG serum-and-sunscreen regimen in 60 women with phototype IV-VI skin and hyperpigmentation, reporting "significant improvement in skin brightness and radiance" at two weeks and "significant reduction in dyschromia and hyperpigmentation" at four weeks. The paper states plainly that the study "did not specifically address the effectiveness of individual ingredients, nor include a comparison to a gold standard treatment."[^dumbuya]

**Comparative ranking against other pigment actives (company-run, network meta-analysis).** Muller and colleagues, funded by "L'Oreal Research & Innovation Department" and consisting mostly of L'Oreal employees plus two outside academics (an anthropologist and the same Nice-based dermatologist who co-authored the hydroquinone trial above), ran 14 small studies (15 to 30 women of phototype III each, in Shanghai and Paris) testing 2-MNG 0.5% against 13 reference anti-pigmenting ingredients, including hydroquinone 4%, niacinamide 4%, and kojic acid 1%, for preventing UV-daylight-induced pigmentation, then pooled the results in a Bayesian network meta-analysis. "2-MNG 0.5% dominated the ranking at all time points," and the paper concludes "2-MNG 0.5% ranked first, with immediate and lasting effect compared to 13 other references."[^muller] The comparison design, protocol, and funding all belong to the company whose molecule ranked first, and it measures the same UV-darkening-prevention endpoint as the de Dormael trial above, not treatment of existing hyperpigmentation.

## Compared with Other Pigment Actives

Hydroquinone is the long-standing topical depigmenting standard, reliably lightening melasma more than placebo, though a 59-trial network meta-analysis of 14 treatments ranked it comparatively low on efficacy among them; see [[hydroquinone]]. Azelaic acid, FDA-approved for rosacea and acne, also has independent trial evidence, including a meta-analysis finding it outperforms hydroquinone 2% for melasma; see [[azelaic-acid]]. Topical L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is a well-characterized antioxidant with modest evidence for photoprotection and collagen support; one small independent trial found it less effective by patient rating than hydroquinone 4% for melasma but far better tolerated; see [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]]. Oral tranexamic acid has modest-to-notable independent trial evidence for melasma from a placebo-controlled RCT and several meta-analyses, though its effect largely relapses after the drug is stopped; see [[tranexamic-acid]]. Melasyl has no trial of this kind conducted independently of its manufacturer.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"A major, long-researched proprietary breakthrough."** L'Oreal's own corporate filings describe extensive internal research and testing behind Melasyl; those are the company's own claims about its own product, documented on [[la-roche-posay]] rather than repeated here. The peer-reviewed record confirms L'Oreal has run a real research program on the molecule, including a genuine mechanism paper, but every efficacy trial located for this page was also authored, funded, or run by the same company.[^sextius][^dedormael][^passeron][^demessant][^dumbuya][^muller]
- **"Matches the efficacy of the dermatological gold standard in hyperpigmentation."** The one trial that compares a Melasyl-containing product to hydroquinone directly found the finished, niacinamide-containing Mela B3 serum statistically non-inferior to hydroquinone 4%, in a company-run trial that does not isolate Melasyl's own contribution.[^passeron] The word "Melasyl" carries a trademark symbol even inside the peer-reviewed papers describing it, for example Dumbuya and colleagues introducing "2-mercaptonicotinoyl glycine (2-MNG, MelasylTM)," a sign of how closely the clinical publishing record and the brand are tied together.[^dumbuya]
- **"Works through a broad, novel mechanism unlike other pigment correctors."** Supported, at the mechanism-paper level, by L'Oreal's own in vitro and ex vivo data showing 2-MNG binds melanin precursors rather than inhibiting tyrosinase.[^sextius] That is laboratory and tissue-model evidence; no fetched study measures this mechanism directly in living human skin.
