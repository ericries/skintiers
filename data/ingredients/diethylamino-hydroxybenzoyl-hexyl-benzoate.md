Diethylamino Hydroxybenzoyl Hexyl Benzoate (INCI), sold by BASF under the trade name Uvinul A Plus and commonly abbreviated DHHB, is a synthetic organic ultraviolet filter dedicated to the long-wave UVA-I band, with an absorption peak at 354 nm.[^basftds][^kawakami] It is oil-soluble and is positioned in the peer-reviewed literature as an alternative to [[avobenzone]], the older UVA filter it is most often compared with: one laboratory study describes DHHB's "high absorption in the UVA range (peak at 354 nm) and good photostability" as making it "an interesting alternative to AVO."[^kawakami] That same independent study found DHHB itself photostable across the formulations it tested, but found no photostabilizing effect when DHHB was combined with avobenzone, contrary to how the pairing is sometimes marketed.[^kawakami] DHHB is an authorised UV filter in the European Union, entry 28 of Annex VI to the Cosmetics Regulation, at a maximum concentration of 10%; the manufacturer's own regulatory listing shows the same 10% ceiling registered across Switzerland, India, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, China, Australia, and Mexico, with no entry for the United States or Canada.[^eu2008][^basftds] It is not among the ingredients addressed by the US FDA's sunscreen monograph process and so is absent from sunscreens sold in the US.[^fda2019][^fda2026] Regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use is one of the few skincare practices with randomized-trial support for slowing photoaging and reducing skin-cancer burden; that benefit belongs to the practice of regular use, documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], not to DHHB in isolation.

> DHHB is a photostable, oil-soluble organic UVA-I filter with a 354 nm absorption peak, authorised across the EU and other major markets at up to 10% and described by BASF as an alternative to avobenzone, though an independent study found it does not photostabilize avobenzone when the two are combined in the same formula; it sits outside the US OTC sunscreen monograph and is not found in sunscreens sold in the United States.[^basftds][^kawakami][^eu2008][^fda2019][^fda2026]

## The Rubric

The grade below is for DHHB's job in a sunscreen, ultraviolet-A photoprotection, framed relative to [[avobenzone]], the filter it is most often measured against. The 2025 European review of a manufacturing impurity is a separate safety question, graded separately and not folded into the photoprotection grade.

**Ultraviolet-A photoprotection**
- **Effect size: notable, dedicated UVA-I coverage.** DHHB's absorption peaks at 354 nm, in the long-wave UVA-I band it is designed to target. BASF's own quality-control specification confirms the identity peak sits between 352 and 356 nm in ethanol, and the EU regulator's own toxicological dossier independently records an absorption maximum near 356 nm.[^basftds][^sccp2008] BASF's technical literature describes Uvinul A Plus as "the only photostable organic UVA-I absorber that covers the long wavelengths of the UVA spectrum," a manufacturer's claim rather than an independent comparative finding.[^basftds] Independently, a laboratory study of DHHB-containing sunscreen formulations, tested by HPLC and spectrophotometry, reported that "all formulations provided ultra-protection against UVA radiation."[^kawakami]
- **Evidence quality: solid for DHHB's own spectral and photostability characterization; a specific pairing claim does not hold up.** The same independent study found DHHB "was considered to be photostable for all formulations studied," corroborating the manufacturer's photostability claim for the molecule itself.[^kawakami] But when the study tested whether DHHB stabilizes avobenzone, a formulation pairing sometimes marketed as complementary, HPLC results showed "DHHB did not present a photostabilizing effect on AVO," and fluorescence spectroscopy indicated the two interact by a static quenching mechanism rather than one that protects avobenzone's excited state from breakdown.[^kawakami] What is not demonstrated for DHHB on its own is a clinical-endpoint benefit; the randomized evidence that broad-spectrum sunscreen slows photoaging and lowers skin-cancer burden is for the practice of regular use, covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^kawakami]

**Regulatory status and the 2025 impurity review (a separate axis, not an efficacy grade)**
- DHHB is an authorised UV filter in the European Union, entry 28 of Annex VI to the Cosmetics Regulation. The Scientific Committee on Consumer Products concluded in 2008 that "the use of this substance at a maximum concentration of 10% in cosmetic products, including sunscreen products, does not pose a risk to the consumer," a conclusion that extended its permitted use beyond sunscreens alone.[^eu2008] BASF's own regulatory listing registers the same 10% ceiling across the EU, Switzerland, India, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, China, Australia, ASEAN, South America, and Mexico, with no entry for the United States or Canada.[^basftds] DHHB is not among the sixteen filters addressed by the FDA's 2019 proposed sunscreen rule, nor was it added by the FDA's June 2026 final order, which added only bemotrizinol to the OTC monograph; it therefore remains outside the US framework.[^fda2019][^fda2026] In 2025, after German authorities detected a phthalate metabolite in children's urine potentially traceable to a manufacturing impurity in DHHB (di-n-hexyl phthalate, DnHexP), the EU's Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety re-evaluated the ingredient. In its final opinion of 26 June 2025, the SCCS "calculated a maximum safe level of 0.026% (260 ppm) for this impurity in the UV filter DHHB," applying "when DHHB is used up to 10% in cosmetic products and only if DnHexP is an unavoidable impurity," and separately concluded that "a trace level of 1 ppm should be the target for the maximal level of DnHexP as an impurity in DHHB," since an alternative manufacturing process can reach that level.[^sccs2025] The SCCS did not revisit DHHB's own safety as a filter, stating that "no new information is available on DHHB, which has been already approved" for use up to 10%.[^sccs2025]

## What We Actually Know

DHHB, sold by BASF as Uvinul A Plus, is a well-characterized organic UVA-I filter with an absorption peak at 354 nm, confirmed both in BASF's own quality-control specification and in an independent laboratory study.[^basftds][^kawakami] That study found DHHB itself photostable in the formulations tested but found it did not photostabilize avobenzone when the two were combined, undercutting a pairing sometimes framed as complementary.[^kawakami] DHHB is authorised in the European Union at a maximum concentration of 10% and is registered at the same ceiling across most major cosmetics markets outside North America, per the manufacturer's own regulatory listing.[^eu2008][^basftds] It is not part of the US OTC sunscreen monograph and is absent from sunscreens sold in the United States.[^fda2019][^fda2026] A 2025 European safety review, prompted by a phthalate manufacturing impurity rather than any new concern about DHHB itself, set an impurity limit without reopening the question of the filter's own safety.[^sccs2025] The health value of using it is the value of regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use, documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], not a property of DHHB alone.

## How It Works

DHHB is an organic (chemical) filter: it absorbs a UV photon and dissipates the energy rather than reflecting or scattering light the way mineral filters do. Its structure "was designed according to classical benzophenone chemistry," and its absorption is concentrated around a peak of 354 nm, in the long-wave UVA-I region.[^kawakami] BASF's quality-control identity test places the peak between 352 and 356 nm in ethanol, with a specific extinction (E1/1) of 910 to 940 at 354 nm.[^basftds] The molecule (molecular formula C24H31NO4, molecular weight about 397 g/mol) is supplied as a granular solid that BASF describes as well soluble in a range of cosmetic oils and notably soluble in ethanol, properties that support its use as an oil-phase UV filter in sunscreen and other leave-on formulations.[^basftds]

## The Evidence

Primary evidence, by question.

**Spectral coverage.** BASF's quality-control specification for Uvinul A Plus Granular sets the identity UV spectrum in ethanol at "352 - 356 nm" with a specific extinction (E1/1) at "354 nm" of "910 - 940."[^basftds] An independent laboratory study of DHHB-containing sunscreen formulations, evaluated by HPLC and spectrophotometry, found "all formulations provided ultra-protection against UVA radiation" and described DHHB's "high absorption in the UVA range (peak at 354 nm)."[^kawakami]

**Photostability, on its own and paired with avobenzone.** The same study, which tested DHHB alone and combined with avobenzone in different sunscreen formulations, concluded that "DHHB was considered to be photostable for all formulations studied," but that "although DHHB was considered to be photostable for all formulations studied (F2 and F3) it did not increase the photostability of avobenzone (F3)."[^kawakami] Fluorescence spectroscopy showed avobenzone and DHHB "interact by a static quenching mechanism" and that DHHB "did not affect the AVO excited state lifetime," which the authors offer as the explanation for why DHHB "did not work as a photostabilizer on AVO singlet excited state."[^kawakami] The study also tested phototoxicity in 3T3 fibroblast cultures and found the tested DHHB-containing formulations "did not show a phototoxic potential."[^kawakami]

**Regulatory status.** Commission Directive 2008/123/EC records that "the SCCP concluded in its opinion of 15 April 2008 that the use of this substance at a maximum concentration of 10% in cosmetic products, including sunscreen products, does not pose a risk to the consumer," and amended entry 28 of Annex VII (now Annex VI of Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009) to extend that authorised use.[^eu2008] BASF's own approval-status table for Uvinul A Plus lists a 10% ceiling in the EU, Switzerland, India, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, China, Australia, ASEAN, South America, and Mexico, and shows no authorised concentration for the United States or Canada.[^basftds] The FDA's 2019 proposed sunscreen rule addresses sixteen active ingredients (zinc oxide and titanium dioxide proposed Category I; avobenzone and eleven other organic filters proposed Category III; aminobenzoic acid and trolamine salicylate Category II), and DHHB is not among them.[^fda2019] The FDA's June 2026 final administrative order added bemotrizinol, at up to 6%, as a GRASE sunscreen active, and did not add DHHB.[^fda2026]

**The 2025 DnHexP impurity review.** The SCCS's final scientific advice of 26 June 2025 states the trigger for the review was that German authorities "informed the Commission services on the detection of the degradation product of a plasticizer in urine samples of children," a finding that could reflect exposure from a phthalate impurity, di-n-hexyl phthalate (DnHexP), in DHHB production.[^sccs2025] The SCCS "calculated a maximum safe level of 0.026% (260 ppm) for this impurity in the UV filter DHHB," applicable "when DHHB is used up to 10% in cosmetic products and only if DnHexP is an unavoidable impurity," and noted that DnHexP "was below the level of detection in several DHHB containing sunscreen products and other DHHB sources, provided that a specific manufacturing process is applied."[^sccs2025] Because an alternative manufacturing process can reach even lower levels, the committee set a preferred target of "a trace level of 1 ppm."[^sccs2025] The advice explicitly did not reassess DHHB's core safety, stating the review "is focused on DnHexP... because no new information is available on DHHB, which has been already approved as an ingredient which can constitute up to 10% of a cosmetic product."[^sccs2025]

## Uses

Well-supported: as a dedicated UVA-I filter in sunscreen and other leave-on cosmetic formulations sold across the EU and other markets outside North America, at up to 10%, where DHHB's peak absorption at 354 nm targets the long-wave UVA band.[^basftds][^kawakami] That coverage contributes to the protection delivered by regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use, documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], not a benefit of DHHB alone.[^kawakami] Its practical limits are two: it is not part of the US OTC sunscreen monograph, so it is absent from US sunscreens, and despite being marketed alongside avobenzone, an independent study found it does not photostabilize avobenzone in combination.[^fda2019][^fda2026][^kawakami]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"The only photostable organic UVA-I absorber."** This is BASF's own description in its technical literature.[^basftds] An independent study did find DHHB itself photostable in the formulations tested, corroborating that it resists photodegradation, but that study did not benchmark DHHB against other organic UVA filters, so the "only" claim rests on the manufacturer's own literature rather than a comparative study.[^basftds][^kawakami]
- **"Pairs with avobenzone for stable, broad UVA protection."** The one independent study to test this pairing directly found the opposite of a stabilizing effect: "DHHB did not present a photostabilizing effect on AVO," with fluorescence spectroscopy showing the two filters interact by static quenching rather than a mechanism that protects avobenzone from photodegradation.[^kawakami]
- **"A safer, more modern alternative."** The 2025 SCCS review that put DHHB back under scrutiny was triggered by a manufacturing impurity, di-n-hexyl phthalate, not by any new finding about DHHB itself; the committee set an impurity limit and explicitly left its 2008 safety conclusion on DHHB unchanged.[^sccs2025]
