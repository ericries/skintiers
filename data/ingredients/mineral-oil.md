Mineral oil (INCI: Paraffinum Liquidum, also called liquid paraffin; CAS 8042-47-5) is a highly refined, colorless, odorless mixture of saturated hydrocarbons derived from petroleum, used across moisturizers, cleansing balms, and ointment bases as a pure occlusive emollient, typically making up anywhere from a few percent to the bulk of a formula's oil phase.[^pubchem] Because it is a complex hydrocarbon mixture rather than a single molecule, it has no PubChem CID or molecular formula of its own; cosmetic and pharmaceutical grades are held to strict purity standards (USP/NF, and food-grade equivalents) that remove the polycyclic aromatic contaminants found in crude, industrial-grade mineral oil.[^pubchem] Its evidence base is strongest, and least controversial, for what it actually does: sit on the skin surface and slow water loss, an effect confirmed directly on human skin, while its reputation for clogging pores rests mainly on decades-old animal-model data whose relevance to real human acne has been directly disputed in the dermatology literature.[^choe][^zimmermann][^frank]

> In an in vivo confocal Raman microspectroscopy study on human skin, topically applied mineral-derived paraffin oil produced "an occlusion effect similar to petrolatum" on stratum corneum water binding, even though, unlike petrolatum, it did not cause measurable stratum corneum swelling.[^choe]

## The Rubric

**Occlusion and moisture retention (topical, as a moisturizer/barrier emollient)**
- **Effect size: real and mechanistically confirmed, on par with petrolatum by one direct in vivo comparison.** Confocal Raman microspectroscopy on human skin found that topically applied paraffin oil (mineral oil) shifted water in the upper stratum corneum toward a more loosely bound state and increased free-water content, the same water-binding signature produced by petrolatum, the reference occlusive; the difference was that petrolatum alone caused measurable stratum corneum swelling, while the mineral-derived oil did not.[^choe] In a separate randomized, double-blind, controlled trial, twice-daily mineral oil applied to legs with mild-to-moderate xerosis produced a significant improvement in skin hydration (measured by Corneometer) over 2 weeks, comparable to virgin coconut oil, with no significant change in transepidermal water loss (TEWL) or skin pH and no adverse reactions.[^agero]
- **Evidence quality: solid for the core mechanism, thinner for head-to-head potency claims.** The occlusion mechanism rests on one well-designed instrumental human study directly comparing mineral-derived and plant-derived oils to petrolatum, and one randomized clinical trial establishing efficacy and safety as a moisturizer against an active comparator (not a vehicle/placebo).[^choe][^agero] Neither study quantifies a percentage TEWL reduction for mineral oil specifically, and there is no large, independent, vehicle-controlled trial isolating mineral oil against an inert control, so its magnitude relative to "doing nothing" is inferred rather than directly measured.

**Comedogenicity / pore-clogging (topical, on acne-prone skin)**
- **Effect size: contested, and reputation exceeds the direct human evidence.** The main data implicating mineral oil come from the rabbit-ear comedogenicity assay, where a "strongly comedogenic mineral oil" grade produced histologic follicular and sebaceous-duct changes.[^zimmermann] But a contemporaneous review of that same assay concluded there is an "absence of correlation with experience in the human," and cautioned against treating rabbit-ear results as prophetic of real-world acnegenicity.[^frank] Consistent with that caution, a widely cited classic rabbit-ear ingredient screen that flagged isopropyl myristate and its analogs, certain lanolin derivatives, and D&C Red dyes as comedogenic offenders did not name mineral oil among them.[^fulton]
- **Evidence quality: weak and indirect in both directions.** No randomized controlled trial has tested cosmetic-grade mineral oil against a vehicle for acne outcomes in humans; the "comedogenic" case rests on an animal model of disputed human relevance using an unspecified mineral oil grade, and the "non-comedogenic" case rests on its absence from ingredient screens and on a 2005 clinical commentary titled specifically "Is mineral oil comedogenic?" rather than on a dedicated human trial.[^zimmermann][^frank][^fulton][^dinardo] Grade quality (crude versus highly refined, cosmetic/USP-grade) plausibly matters and is rarely specified in the older literature, which is a real limitation, not a marketing footnote.

## How It Works

Mineral oil is a mixture of C15 to C40 saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) refined from petroleum distillates; unlike plant oils, it contains no fatty acids, sterols, or unsaturated bonds, which makes it chemically inert on skin and resistant to oxidative rancidity.[^pubchem] As a pure occlusive, its mechanism is physical rather than biochemical: it forms a hydrophobic film on the skin surface that slows evaporative water loss. In vivo confocal Raman spectroscopy shows this shows up at the molecular level as an increase in the water content of the mid-depth stratum corneum and a shift of surface water from tightly to loosely hydrogen-bonded states, the same signature produced by petrolatum, the reference standard occlusive.[^choe] Separately, controlled human studies of occlusion and barrier-perturbed skin (using petrolatum, mineral oil's semisolid paraffin relative) have found that occluding damaged skin does not delay the skin's own barrier-repair process, undercutting the older claim, based on hairless-mouse data, that occlusives slow healing.[^welzel] Because mineral oil supplies no active biochemical signal, it does not participate in ceramide or lipid-bilayer synthesis the way "skin-identical lipid" formulations are sometimes marketed to; in one controlled comparison, a skin-identical-lipid cream base was no better than plain petrolatum at normalizing barrier-perturbed human skin, suggesting the physical occlusive effect, not a specific lipid composition, does most of the work.[^loden]

## The Evidence

**Mineral oil versus petrolatum, occlusion mechanism, in vivo (topical).** A confocal Raman microspectroscopy study applied jojoba oil, almond oil, mineral-derived paraffin oil, and petrolatum to human skin and measured depth-resolved water-binding states, keratin conformation, and lipid organization. Significant stratum corneum swelling occurred only with petrolatum, but water concentration increased in the mid-depth stratum corneum for all four oils, and the amount of free and weakly bound water rose (while strongly bound water fell) in the uppermost stratum corneum for all of them; the authors concluded that "almond, jojoba and paraffin oils, which are not occlusive from the conventional viewpoint, have an occlusion effect similar to petrolatum on the SC."[^choe] *A rigorous instrumental study found that mineral-derived paraffin oil produces the same core occlusive water-binding effect as petrolatum, even though it does not visibly swell the skin the way petrolatum does.*

**Mineral oil versus virgin coconut oil, randomized double-blind trial, xerosis (topical).** Thirty-four patients with mild-to-moderate xerosis and negative patch tests were randomized to apply either virgin coconut oil or mineral oil to the legs twice daily for 2 weeks, with hydration (Corneometer), skin lipids (Sebumeter), TEWL (Tewameter), and pH tracked weekly. Both oils produced "significant improvement in skin hydration... and increase in skin surface lipid levels," with "no significant difference in TEWL and skin pH" between arms and no adverse reactions in either group; investigator- and patient-rated dryness/scaling/roughness trended better with coconut oil but the difference was not statistically significant. The authors concluded "Coconut oil is as effective and safe as mineral oil when used as a moisturizer."[^agero] *A real randomized trial, beyond the in vitro models, confirms mineral oil meaningfully hydrates dry skin and is safe on TEWL and pH, though it used an active comparator rather than a vehicle, so it cannot show mineral oil beats doing nothing.*

**Rabbit-ear comedogenicity assay, and its disputed human relevance (animal model).** A German rabbit-ear histology study applying a "strongly comedogenic mineral oil" reported follicular and sebaceous-duct changes that the author considered to correlate with human acne vulgaris.[^zimmermann] But a separate, contemporaneous review of the same assay methodology concluded there was an "absence of correlation with experience in the human, and, consequently, the serious limitations of the conclusions that can reasonably be drawn, especially for the clinical dermatologist."[^frank] A widely cited classic rabbit-ear ingredient screen of cosmetic actives and finished products flagged isopropyl myristate and its analogs, certain acetylated/ethoxylated lanolins, and D&C Red dyes as comedogenic offenders, but did not list mineral oil among them.[^fulton] *The strongest "mineral oil clogs pores" evidence comes from an animal assay whose own contemporaries questioned its predictive value for human skin, and a separate classic screen using the same model did not flag mineral oil as an offender.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Non-comedogenic, won't clog pores."** Defensible for highly refined, cosmetic/USP-grade mineral oil: it is absent from the classic rabbit-ear offender list, and the assay results implicating "mineral oil" broadly used an unspecified, explicitly "strongly comedogenic" grade rather than refined cosmetic material, with the model's human relevance itself disputed.[^fulton][^zimmermann][^frank] But no dedicated human RCT has tested cosmetic-grade mineral oil against vehicle for acne outcomes, so "non-comedogenic" is a reasonable inference from indirect evidence, not a settled clinical fact.
- **"Deeply hydrating."** Misleading in mechanism, fair in outcome. Mineral oil is not a humectant and adds no water to skin; it works by trapping existing water and reducing evaporative loss, and one randomized trial confirms this translates into real, measured hydration gains on dry skin comparable to virgin coconut oil.[^choe][^agero] The correct claim is "moisture-retaining," not "moisture-adding."
