Cosmetic preservatives are the antimicrobial ingredients added to water-containing products to stop bacteria, mold, and yeast from growing in the bottle; the main classes on regulatory approved lists are parabens, phenoxyethanol, formaldehyde-releasers, isothiazolinones, and organic acids such as benzoic and sorbic acid.[^halla][^fda]

> The preservative classes differ sharply in where the real, documented risk sits. The isothiazolinone MI is a genuine and common contact allergen, named the American Contact Dermatitis Society Contact Allergen of the Year for 2013.[^castanedo] Formaldehyde-releasers carry a real but narrower risk: allergic contact dermatitis, concentrated in people already sensitized to formaldehyde.[^degroot2] Parabens, the most feared class in marketing, are by the patch-test record one of the least allergenic preservatives available, and the FDA states it does not have information showing that parabens as used in cosmetics affect human health.[^fransway-allergy][^fda] Phenoxyethanol is described in a dermatology safety review as a rare sensitizer and one of the most well-tolerated preservatives in use.[^dreno]

## Why They Are There

A 2018 peer-reviewed review of cosmetic preservation states the baseline fact: "Cosmetics, like any product containing water and organic/inorganic compounds, require preservation against microbial contamination to guarantee consumer's safety and to increase their shelf-life."[^halla] The organisms most frequently found in contaminated cosmetics include Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Burkholderia cepacia, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans, and the review notes that their presence in a product "can increase the risk of microbial infection."[^halla] Contamination can happen during manufacture (primary contamination) and again during consumer use (secondary contamination), so the preservative system has to protect the product both in its closed packaging and in the open container throughout its use.[^halla] The review also records the outcome under current practice: "In recent years, the safety record for personal care products has been excellent, resulting in a scarce occurrence of infections due to contaminated products."[^halla] The FDA describes the purpose in one sentence: preservatives "prevent the growth of harmful bacteria and mold, in order to protect both the products and consumers."[^fda]

Dosing is a balance the formulator has to strike. The review notes that high preservative concentrations preserve better but raise toxicity to the user, "whereas at low concentrations microbial resistance can develop."[^halla]

## The Classes

- **Parabens** (methyl-, ethyl-, propyl-, butylparaben) are esters of p-hydroxybenzoic acid and, per the FDA, the parabens "used most commonly in cosmetics"; they are often combined with other preservatives to cover a broader range of microorganisms.[^fda][^petric]
- **Phenoxyethanol** has a large spectrum of activity against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria and yeasts, with "only a weak inhibitory effect on resident skin flora," and has been widely used in cosmetics for decades.[^dreno]
- **Formaldehyde-releasers** (DMDM hydantoin, imidazolidinyl urea, diazolidinyl urea, quaternium-15, 2-bromo-2-nitropropane-1,3-diol) work by slowly releasing small amounts of formaldehyde into the product. In a US survey cited by the de Groot review, "approximately 20% of cosmetics and personal care products (stay-on products: 17%, rinse-off products 27%) contain a formaldehyde-releaser."[^degroot1]
- **Isothiazolinones** (methylisothiazolinone, MI, and methylchloroisothiazolinone, MCI, used together as the MCI/MI blend and MI alone) are biocide preservatives used in cosmetics and in many non-cosmetic products.[^castanedo][^liden]
- **Organic acids** on the EU approved list include benzoic acid, sorbic acid, propionic acid, dehydroacetic acid, and salicylic acid, which is better known as a well-evidenced exfoliant for acne; see [[salicylic-acid]].[^halla]

## The Rubric

Graded by the documented signal for each concern, from the patch-test and regulatory record.

**Microbial protection (the reason they exist)**
- **Effect: necessary in any water-containing product.** The preservation system is what protects a product from organisms such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus, in its closed packaging and in the open container throughout consumer use; under current preservation practice, infections from contaminated personal care products are scarce.[^halla]

**Isothiazolinone (MI/MCI) contact allergy**
- **Real signal: the strongest of any preservative class.** MI was the American Contact Dermatitis Society Contact Allergen of the Year for 2013, with European groups documenting allergy frequencies of "approximately 1.5%" at that time; standard MCI/MI patch testing "misses approximately 40% of allergy to MI."[^castanedo] A 2024 review in Contact Dermatitis states that frequent cosmetic use of MCI/MI and MI "has been the main cause of widespread sensitization and allergic contact dermatitis to these preservatives."[^liden]

**Formaldehyde-releaser contact allergy**
- **Real signal: genuine, and concentrated in formaldehyde-allergic people.** European sensitization frequencies to the individual releasers run 0.3% to 1.9%, higher in the USA.[^degroot1] The de Groot risk assessment found 40-60% of patch-test reactions to most releasers are caused by the formaldehyde they release, and that releasers can, in the right formula, release more than 200 ppm free formaldehyde, enough to provoke dermatitis in sensitized individuals.[^degroot2] Formaldehyde itself was Contact Allergen of the Year for 2015.[^ponten]

**Paraben contact allergy**
- **Real signal: minimal.** The American Contact Dermatitis Society designated paraben mix its "(non)allergen of the year" for 2019, reporting that sensitivity "has remained low and remarkably stable for many decades" and that "parabens remain one of the least allergenic preservatives available."[^fransway-allergy]

**Paraben systemic and endocrine toxicity**
- **Real signal: not established in humans; the in-vitro effect is weak.** In the study that started the concern, the most potent paraben was "10,000-fold less potent than 17 beta-estradiol" in a yeast estrogen assay, oral dosing in rats was inactive, and even injected butylparaben was about 100,000 times less potent than estradiol.[^routledge] A 2021 toxicology review reports parabens are "non-teratogenic, non-mutagenic, non-carcinogenic" in the available studies and that real evidence of human toxicity has not been established.[^petric] The Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel concluded in 2020 that 20 of 21 parabens are safe as used when total parabens in a formula do not exceed 0.8%.[^cir] The FDA states: "At this time, we do not have information showing that parabens as they are used in cosmetics have an effect on human health."[^fda]

**Phenoxyethanol**
- **Real signal: low.** A dermatology safety review reports that "according to the European Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety, phenoxyethanol is safe for all consumers - including children of all ages - when used as a preservative in cosmetic products at a maximum concentration of 1%," that adverse effects in animal studies appeared only at exposures around 200-fold higher than consumer exposure, and that despite widespread use it "is a rare sensitizer."[^dreno]

## The Evidence

The safety debate around preservatives is really three separate stories, and lumping them together is what fuels most of the fear. Each is treated on its own evidence below: a marketing scare with weak support, a genuine allergen lesson, and a real but narrow allergy issue.

### The Paraben Scare

The scare traces largely to two findings. Routledge and colleagues showed in 1998 that parabens are weakly estrogenic in vitro, with the qualifiers above: the most potent was 10,000-fold weaker than estradiol, and oral dosing did nothing in rats.[^routledge] Darbre and colleagues then reported in 2004 that intact parabens could be measured in 20 human breast tumour samples, at a mean of 20.6 ng per gram of tissue.[^darbre] The study measured no healthy tissue for comparison and its abstract frames the result as a starting point for measuring body burdens, an open question the authors themselves pose, rather than evidence that parabens cause cancer.[^darbre]

The regulatory and toxicology reviews that followed reached consistent conclusions. The Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel's 2020 amended assessment found 20 of 21 parabens safe at current use, with total parabens capped at 0.8% of a formula (benzylparaben alone had insufficient data).[^cir] The FDA continues to state it has no information showing cosmetic paraben use affects human health, and lists among its open questions "What are the hazards and risks of not using parabens?"[^fda] The 2021 Petric review concludes that "for now, methyl-, ethyl- and propylparaben are considered safe for use in cosmetics and pharmaceuticals within the recommended range of doses."[^petric] A 2019 toxicology review in Dermatitis judged that claims tying parabens to endocrine disease or carcinogenesis "are premature," and warned that hasty removal "could result in their substitution with alternative, less proven, and potentially unsafe alternatives."[^fransway-tox]

That substitution already happened. The companion review by the same group records that the unsubstantiated public perception of parabens led to their replacement in many products "with preservatives having far greater allergenic potential."[^fransway-allergy]

### The Isothiazolinone Lesson

MI is the clearest case of a preservative concern that is real and well documented. As its use in cosmetics and toiletries rose, exposure rose with it.[^castanedo] By 2013 it was Contact Allergen of the Year, European groups had documented allergy frequencies of approximately 1.5%, and the standard MCI/MI patch test was missing about 40% of MI allergy.[^castanedo] The 2024 Lidén and White review attributes the widespread sensitization mainly to cosmetic use, notes that contact allergy to MI and to the industrial isothiazolinones BIT and OIT "has increased," and records that BIT, OIT, and DCOIT have never been permitted in cosmetic products in Europe.[^liden]

The allergy is a contact allergy: it shows up as dermatitis where the product touches the skin and is diagnosed by patch testing.[^castanedo] Avoidance by people who develop it depends on the label; cosmetics sold to consumers in stores or online must carry an ingredient list, per the FDA.[^fda]

### Formaldehyde-Releasers: The Real Issue Is Allergy

The word formaldehyde invites fears of systemic harm, but the documented health issue with releaser preservatives in cosmetics is allergic contact dermatitis, and mostly in people already sensitized to formaldehyde. The de Groot two-part review quantifies it: 40-60% of patch-test reactions to most releasers are reactions to the released formaldehyde; releasers can in some formulas exceed 200 ppm free formaldehyde, a level that can provoke dermatitis in allergic individuals; and the label does not reveal how much free formaldehyde a given product contains.[^degroot2] On that basis the reviewers recommend that patients with formaldehyde allergy be advised to avoid leave-on cosmetics preserved with quaternium-15, diazolidinyl urea, DMDM hydantoin, or imidazolidinyl urea, "acknowledging that many would tolerate some products."[^degroot2] For everyone else, the European sensitization frequencies to the releasers are low, 0.3% to 1.9% of patch-tested patients depending on the compound.[^degroot1]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Preservative-free, so it's safer."** A water-containing product requires preservation against microbial contamination; the organisms found in contaminated cosmetics include Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus, and preservation is credited for infections from personal care products being scarce.[^halla] The FDA's own list of open questions about removing parabens includes what the hazards and risks of not using them would be, and whether the alternatives are safer.[^fda]
- **"Paraben-free, so it's gentler."** The patch-test record points the other way: parabens are "one of the least allergenic preservatives available," and the switch away from them moved many products onto preservatives with far greater allergenic potential.[^fransway-allergy] MI, a genuine and common contact allergen, is the contrast case.[^castanedo]
- **"Parabens are linked to breast cancer."** The founding study measured parabens in 20 tumour samples without healthy-tissue controls and did not test causation.[^darbre] The toxicology reviews and the FDA find no established human harm from cosmetic paraben use, and the CIR panel concluded the common parabens are safe as used.[^petric][^cir][^fda]
- **"Contains formaldehyde, a known carcinogen."** What the cosmetic evidence documents for releaser preservatives is contact allergy: reactions concentrated in formaldehyde-sensitized individuals, with low sensitization frequencies observed in European patch-test populations and higher ones in the USA.[^degroot1][^degroot2]
- **"Natural preservative systems are better."** The Petric review's caution about paraben alternatives applies generally: "every substitute would need to be tested rigorously for toxicity and safety."[^petric]
