Stearyl glycyrrhetinate is a fatty ester of glycyrrhetinic acid, the aglycone that licorice root yields, used in cosmetics as a soothing agent. The licorice family's broader case, including glabridin and dipotassium glycyrrhizate, is on [[licorice-root-extract]] and not repeated here. **Bottom line:** graded `weak` because every human study that names this ingredient buries it in a cream with four or five other actives and runs without a control arm.

> **It has never been tested on its own.** The two clinical reports that name stearyl glycyrrhetinate both evaluate the same family of multi-active non-steroidal creams. One lists the formula as containing "zinc PCA, piroctone olamine, hydroxyphenyl propamidobenzoic acid, biosaccharide gum-2, and stearyl glycyrrhetinate".[^sd] The other tests "piroctone olamine (antifungal), biosaccharide gum-2 (antifungal), stearyl glycyrrhetinate (anti-inflammatory), and zinc l-pyrrolidone carboxylate (zinc-PCA) (antiseborrheic)".[^cradle] Two of those co-ingredients, [[piroctone-olamine]] and [[zinc-pca]], carry their own evidence here.

## The Rubric

**Soothing irritated skin**
Graded minimal on anecdote. The anti-inflammatory role is asserted in the second paper's aims sentence as a parenthetical descriptor of the ingredient, alongside antifungal and antiseborrheic labels for its companions.[^cradle] That is a statement of what each component is believed to do, written before the results, not an outcome the study measured. No trial consulted here compared a formula with stearyl glycyrrhetinate against the same formula without it, which is the comparison that would isolate it.

**Seborrhoeic dermatitis and cradle cap**
Graded minimal on anecdote, and the designs are the reason rather than the results. The adult report is a "pilot clinical study" in "20 HIV-positive patients" with mild to moderate facial seborrhoeic dermatitis, applied twice daily for 12 weeks with no antifungal or corticosteroid alongside; "All of the patients showed clinical improvement after 4 and 12 weeks of treatment" and "no adverse effects were reported".[^sd] The infant report is a "prospective, open-label trial" in "10 infants" using the gel cream twice daily for 30 days, finding "a statically significant reduction from baseline in erythema and desquamation severity" plus reduced colony counts for *Malassezia furfur* and *Staphylococcus aureus*.[^cradle] Both compare each patient against their own baseline. Neither has a control group.

## The Evidence

**Self-limiting is the word that matters.** The infant paper opens by calling cradle cap "a benign and self-limiting variant of seborrheic dermatitis".[^cradle] An uncontrolled before-and-after study of a condition that resolves on its own cannot separate the cream from time. That is a limitation of the design, not a criticism of the authors, who state the condition's nature plainly in their own background.

**The sponsorship is total in one of the two.** Every author of the adult study declares a relationship with ISDIN, the company whose cream was tested: two are employees reporting grants, one reports personal fees and non-financial support as a clinical investigator, one reports fees and outside grants, and one is a company speaker.[^sd] Industry funding does not make a result wrong, and a pilot case series with no control arm and universal sponsor ties is the weakest combination the two together can produce.

**The title undercounts the formula.** The adult paper's title names a cream "Containing Piroctone Olamine, Biosaccharide Gum-2 and Stearyl Glycyrrhetinate", three ingredients; its methods describe five, adding zinc PCA and hydroxyphenyl propamidobenzoic acid.[^sd] Anyone reading titles rather than methods would credit the three named actives for the work of five.

**Where you will actually meet it.** On this site it is declared in the ingredient list of [[dhc-deep-cleansing-oil]], a rinse-off cleanser, which is a long way from a twice-daily leave-on cream used for twelve weeks. Neither study above says anything about a cleansing oil. Conditions where the co-ingredients have real evidence are on [[seborrheic-dermatitis]] and [[dandruff]], and the better-evidenced options there are [[ketoconazole]] and [[piroctone-olamine]].

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Seborrhoeic dermatitis in someone who is immunosuppressed, and any persistent scalp or facial rash in an infant, belongs with a doctor rather than a cosmetic cream.

## Common Marketing Claims

These are the recurring claim patterns for this ingredient rather than quotations from any one brand, since no brand page was fetched for it.

**Licorice-derived soothing agent.** Accurate as chemistry and as intended function. It is not a statement that the ingredient has been shown to soothe anything in a trial that could attribute the effect to it.

**Clinically studied.** True in the narrow sense that two published studies name it. Both are uncontrolled, both test it inside a four or five active combination, and the larger of the two has twenty participants.[^sd][^cradle]
