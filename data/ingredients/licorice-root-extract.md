Licorice root extract (*Glycyrrhiza glabra*) supplies several distinct actives to skincare: glabridin, a flavonoid studied for pigment and inflammation; liquiritin, a flavonoid glycoside tested in melasma creams; and glycyrrhizinic acid (glycyrrhizin) and its salt dipotassium glycyrrhizate, used mainly as anti-inflammatory, soothing agents.[^yokota][^saeedi] Most published trials test the crude extract, a licorice fraction (glabridin, liquiritin), or glycyrrhizinic acid content rather than isolated dipotassium glycyrrhizate itself, so evidence for that specific salt in a finished serum is thinner than for the extract as a whole.

> A double-blind RCT found 2% licorice gel reduced eczema-type erythema, edema, and itching more than a 1% strength over two weeks (p<0.05), and mechanistic work shows glabridin inhibits tyrosinase in melanoma cells and calmed UVB-induced pigmentation and redness in guinea pig skin at a 0.5% topical dose.[^saeedi][^yokota] For melasma, a small early trial found a licorice cream produced an "excellent" pigment response in about 80% of treated lesions versus none on placebo, but later trials found licorice weaker than 4% [[hydroquinone]] and generally used it as a slower, gentler option rather than a stronger one.[^scoping]

## The Rubric

**Inflammation and skin irritation (topical)**
- **Effect size: modest.** In a double-blind trial, 30 patients per group used 1% or 2% licorice extract gel (standardized to 19.6% glycyrrhizinic acid) versus a base gel; the 2% strength reduced erythema, edema, and itching scores more than the 1% strength over two weeks (p<0.05).[^saeedi] The trial's own patient population is described as having "atopic dermatitis"-type eczema, not a cosmetic-tolerability panel, so this is a modest health-relevant anti-inflammatory signal rather than a pure cosmetic soothing claim. Separately, in cultured B16 melanoma cells and guinea pig skin, glabridin suppressed superoxide anion production and cyclooxygenase activity, and 0.5% topical glabridin reduced UVB-induced erythema alongside pigmentation.[^yokota]
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One double-blind RCT with a base-gel control and a two-week endpoint, plus supporting cell-culture and animal (guinea pig) mechanistic data; no large or independently replicated human trial isolates dipotassium glycyrrhizate specifically.[^saeedi][^yokota]

**Hyperpigmentation and melasma (topical)**
- **Effect size: modest, and inconsistent against an active comparator.** An early small placebo-controlled trial of 20 women applied a 2% licorice cream against a placebo cream for 4 weeks; a later scoping review of melasma trials reports that "about 80% of the lesions treated with licorice had excellent response," versus 0% on placebo, with erythema and sensitivity in two patients.[^scoping] But head-to-head against an active comparator, licorice performed worse: a trial comparing once-daily 4% licorice cream to 4% [[hydroquinone]] cream over 8 weeks found licorice "has less effect than 4% hydroquinone" by modified Melasma Area and Severity Index (mMASI) score.[^scoping] A separate placebo-controlled trial of twice-daily 4% licorice cream over 12 weeks reported a significant mMASI reduction with no side effects, without a direct comparator.[^scoping] The mechanism differs from arbutin's or hydroquinone's route: glabridin inhibited tyrosinase activity in B16 melanoma cells at 0.1 to 1.0 microg/ml with no detectable effect on the cells' DNA synthesis.[^yokota] Licorice-derived pigment agents are sometimes paired with [[arbutin]] or vitamin C rather than used alone; one trial found 4% licorice plus 5% ascorbic acid outperformed licorice alone.[^scoping]
- **Evidence quality: mixed.** Several small trials (20 to 84 patients), heterogeneous concentrations (2% to 4%) and durations (4 to 12 weeks), inconsistent comparators (placebo in some, active 4% hydroquinone in others), and no large or long-term controlled trial.[^scoping]

## How It Works

Glabridin, one of licorice's flavonoid constituents, inhibited tyrosinase activity in B16 murine melanoma cells "at concentrations of 0.1 to 1.0 microg/ml" with "no detectable effect on their DNA synthesis," and topical 0.5% glabridin reduced UVB-induced pigmentation and erythema in guinea pig skin; the same paper reports glabridin suppressed superoxide anion production and cyclooxygenase activity, the basis for its anti-inflammatory activity.[^yokota] Glycyrrhizinic acid, the licorice constituent behind dipotassium glycyrrhizate, is the standardized active in the Saeedi trial's anti-inflammatory gel, tested at 19.6% concentration within the finished topical preparation.[^saeedi]

## The Evidence

**Atopic eczema, licorice gel versus base gel (topical).** A double-blind clinical trial randomized patients to 1% or 2% licorice extract gel, standardized to 19.6% glycyrrhizinic acid, or a base gel, 30 patients per group, for two weeks. The 2% licorice gel was "more effective than 1% in reducing the scores for erythema, oedema and itching over two weeks (p<0.05)."[^saeedi] *A modest, statistically significant reduction in eczema-type inflammation and itch favoring the higher concentration over two weeks.*

**Melasma, licorice cream versus placebo (topical).** A small placebo-controlled trial of 20 women with melasma applied 2% licorice cream twice daily for 4 weeks against a placebo cream; a 2024 scoping review of herbal melasma trials reports "about 80% of the lesions treated with licorice had excellent response," compared with 0% on placebo, with erythema and sensitivity observed in two patients on licorice.[^scoping] *A meaningful early signal versus placebo in a small, short trial.*

**Melasma, licorice cream versus 4% hydroquinone (topical).** A trial applied 4% licorice cream once daily against 4% [[hydroquinone]] cream over 8 weeks; the scoping review reports licorice "has less effect than 4% hydroquinone" by mMASI score, with no complications reported in the licorice group.[^scoping] *Licorice underperformed an active pigment-lightening comparator on the same objective severity scale.*

**Melasma, licorice cream versus placebo, longer course (topical).** A separate trial applied 4% licorice cream twice daily for 12 weeks against placebo; the scoping review reports the mMASI score decreased significantly in the licorice group with no side effects reported.[^scoping] *A longer course against placebo reported a significant severity-score improvement, without a direct comparison to a stronger active.*

**Melasma, licorice plus vitamin C versus licorice alone (topical).** A trial combining 4% licorice with 5% ascorbic acid was reported by the scoping review as producing a significant decrease in the licorice-plus-ascorbic-acid group's MASI score compared with licorice alone.[^scoping] *Combining licorice with vitamin C outperformed licorice by itself in one trial, though the design cannot isolate how much of that gain came from the added ascorbic acid.*

*Evidence-transparency note: all four melasma trials above are cited via the single 2024 scoping review rather than the original trial papers, so the melasma efficacy claims rest on one secondary source; the anti-inflammatory data above draw on the primary Saeedi RCT and the Yokota mechanistic paper directly.[^scoping][^saeedi][^yokota]*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Natural, gentle skin brightener."** The best-supported pigment trial is small (20 patients) and short (4 weeks), and where licorice was tested head-to-head against 4% [[hydroquinone]] it performed worse by mMASI score, so "gentle but weaker" is closer to the evidence than a blanket brightening claim.[^scoping]
- **"Soothes irritated or sensitive skin."** The eczema-gel trial supports a real, modest anti-inflammatory effect at 2% concentration over a hydroquinone-free base gel, but it tested a standardized 19.6%-glycyrrhizinic-acid extract, not the isolated dipotassium glycyrrhizate salt often listed on an ingredient panel.[^saeedi]
