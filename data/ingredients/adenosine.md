Adenosine is a purine nucleoside, the same molecule that forms the backbone of ATP (molecular formula C10H13N5O4), sold in leave-on creams and serums as an anti-wrinkle active, usually alongside marketing that also invokes its separate role in wound healing.[^pubchem] The wrinkle evidence is a handful of small trials run or funded by the companies selling the delivery system being tested; the wound-healing story comes from mouse experiments on synthetic drugs that activate the same cell-surface receptors adenosine binds, not from adenosine itself applied to skin.

> Topical adenosine has thin, industry-linked evidence for reducing the look of wrinkles: an L'Oreal-run trial of a 1% adenosine film and a microneedle-patch maker's 22-woman trial of an adenosine cream comparator both reported statistically significant improvement on cosmetic measures.[^legendre][^kang] Its "wound healing" reputation rests on pharmacology showing synthetic adenosine A2A-receptor agonist drugs speed wound closure in mice, a mechanism related to adenosine but not a demonstration that a cosmetic adenosine product heals skin.[^victorvega][^valls]

## The Rubric

**Facial wrinkles and skin roughness (topical, cosmetic)**
- **Effect size: modest and cosmetic-only.** In a randomized, placebo-controlled, investigator-blind trial run by L'Oreal Recherche, a twice-daily 1% adenosine dissolvable film produced "a significant decrease in the skin roughness parameters as observed using fast optical in vivo topometry (FOITS)" after three and eight weeks.[^legendre] In a separate 22-subject trial of crow's-feet wrinkles (8 weeks of treatment within a 10-week observation window) funded by microneedle-patch maker Juvic Inc, a standard twice-daily adenosine cream (the comparator arm) produced statistically significant improvement in wrinkle depth, dermal density, elasticity, and hydration (P<0.05 across parameters); the microneedle patch beat the cream specifically on hydration (P<0.001).[^kang] Both are surface/appearance measurements, not evidence the ingredient changes skin biology, and both sit below the demonstrated collagen and photodamage effects of [[retinoids]].
- **Evidence quality: preliminary and industry-linked.** The two controlled trials that exist are run by, or funded by, the company selling the product tested (L'Oreal for its own film; Juvic Inc for its microneedle patch, with adenosine cream only as the comparator arm), and neither states an independent sample size large enough to rule out a small true effect.[^legendre][^kang]

**Wound healing (topical)**
- **Effect size: not established for the cosmetic ingredient.** Adenosine A2A-receptor agonist drugs, not adenosine itself, sped 50% wound closure in mice (day 1.9 to 3.5, versus day 4 for control, P<0.05 ANOVA) faster than PDGF gel (Becaplermin), which showed no significant benefit over control.[^victorvega] A review of this line of research frames it as a case for developing adenosine-receptor-agonist drugs (one candidate, Sonedenoson, was in development for diabetic foot ulcers) rather than as evidence for topical adenosine as sold in skincare.[^valls]
- **Evidence quality: preclinical and mechanistic only.** Rodent wounds, synthetic ligands selective for the A2A receptor, no human skin trial of adenosine itself for wound healing.[^victorvega][^valls]

## The Evidence

**Facial wrinkles (topical).** The randomized, placebo-controlled, investigator-blind L'Oreal Recherche trial applied a 1% adenosine-containing dissolvable film twice daily and measured skin roughness by fast optical in vivo topometry (FOITS), reporting "a significant decrease in the skin roughness parameters" at both the three-week and eight-week assessments; the abstract does not state the number of subjects.[^legendre] Separately, a 22-subject trial in women with crow's-feet wrinkles (8 weeks of treatment within a 10-week observation window), funded by microneedle-patch company Juvic Inc, compared an adenosine-loaded dissolving microneedle patch (applied once every three days) against a standard adenosine cream (applied twice daily) as the active comparator. Both arms "showed statistically significant efficacy for almost all parameters (P < 0.05)" on wrinkle depth (PRIMOS), dermal density (Dermascan C), elasticity (Cutometer), and hydration (Corneometer); the patch beat the cream specifically on hydration (P<0.001), with "no adverse effects... observed in either group."[^kang]

**Wound healing (topical, mechanistic).** In BALB/c mice, dorsal wounds (about 12mm) treated with the synthetic adenosine A2A-receptor agonists MRE0094 or CGS-21680 "achieved 50% wound closure significantly more rapidly than control application (day 1.9, 1.9, 3.5, 3.2, respectively, versus control day 4, p < 0.05 ANOVA)"; 0.01% Becaplermin gel (recombinant PDGF, an approved diabetic-ulcer drug) "did not increase the rate at which wounds closed" (day 7.2, not significant versus control).[^victorvega] A 2009 review by an overlapping research group (co-authors Cronstein and Montesinos, both on the mouse study above; lead author at Universitat de Valencia) covers adenosine A2A-receptor agonists as candidate wound-healing drugs, noting one such agonist, Sonedenoson, "is currently being evaluated as a prospective new therapy of diabetic foot ulcers."[^valls] Neither paper tests adenosine itself, topically, on skin.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Heals and repairs skin."** The controlled evidence behind this claim is mouse wound experiments using synthetic drugs that target the same receptor adenosine binds, not adenosine itself applied to skin, and not a human trial of any kind.[^victorvega][^valls]
- **"Reduces wrinkles."** Supported only by two small trials run or funded by the companies selling the product tested, measuring surface roughness or instrumental wrinkle depth rather than collagen or histology, on a similar evidence tier to cosmetic [[peptides]].[^legendre][^kang]
