Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate is a water-soluble, phosphate-bonded derivative of [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]], the magnesium salt of ascorbyl-2-phosphate. Like [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]] it is inactive as applied and depends on skin enzymes cleaving the phosphate group to release free ascorbic acid before it can act, a conversion step that has not been demonstrated in human skin with a measured rate. Its phosphate bond keeps it stable near neutral pH, in contrast with L-ascorbic acid, which is unstable in that same neutral-pH range.[^geesin] Its pigmentation reputation rests mainly on a single small, uncontrolled 1996 case series; the randomized controlled trials that exist tested it only as one ingredient in a combination product or as an adjunct to a chemical peel, not as an isolated agent compared with a vehicle.

> A 10% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate cream lightened pigmentation in 19 of 34 patients with melasma or senile freckles in a 1996 open-label case series with no control arm, a response seen far less often on normal, non-pigmented skin.[^kameyama] In two later randomized controlled trials it was tested only as part of a combination, not alone: a 10% niacinamide, 5% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, and 5% hyaluronic acid product reduced melasma severity (mMASI) by about 32% at 60 days, not significantly different from 4% hydroquinone's about 43% reduction in the same trial;[^barbosa] and adding 5% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate after a weekly trichloroacetic acid peel raised the responder rate to 81.1%, versus 66.2% for the peel alone.[^murtaza] In cultured human dermal fibroblasts, the magnesium salt matched ascorbic acid for stimulating collagen synthesis, while the sodium salt needed at least tenfold more to do the same.[^geesin] Tested head-to-head against ascorbic acid in one human comparative study using non-invasive skin instruments, only the ascorbic acid formulation improved transepidermal water loss; the magnesium ascorbyl phosphate formulation only shifted a cosmetic elasticity ratio.[^campos] No published human trial has compared magnesium ascorbyl phosphate directly with L-ascorbic acid for pigmentation, nor tested it alone against a vehicle in a controlled design, and its chief documented advantage remains chemical stability rather than a stronger measured effect.[^geesin][^cir]

## The Rubric

**Hyperpigmentation and melasma (topical, 10% concentration)**
- **Effect size: modest and selective for already-pigmented skin, in an uncontrolled series; combination and adjunct effects are also documented.** In a 1996 open-label case series with no control arm, a 10% magnesium L-ascorbyl-2-phosphate (VC-PMG) cream produced significant lightening in 19 of 34 patients (56%) with melasma or senile freckles, compared with a response in only 3 of 25 volunteers (12%) with normal, non-pigmented skin, and it suppressed tyrosinase-driven melanin formation in cultured melanoma cells.[^kameyama] Two later randomized controlled trials tested magnesium ascorbyl phosphate only in combination, not as an isolated agent: a 10% niacinamide, 5% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, 5% hyaluronic acid product taken twice daily reduced mMASI by about 32% at 60 days versus about 43% for 4% hydroquinone applied nightly, with no statistically significant difference between the two arms (n=50, randomized, double-blind, p>0.2 at day 60);[^barbosa] and adding 5% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate cream daily to a weekly 20% trichloroacetic acid peel raised the MASI-score responder rate to 81.1% (60 of 74), versus 66.2% (49 of 74) for the peel alone, in a 148-patient RCT (p=0.040).[^murtaza] "Modest and selective for hyperpigmented skin over normal skin in one uncontrolled 1996 case series; the controlled human trials add magnesium ascorbyl phosphate to niacinamide and hyaluronic acid or to a chemical peel rather than testing it alone against a vehicle."
- **Evidence quality: uncontrolled for the ingredient alone; randomized and controlled only in combination or as an adjunct.** The pigmentation case series (59 participants total) is more than three decades old, open-label, and describes no vehicle-controlled or blinded design.[^kameyama] The combination-versus-hydroquinone trial was randomized and double-blind but did not isolate magnesium ascorbyl phosphate's own contribution to the mixed product.[^barbosa] The peel-adjunct trial was a randomized controlled trial (n=148) but likewise tested magnesium ascorbyl phosphate only added to a peel, not alone.[^murtaza]

**Collagen synthesis (mechanism, cell culture only)**
- **Effect size: comparable to ascorbic acid, but only in cultured cells.** In monolayer cultures of human dermal fibroblasts, the magnesium salt of ascorbyl-2-phosphate "was found to be equivalent to ascorbic acid in stimulating collagen synthesis," while the sodium salt required at least a tenfold greater concentration to produce the same effect.[^geesin] This is a cell-culture finding about collagen gene activity, not a measurement of collagen in human skin after topical use. "Equivalent to ascorbic acid at stimulating collagen synthesis in cultured fibroblasts; never shown as a skin-level outcome in people."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** A single 1993 in vitro fibroblast-culture study; no human in vivo collagen follow-up located.[^geesin]

**Skin elasticity and moisture (topical, comparative human study)**
- **Effect size: modest, and narrower than ascorbic acid's.** In a human study using non-invasive instruments (transepidermal water loss, corneometry, cutometry), a topical magnesium ascorbyl phosphate formulation altered the skin's viscoelastic-to-elastic ratio, but only the ascorbic acid formulation in the same comparison changed transepidermal water loss.[^campos] The measured MAP effect is a cosmetic elasticity shift, not the barrier/water-loss measure that moved with plain ascorbic acid. "A cosmetic elasticity effect only; the barrier-related water-loss measure moved for ascorbic acid, not for magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, in the same study."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One comparative human study; concentrations and sample size are not given in the available abstract.[^campos]

**UV-related skin tumor delay (animal model, not a human finding)**
- **Effect size: unestablished in humans.** The Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel cites data that magnesium ascorbyl phosphate given immediately after UV exposure in hairless mice "significantly delayed skin tumor formation and hyperplasia induced by chronic exposure to UV radiation."[^cir] That is a mouse-model result; no human trial of topical magnesium ascorbyl phosphate reports a photoprotection or skin-cancer endpoint. "A mouse-only finding; no human data support a skin-cancer or photoprotective effect for topical magnesium ascorbyl phosphate."
- **Evidence quality: not applicable to humans.** Cited from an animal toxicology/safety review, not a clinical trial.[^cir]

## The Evidence

**Hyperpigmentation.** Kameyama and colleagues applied 10% VC-PMG (magnesium L-ascorbyl-2-phosphate) cream to patients with melasma or senile freckles and to volunteers with normal skin. "The lightening effect was significant in 19 of 34 patients with chloasma or senile freckles and in 3 of 25 patients with normal skin," and the paper's in vitro work found VC-PMG "suppressed melanin formation by tyrosinase and melanoma cells." Percutaneous absorption testing in dermatomed human skin found 1.6% of the applied dose remained 48 hours after application. The authors concluded "VC-PMG is effective in reducing skin hyperpigmentation in some patients."[^kameyama]

**Combination trial versus hydroquinone.** In a randomized, double-blind trial, women with facial melasma applied twice daily either a product containing 10% niacinamide, 5% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, and 5% hyaluronic acid, or 4% hydroquinone nightly with a placebo in the morning (50 enrolled, 48 completed, 60 days). mMASI fell about 16% by day 14 and about 32% by day 60 in the combination group, versus about 10% and about 43% for hydroquinone; the authors reported "both interventions led to a progressive improvement in mMASI, MELASQoL, and GAIS, without a difference between them on D14 and D60 (p>0.2)." Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate was not tested as a standalone agent in this trial.[^barbosa]

**Adjunct to a chemical peel.** In a randomized controlled trial of 148 patients with epidermal melasma, one group received a weekly 20% trichloroacetic acid peel plus daily 5% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate cream and the other received the peel alone. A significant MASI-score response occurred in 81.1% (60 of 74) of the peel-plus-MAP group versus 66.2% (49 of 74) of the peel-alone group (p=0.040). Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate was tested only as an adjunct to the peel, not as a standalone treatment.[^murtaza]

**Collagen synthesis.** Geesin, Gordon, and Berg compared the sodium and magnesium salts of ascorbyl-2-phosphate against ascorbic acid in monolayer cultures of human dermal fibroblasts. The magnesium salt "was found to be equivalent to ascorbic acid in stimulating collagen synthesis," while "the sodium salt required at least a tenfold greater concentration to produce the same effect as ascorbic acid." On stability, they found "ascorbic acid was unstable at neutral pH compared to solutions of either sodium or magnesium ascorbyl-2-phosphate," and recommended the phosphate salts for "experiments where stability of ascorbic acid is a concern, e.g. in long-term cultures or in in vivo studies."[^geesin]

**Comparative human skin measures.** Maia Campos, Gonçalves, and Gaspar tested topical formulations of ascorbic acid, magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, and ascorbyl tetra-isopalmitate using non-invasive instruments (Tewameter for transepidermal water loss, Corneometer for stratum corneum moisture, Cutometer for viscoelasticity). In the in vivo comparison, "only the formulation containing AA caused alterations in TEWL values," while the "formulations containing MAP caused alterations in the viscoelastic-to-elastic ratio." In antioxidant testing, "AA had the best antioxidant potential, and MAP was more effective than ATIP" in aqueous systems, though "ATIP was more effective than MAP" in lipid systems.[^campos]

**Regulatory safety review.** The Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel assessed magnesium ascorbyl phosphate as part of a group review with ascorbic acid and its other salts and esters, reporting cosmetic use concentrations of "0.001% to 3%" and concluding the ingredient group "does not present a risk of skin sensitization." The same review cites animal data that magnesium ascorbyl phosphate "administration immediately after exposure in hairless mice significantly delayed skin tumor formation and hyperplasia induced by chronic exposure to UV radiation."[^cir]

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Brightens skin as effectively as vitamin C, but gentler."** No published human trial has tested magnesium ascorbyl phosphate alone, head-to-head, against L-ascorbic acid for pigmentation. The only direct comparison found tested a different endpoint (skin elasticity and water loss) and found ascorbic acid, not magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, moved the barrier-related measure.[^campos] Where magnesium ascorbyl phosphate has been tested for melasma in a controlled design, it was combined with niacinamide and hyaluronic acid, and its group's mMASI reduction (about 32%) was numerically, though not statistically significantly, below hydroquinone's (about 43%).[^barbosa]
- **"Boosts collagen production."** Supported only in cultured human fibroblasts, where the magnesium salt matched ascorbic acid; no human topical study measuring skin collagen after magnesium ascorbyl phosphate use was located.[^geesin]
- **"A stable form of vitamin C that works just like the pure form."** Stability is real and documented: the magnesium salt resists the neutral-pH degradation that affects ascorbic acid in solution, allowing formulation near neutral pH rather than the low pH L-ascorbic acid needs to stay stable, which is also relevant to stinging and irritation potential.[^geesin] But it is inactive until skin enzymes convert it to ascorbic acid, a conversion not demonstrated in human skin with a measured rate, and its human clinical evidence is either one small, uncontrolled 1996 pigmentation case series or randomized trials that added it to other ingredients or to a peel rather than testing it alone, narrower than the trial base behind [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]] itself.
