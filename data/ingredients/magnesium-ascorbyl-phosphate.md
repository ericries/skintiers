Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate is a water-soluble, phosphate-bonded derivative of [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]], the magnesium salt of ascorbyl-2-phosphate. Like [[sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate]] it is inactive as applied and depends on skin enzymes cleaving the phosphate group to release free ascorbic acid before it can act; its human evidence is older and thinner than L-ascorbic acid's, resting mainly on a single 1996 pigmentation case series plus small comparative and cell-culture data.

> A 10% magnesium ascorbyl phosphate cream lightened pigmentation in 19 of 34 patients with melasma or senile freckles in a 1996 case series, a response seen far less often on normal, non-pigmented skin.[^kameyama] In cultured human dermal fibroblasts, the magnesium salt matched ascorbic acid for stimulating collagen synthesis, while the sodium salt needed at least tenfold more to do the same.[^geesin] Tested head-to-head against ascorbic acid in one human comparative study using non-invasive skin instruments, only the ascorbic acid formulation improved transepidermal water loss; the magnesium ascorbyl phosphate formulation only shifted a cosmetic elasticity ratio.[^campos] No published human trial has compared magnesium ascorbyl phosphate directly with L-ascorbic acid for pigmentation, and its chief documented advantage remains chemical stability rather than a stronger measured effect.[^geesin][^cir]

## The Rubric

**Hyperpigmentation and melasma (topical, 10% concentration)**
- **Effect size: modest and selective for already-pigmented skin.** In a 1996 case series, a 10% magnesium L-ascorbyl-2-phosphate (VC-PMG) cream produced significant lightening in 19 of 34 patients (56%) with melasma or senile freckles, compared with a response in only 3 of 25 volunteers (12%) with normal, non-pigmented skin, and it suppressed tyrosinase-driven melanin formation in cultured melanoma cells.[^kameyama] This is the oldest and most-cited human data point for the ingredient and predates the vehicle-controlled trial designs used to grade [[hyperpigmentation]] treatments today. "Modest and selective for hyperpigmented skin over normal skin, from one 1996 case series with no reported comparison arm against L-ascorbic acid itself."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One clinical case series (59 participants total), more than three decades old, with no vehicle-controlled or blinded design described in the available report.[^kameyama]

**Collagen synthesis (mechanism, cell culture only)**
- **Effect size: comparable to ascorbic acid, but only in cultured cells.** In monolayer cultures of human dermal fibroblasts, the magnesium salt of ascorbyl-2-phosphate "was found to be equivalent to ascorbic acid in stimulating collagen synthesis," while the sodium salt required at least a tenfold greater concentration to produce the same effect.[^geesin] This is a cell-culture finding about collagen gene activity, not a measurement of collagen in human skin after topical use. "Equivalent to ascorbic acid at stimulating collagen synthesis in cultured fibroblasts; never shown as a skin-level outcome in people."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** A single 1993 in vitro fibroblast-culture study; no human in vivo collagen follow-up located.[^geesin]

**Skin elasticity and moisture (topical, comparative human study)**
- **Effect size: modest, and narrower than ascorbic acid's.** In a human study using non-invasive instruments (transepidermal water loss, corneometry, cutometry), a topical magnesium ascorbyl phosphate formulation altered the skin's viscoelastic-to-elastic ratio, but only the ascorbic acid formulation in the same comparison changed transepidermal water loss.[^campos] The measured MAP effect is a cosmetic elasticity shift, not the barrier/water-loss measure that moved with plain ascorbic acid. "A cosmetic elasticity effect only; the barrier-related water-loss measure moved for ascorbic acid, not for magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, in the same study."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** One comparative human study; concentrations and sample size are not given in the available abstract.[^campos]

**UV-related skin tumor delay (animal model, not a human finding)**
- **Effect size: unestablished in humans.** The Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel cites data that magnesium ascorbyl phosphate given immediately after UV exposure in hairless mice "significantly delayed skin tumor formation and hyperplasia induced by chronic exposure to UV radiation."[^cir] That is a mouse-model result; no human trial of topical magnesium ascorbyl phosphate reports a photoprotection or skin-cancer endpoint. "A mouse-only finding; no human data support a skin-cancer or photoprotective effect for topical magnesium ascorbyl phosphate."
- **Evidence quality: not applicable to humans.** Cited from an animal toxicology/safety review, not a clinical trial.[^cir]

## The Evidence

**Hyperpigmentation.** Kameyama and colleagues applied 10% VC-PMG (magnesium L-ascorbyl-2-phosphate) cream to patients with melasma or senile freckles and to volunteers with normal skin. "The lightening effect was significant in 19 of 34 patients with chloasma or senile freckles and in 3 of 25 patients with normal skin," and the paper's in vitro work found VC-PMG "suppressed melanin formation by tyrosinase and melanoma cells." Percutaneous absorption testing in dermatomed human skin found 1.6% of the applied dose remained 48 hours after application. The authors concluded "VC-PMG is effective in reducing skin hyperpigmentation in some patients."[^kameyama]

**Collagen synthesis.** Geesin, Gordon, and Berg compared the sodium and magnesium salts of ascorbyl-2-phosphate against ascorbic acid in monolayer cultures of human dermal fibroblasts. The magnesium salt "was found to be equivalent to ascorbic acid in stimulating collagen synthesis," while "the sodium salt required at least a tenfold greater concentration to produce the same effect as ascorbic acid." On stability, they found "ascorbic acid was unstable at neutral pH compared to solutions of either sodium or magnesium ascorbyl-2-phosphate," and recommended the phosphate salts for "experiments where stability of ascorbic acid is a concern, e.g. in long-term cultures or in in vivo studies."[^geesin]

**Comparative human skin measures.** Maia Campos, Gonçalves, and Gaspar tested topical formulations of ascorbic acid, magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, and ascorbyl tetra-isopalmitate using non-invasive instruments (Tewameter for transepidermal water loss, Corneometer for stratum corneum moisture, Cutometer for viscoelasticity). In the in vivo comparison, "only the formulation containing AA caused alterations in TEWL values," while the "formulations containing MAP caused alterations in the viscoelastic-to-elastic ratio." In antioxidant testing, "AA had the best antioxidant potential, and MAP was more effective than ATIP" in aqueous systems, though "ATIP was more effective than MAP" in lipid systems.[^campos]

**Regulatory safety review.** The Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel assessed magnesium ascorbyl phosphate as part of a group review with ascorbic acid and its other salts and esters, reporting cosmetic use concentrations of "0.001% to 3%" and concluding the ingredient group "does not present a risk of skin sensitization." The same review cites animal data that magnesium ascorbyl phosphate "administration immediately after exposure in hairless mice significantly delayed skin tumor formation and hyperplasia induced by chronic exposure to UV radiation."[^cir]

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"Brightens skin as effectively as vitamin C, but gentler."** No published human trial has tested magnesium ascorbyl phosphate head-to-head against L-ascorbic acid for pigmentation. The only direct human comparison found tested a different endpoint (skin elasticity and water loss) and found ascorbic acid, not magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, moved the barrier-related measure.[^campos]
- **"Boosts collagen production."** Supported only in cultured human fibroblasts, where the magnesium salt matched ascorbic acid; no human topical study measuring skin collagen after magnesium ascorbyl phosphate use was located.[^geesin]
- **"A stable form of vitamin C that works just like the pure form."** Stability is real and documented: the magnesium salt resists the neutral-pH degradation that affects ascorbic acid in solution.[^geesin] But it is inactive until skin enzymes convert it to ascorbic acid, and its human clinical evidence (one 1996 pigmentation case series) is narrower than the trial base behind [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]] itself.
