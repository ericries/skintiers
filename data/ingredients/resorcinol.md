Resorcinol (1,3-dihydroxybenzene) is a phenol-derived keratolytic and antiseptic that has been used in dermatology "for over a century," most familiarly at low concentration paired with sulfur in old-school OTC acne products, at higher concentration in chemical peels such as Jessner's solution, and, more recently, as a 15% cream or peel proposed for mild-to-moderate hidradenitis suppurativa (HS).[^ijpgd] Its regulatory footing is narrow: FDA's final OTC acne monograph recognizes only resorcinol 2% combined with sulfur 3-8%, or resorcinol monoacetate 3% combined with sulfur 3-8%; it does not recognize resorcinol alone, at any concentration, as a safe-and-effective standalone acne active.[^fda]

> Resorcinol's dermatologic evidence is old and thin. Its acne role is inseparable from the sulfur it is always paired with in the FDA monograph, and modern acne actives such as [[benzoyl-peroxide]], the [[retinoids]], and [[azelaic-acid]] all carry far larger modern controlled-trial programs.[^fda] The best-documented modern use is as a 15% cream or peel for mild-to-moderate HS, an area covered in one clause on the [[hidradenitis-suppurativa]] page, where it sits well behind biologics and surgery as a narrow topical option and has never been tested against placebo, only against topical clindamycin.[^boer][^molinelli] Safety is generally reassuring at low concentration on intact skin, but real caveats exist: historical case reports of reversible thyroid effects and reports of methemoglobinemia after high-concentration, large-area, or damaged-skin use, plus a documented tendency to cause brown hyperpigmentation.[^welsch][^cassano][^ijpgd]

## The Rubric

**Acne vulgaris (OTC, resorcinol 2% combined with sulfur, per the FDA monograph)**
- **Effect size: not established by a modern controlled trial.** FDA's final OTC monograph lists "Resorcinol, 2 percent, when combined with sulfur" and "Resorcinol monoacetate, 3 percent, when combined with sulfur" as permitted acne actives, but the monograph does not cite a modern vehicle-controlled efficacy trial, and it never permits resorcinol as a standalone active.[^fda] A 2024 dermatology review notes resorcinol's comedolytic mechanism (disrupting keratin's hydrogen bonds to loosen the follicular plug) but does not cite a modern head-to-head acne trial against benzoyl peroxide, retinoids, or azelaic acid.[^ijpgd] "Regulatory permission to sell, not evidence of superiority; the monograph itself only allows resorcinol bundled with sulfur, never alone."
- **Evidence quality: weak and dated.** No retrievable randomized controlled trial isolates resorcinol's contribution to the resorcinol-sulfur combination for acne. Modern acne care has moved to [[benzoyl-peroxide]], the [[retinoids]], and [[azelaic-acid]], each with large controlled-trial programs the resorcinol-sulfur combination never ran.[^fda]

**Hidradenitis suppurativa, mild-to-moderate (topical, 15% cream or peel)**
- **Effect size: modest, comparator-beating in one retrospective study, but with no placebo arm.** A 2022 retrospective study of 134 patients with mild-to-moderate HS compared topical resorcinol 15% (n=61) to topical clindamycin 1% (n=73) over 12 weeks and found "patients treated with resorcinol 15% showed a significant improvement in Hidradenitis Suppurativa Clinical Response, International Hidradenitis Suppurativa Severity Score System, and Pain Visual Analogue Scale score from baseline compared to patients treated with clindamycin 1%," with no patient in either group stopping treatment.[^molinelli] An earlier open, uncontrolled case-note review of 12 women with Hurley stage 1-2 HS self-treating with resorcinol 15% over at least a year found "all patients experienced a significant decrease in pain."[^boer] "Beat an active comparator in a retrospective study and helped pain in an open case series; neither design has a placebo or blinding, so the true effect size is unknown."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Both studies are retrospective or open-label and uncontrolled by a placebo arm; this is the same evidence tier as the topical options on the [[hidradenitis-suppurativa]] page generally, where real treatment (biologics, oral antibiotics, surgery) carries the strongest evidence and topicals are reserved for mild, localized disease.[^boer][^molinelli]

**Safety (topical, all uses)**
- **Reassuring at low concentration on intact skin, with real caveats at higher concentration or on damaged skin.** A small human study of 2% resorcinol in a hydroalcoholic vehicle (n=3) found a skin penetration rate of "0.37 micrograms/cm2/hour," with only "1.64%" of a two-week, twice-daily 800 mg dose over about 30% of body surface recovered in urine, no resorcinol detected in blood at weeks 1 through 4, and "no... abnormalities in thyroid function or blood chemistries," concluding "resorcinol (2%) appears safe for topical use in humans."[^yeung] But a 2008 toxicology review of case reports found resorcinol "caused reversible adverse effects on the human thyroid gland manifested as hypothyroidism" specifically in patients "who had ulcerating leg varicosities and underwent prolonged treatment with ointments containing high concentrations of resorcinol," attributing the effect to compromised skin barrier function increasing absorption; the same review found no thyroid or reproductive effects in a two-generation rat study even at high oral doses.[^welsch] A separate toxicology review of peeling agents notes resorcinol among the phenol derivatives capable of systemic toxicity, with red-blood-cell changes such as methemoglobinemia, hemolytic anemia, hemoglobinuria, and cyanosis "documented in a few studies," and identifies resorcinol as the peeling agent most associated with allergic contact reactions.[^cassano][^ijpgd] Brown hyperpigmentation that resolves on its own has also been documented after resorcinol use, including a case shown after a resorcinol-sulfur lotion for truncal acne.[^ijpgd]

## How It Works

Resorcinol is a phenol in which a hydroxyl group substitutes for a hydrogen atom at the meta position; it is water-soluble, hygroscopic, and darkens to pale red on exposure to air and light.[^ijpgd] Its keratolytic action comes from disrupting keratin's weak hydrogen bonds, producing "a significant concentration-dependent desquamation action" that loosens the follicular keratin plug implicated in acne and related follicular occlusion disorders.[^ijpgd] It is absorbed by the oral, dermal, and subcutaneous routes, metabolized quickly, and eliminated mostly as glucuronide conjugates in urine; a 2024 review notes "no evidence of bioaccumulation" and that absorption through undamaged skin from a hydroalcoholic vehicle "is limited."[^ijpgd] It also carries anti-inflammatory (via prostaglandin E), antifungal, and antiseptic activity, which is why it has historically been used far beyond acne, in conditions from tinea and viral warts to seborrheic dermatitis.[^ijpgd]

## The Evidence

**Regulatory status (acne, OTC).** FDA's final OTC monograph for topical acne drug products lists the permitted active ingredients: "Resorcinol, 2 percent, when combined with sulfur," "Resorcinol monoacetate, 3 percent, when combined with sulfur," alongside benzoyl peroxide 2.5-10%, salicylic acid 0.5-2%, and sulfur 3-10% (or 3-8% in combination with resorcinol).[^fda] The monograph's combination rule is explicit: resorcinol "may be combined with sulfur," and resorcinol monoacetate likewise; the monograph never lists resorcinol as a permitted standalone active at any concentration.[^fda] *This is the durable, primary-source ceiling on resorcinol's OTC acne credentials: regulatory permission for a fixed low-dose combination, not a citation to a modern efficacy trial.*

**Hidradenitis suppurativa, comparative study.** Molinelli and colleagues retrospectively compared topical resorcinol 15% (n=61) against topical clindamycin 1% (n=73) in 134 patients with mild-to-moderate HS over 12 weeks, finding resorcinol produced "significant improvement" over clindamycin on clinical response, severity score, and pain visual-analogue-scale measures, with no discontinuations in either group.[^molinelli] *A retrospective, non-blinded, non-placebo-controlled comparison; useful for showing resorcinol is at least as usable as the one topical HS treatment with actual RCT evidence (clindamycin), but it cannot establish resorcinol's effect size against no treatment.*

**Hidradenitis suppurativa, self-treatment case series.** Boer and Jemec reviewed the case notes of 12 women with Hurley stage 1-2 HS who self-treated painful nodules with topical resorcinol 15% for at least a year, reporting "all patients experienced a significant decrease in pain" and shorter-lasting abscesses, while recommending "further trials... to confirm these results."[^boer] *Open, uncontrolled, and small; the honest read is that this generated a hypothesis the later Molinelli comparative study followed up on, not proof on its own.*

**Percutaneous absorption and short-term safety.** Yeung and colleagues measured absorption of topically applied 2% resorcinol in three human subjects, finding a skin penetration rate of "0.37 micrograms/cm2/hour" and, after two weeks of twice-daily application of 800 mg resorcinol over roughly 30% of body surface, average urinary recovery of "1.64%" of the applied dose as conjugated metabolites; no resorcinol was detected in blood at any of four weekly draws, and no thyroid-function or blood-chemistry abnormalities were found, leading the authors to conclude 2% resorcinol "appears safe for topical use in humans."[^yeung] *A small (n=3), short-term study of a single concentration on presumably intact skin; reassuring for typical low-dose dermatologic use, but not a large-scale or long-term safety trial.*

**Thyroid toxicity, historical case reports versus animal data.** Welsch's 2008 toxicology review found that historical case reports describe resorcinol causing "reversible adverse effects on the human thyroid gland manifested as hypothyroidism" specifically in patients with ulcerating leg varicosities under "prolonged treatment with ointments containing high concentrations of resorcinol," while standard animal studies, including a two-generation rat reproductive toxicity study, found no thyroid or reproductive effects even at high oral doses; the review attributes the human cases to compromised skin barrier function increasing systemic absorption in damaged, ulcerated skin.[^welsch] *This is the key nuance: the thyroid signal comes from high-concentration, prolonged use on damaged/ulcerated skin, a very different exposure than a short course of low-percent resorcinol on intact acne-prone or HS-affected skin, but it is real and belongs in any honest safety accounting.*

## Common Marketing Claims

None of the claims below is established beyond what The Evidence above supports.

- **"An FDA-approved acne treatment."** Technically true only for the fixed low-dose combination with sulfur under the OTC monograph; FDA has never recognized resorcinol alone as safe and effective for acne, and the monograph does not cite a modern efficacy trial for the combination either.[^fda]
- **"A powerful alternative to retinoids for clearing pores."** A 2024 review describes resorcinol's comedolytic mechanism favorably, but no source in this page's sourcing puts resorcinol through a head-to-head modern trial against the [[retinoids]], [[azelaic-acid]], or [[salicylic-acid]], each of which has a far larger controlled-trial base for acne.[^ijpgd]
- **"Completely safe because it's an old, natural-feeling ingredient."** Low-concentration, short-term, intact-skin use looks reassuring in the small Yeung study, but real safety signals exist: historical thyroid effects with high-concentration, prolonged, damaged-skin use, documented methemoglobinemia and related blood changes, resorcinol's standing as the peeling agent most linked to allergic contact reactions, and self-resolving brown hyperpigmentation.[^welsch][^cassano][^ijpgd][^yeung]
