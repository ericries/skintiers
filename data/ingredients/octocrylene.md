Octocrylene (INCI octocrylene) is an organic sunscreen filter that absorbs ultraviolet-B and short-wave ultraviolet-A, and it is used in United States and European sunscreens both as a filter in its own right and as a photostabilizer that slows the breakdown of the ultraviolet-A filter avobenzone.[^degroot][^fda2019][^chaudhuri] Its absorption is concentrated in UVB (about 290 to 320 nm) and the adjacent UVA II band (about 320 to 340 nm), and it covers little of the long-wave UVA I above 340 nm, so it contributes mainly to SPF, a UVB metric, and adds only narrow short-wave UVA coverage; SPF and the separate UVA axis are explained on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^degroot][^cfr352] Regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use is one of the few skincare practices with randomized-trial support for slowing photoaging and reducing skin-cancer burden, and octocrylene is one component of the older US filter set that delivers that protection; the health benefit is established for the practice, not for octocrylene alone, and lives on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]]. Three questions shape how it is graded: how much of the spectrum it actually covers, what its measured systemic absorption and its slow degradation to benzophenone mean, and its record as a cause of photoallergic contact dermatitis.

> Octocrylene is a relatively photostable UVB and short-wave UVA-II filter that also helps stabilize avobenzone, but it covers little long-wave UVA on its own, is absorbed into the bloodstream above the FDA's safety-testing threshold, slowly degrades to benzophenone in the bottle, and is a documented cause of photoallergic contact dermatitis, especially in people previously sensitized to the drug ketoprofen; the European Union's safety committee nonetheless judged it safe as a filter at up to 10%.[^degroot][^avenel][^matta2019][^downs][^eu2022]

## The Rubric

The grade below is for octocrylene's job in a sunscreen, ultraviolet photoprotection and avobenzone stabilization, framed relative to the other filters it is measured against. Systemic absorption, benzophenone generation, and skin sensitization are a separate axis and are graded separately, not folded into the photoprotection grade.

**Ultraviolet photoprotection (UVB and short-wave UVA II)**
- **Effect size: modest as a standalone filter, useful as a photostabilizer.** Octocrylene absorbs UVB and the short-wave UVA II band; a dermatology review describes it as "an ultraviolet (UV)B and UVAII absorber."[^degroot] It contributes to SPF and to narrow short-wave UVA, but it covers little of the long-wave UVA I above 340 nm that drives photoaging, so it is not a broad-spectrum UVA filter and complements rather than replaces avobenzone or mineral [[zinc-oxide]].[^degroot] Its distinct value is photostability: the FDA notes that "sunscreen active ingredients (e.g., octocrylene) can serve as photostabilizers" for avobenzone, and an independent formulation study lists it among "current avobenzone photostabilizers."[^fda2019][^chaudhuri] It is an older, narrower filter than the modern broad-spectrum organic filters standard in Europe and Asia (bemotrizinol and bisoctrizole, the Tinosorb filters, and the Mexoryls). "Modest UVB and short-UVA coverage on its own; its main contribution is stabilizing avobenzone, and it is narrower than the broad-spectrum modern filters it is measured against."
- **Evidence quality: solid for the spectral and photostabilizer characterization; the health payoff is inherited, not filter-specific.** The UVB/UVA II coverage and the photostabilizer role are documented across a dermatology review, regulatory text, and formulation studies.[^degroot][^fda2019][^chaudhuri] What is not demonstrated for octocrylene on its own is a clinical-endpoint benefit; the randomized evidence that broad-spectrum sunscreen slows photoaging and lowers skin-cancer burden is for the practice of regular use, covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].

**Systemic absorption, benzophenone, and skin sensitization (a separate axis, not an efficacy grade)**
- Under the FDA's own maximal-use trials, octocrylene is absorbed through skin into the bloodstream well above the 0.5 ng/mL plasma threshold above which the agency recommends further nonclinical safety testing.[^matta2019][^matta2020] No health harm from this absorption has been demonstrated, and the authors of both trials state their results "do not indicate that individuals should refrain from the use of sunscreen."[^matta2019][^matta2020]
- Octocrylene slowly degrades to benzophenone, which accumulates in the product over time; an analysis of commercial sunscreens measured benzophenone in every octocrylene-containing product tested and in none of the product without it.[^downs] That study's authors describe benzophenone as "a mutagen, carcinogen, and endocrine disruptor," a characterization contested in three published commentaries in the same journal, and the European Union's Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety, having considered the impurity, still concluded octocrylene "is safe as a UV-filter at concentrations up to 10 % in cosmetic products when used individually."[^downs][^eu2022]
- Octocrylene is a documented cause of photoallergic contact dermatitis, seen mainly in adults previously sensitized to the anti-inflammatory drug ketoprofen; plain contact allergy is less frequent and is seen mostly in children.[^degroot][^avenel]

## What We Actually Know

Octocrylene is a relatively photostable organic filter whose absorption sits in UVB and the short-wave UVA II band, so it adds to SPF and to narrow short-wave UVA but does little for long-wave UVA I on its own.[^degroot][^cfr352] Its practical value in a formula is twofold: it is a filter, and it is one of the additives formulators use to slow the photodegradation of avobenzone, a role noted by the FDA and by formulation studies.[^fda2019][^chaudhuri] Two FDA maximal-use trials showed octocrylene is absorbed into plasma above the 0.5 ng/mL threshold that triggers a call for further FDA safety testing; the trial authors emphasized that this is not a reason to stop using sunscreen, and no harm has been shown.[^matta2019][^matta2020] Octocrylene degrades to benzophenone over time, which one analysis detected in every octocrylene-containing sunscreen it tested; the toxicological significance is disputed, and the EU's safety committee reassessed octocrylene in 2021 and kept it permitted at up to 10%.[^downs][^eu2022] It is a recognized skin sensitizer, most often causing photoallergic contact dermatitis in adults with prior ketoprofen photoallergy.[^degroot][^avenel] The health value of using it, slowing photoaging and reducing skin-cancer burden, is the value of regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], not any octocrylene-specific clinical-endpoint trial.

## How It Works

Octocrylene is the 2-ethylhexyl ester of 2-cyano-3,3-diphenylacrylic acid, an organic (chemical) filter that absorbs a UV photon and releases the energy as heat rather than scattering light the way mineral filters do. Its absorption is concentrated in UVB (about 290 to 320 nm) and the adjacent short-wave UVA II (about 320 to 340 nm), with little coverage of the long-wave UVA I above 340 nm, which is why it adds mainly to SPF and is paired with dedicated UVA filters.[^degroot][^cfr352] It is comparatively photostable, and it acts as a photostabilizer for the unstable UVA filter avobenzone: photostabilizers of this kind work by accepting the excited triplet-state energy that would otherwise drive avobenzone's breakdown, a mechanism a formulation study describes for a related stabilizer as "triplet-state energy transfer from avobenzone."[^fda2019][^chaudhuri] The same photochemistry has a slow side reaction. According to a stability analysis, "octocrylene generates benzophenone through a retro-aldol condensation," so benzophenone accumulates in the finished product over its shelf life.[^downs]

## The Evidence

Primary evidence, by question.

**Spectral coverage and the photostabilizer role.** A review of contact and photocontact allergy identifies octocrylene as "an ultraviolet (UV)B and UVAII absorber" introduced in the late 1990s and now widely used in sunscreens and skin-care cosmetics.[^degroot] The UVB and UVA II bands it covers correspond to roughly 290 to 340 nm, leaving the long-wave UVA I (340 to 400 nm) to dedicated UVA filters; the band boundaries and why they matter are set out on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^cfr352] The photostabilizer role is documented independently: in its 2019 proposed rule the FDA states that "sunscreen active ingredients (e.g., octocrylene) can serve as photostabilizers" and expects avobenzone maximal-use studies to include such stabilizers, and a study developing a new photostabilizer compares its performance against "current avobenzone photostabilizers, namely octocrylene, ethylhexylmethoxycrylene and diethylhexylsyringylidenemalonate."[^fda2019][^chaudhuri]

**Systemic absorption.** Two randomized FDA maximal-use trials measured how much filter enters the bloodstream. In the first (24 volunteers, four products applied at 2 mg/cm2 to 75% of body surface four times daily for four days), octocrylene reached geometric mean maximum plasma concentrations of 2.9 ng/mL (coefficient of variation 102%) for one spray, 7.8 ng/mL (113.3%) for a second spray, 5.7 ng/mL (66.3%) for a lotion, and 5.7 ng/mL (47.1%) for a cream, and "systemic concentrations greater than 0.5 ng/mL were reached for all 4 products after 4 applications on day 1."[^matta2019] The second trial (48 participants, 44 completing) found octocrylene maximum plasma concentrations of 7.8 ng/mL (CV 87.1%) for the lotion and 6.6 ng/mL for two sprays, again above the 0.5 ng/mL threshold, which "was surpassed on day 1 after a single application for all active ingredients."[^matta2020] The 0.5 ng/mL figure is the level below which the FDA may waive some nonclinical safety testing; exceeding it triggers a request for further study, not a finding of harm, and the authors of both trials wrote that the results "do not indicate that individuals should refrain from the use of sunscreen."[^matta2019][^matta2020]

**Benzophenone generation.** An analysis assayed benzophenone in commercial sunscreens (nine from the EU and eight from the US, in triplicate). The sixteen recently purchased octocrylene-containing product lines "had an average concentration of 39 mg/kg benzophenone, ranging from 6 mg/kg to 186 mg/kg," while "benzophenone was not detectable in the product that did not contain octocrylene."[^downs] After the products were subjected to the FDA-accelerated stability aging protocol for six weeks, the octocrylene-containing products averaged 75 mg/kg (range 9.8 to 435 mg/kg), and the octocrylene-free product still showed none.[^downs] The authors attribute this to octocrylene degrading through a retro-aldol condensation, state that "in vivo, up to 70% of the benzophenone in these sunscreen products may be absorbed through the skin," and call benzophenone "a mutagen, carcinogen, and endocrine disruptor."[^downs] Those toxicological characterizations were disputed in three commentaries published in the same journal, and the EU's Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety, reviewing octocrylene after this work, concluded it "is safe as a UV-filter at concentrations up to 10 % in cosmetic products when used individually."[^downs][^eu2022]

**Photoallergic contact dermatitis.** A case series reported "50 cases of photoallergic contact dermatitis from octocrylene use and/or positive photopatch test reactions," concluding that octocrylene "appears to be a strong allergen leading to contact dermatitis in children and mostly photoallergic contact dermatitis in adults with an often-associated history of photoallergy from ketoprofen."[^avenel] A review of the same phenomenon found the reaction "is seen mainly in adult patients who have previously used topical products containing the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug ketoprofen," while plain contact allergy is "far less frequent, and is seen, in most cases, in children."[^degroot]

**Regulatory status.** In the US, octocrylene is a permitted OTC sunscreen active at up to 10%: the sunscreen monograph lists "Octocrylene up to 10 percent" among ingredients that absorb UV radiation "at wavelengths from 290 to 400 nanometers."[^cfr352] In its 2019 proposed rule the FDA placed octocrylene, with eleven other organic filters, in "Category III," finding that "the public record does not currently contain sufficient data to support" a positive safety-and-effectiveness determination and requesting more data, and noting that at the time these filters had not been studied in an adequate maximal-use trial, a gap the later plasma-absorption trials began to fill.[^fda2019][^matta2019] In the EU, octocrylene is permitted as a UV filter at a maximum of 10% in most products and 9% in propellant sprays, a limit set after the Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety judged it safe at up to 10%.[^eu2022]

## Uses

Well-supported: as a UVB and short-wave UVA-II filter in a broad-spectrum sunscreen, and as a photostabilizer that slows avobenzone's breakdown so the finished product holds its protection.[^degroot][^fda2019][^chaudhuri] The health benefit of that protection, slowing photoaging and lowering skin-cancer burden, is a property of regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use documented on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]], and octocrylene supplies part of the older US filter set that delivers it. Its practical limits are three: it covers little long-wave UVA I on its own, so it is not a standalone broad-spectrum filter; it is absorbed systemically above the FDA's testing threshold, which is why the agency has requested more safety data; and it is a recognized skin sensitizer that most often causes photoallergic contact dermatitis in adults with prior ketoprofen photoallergy.[^degroot][^avenel][^matta2019][^matta2020] It slowly degrades to benzophenone in the bottle, a finding whose toxicological weight is disputed and which the EU's safety committee reviewed before keeping octocrylene permitted at up to 10%.[^downs][^eu2022]

## Common Marketing Claims

This section is quarantined and discounted; treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Broad-spectrum / full UVA protection."** Octocrylene absorbs UVB and short-wave UVA II, roughly 290 to 340 nm, and covers little of the long-wave UVA I above 340 nm that drives photoaging.[^degroot] A product earns broad-spectrum coverage from its UVA filters (such as avobenzone or [[zinc-oxide]]), which octocrylene helps keep stable rather than replaces; the US "Broad Spectrum" label is a single low-bar threshold, not a graded UVA strength, and that landscape is covered on [[sunscreen-uv-filters]].[^fda2019]
- **"Photostable formula."** Octocrylene does help stabilize avobenzone, a role the FDA and formulation studies confirm.[^fda2019][^chaudhuri] The same stability comes with a slow side reaction: octocrylene degrades to benzophenone, which accumulated in every octocrylene-containing sunscreen one analysis tested.[^downs]
- **"Reef-safe / clean sunscreen."** These terms are unregulated. Octocrylene generates benzophenone over time, and its authors flagged it for regulatory review; that toxicological characterization is contested, and the EU's safety committee still judged octocrylene safe as a filter at up to 10%.[^downs][^eu2022] No clean-versus-not verdict follows from that record on its own.
- **"Chemical sunscreens are toxic / hormone disruptors."** The demonstrated fact is systemic absorption above the FDA's 0.5 ng/mL testing threshold, shown in two randomized trials.[^matta2019][^matta2020] The endocrine concern attaches specifically to benzophenone, an impurity octocrylene generates as it degrades, and even that "endocrine disruptor" characterization is disputed in the same journal.[^downs] Exceeding that threshold triggers a request for data, not a finding of harm; the FDA classified octocrylene Category III (insufficient data), the EU judged it safe up to 10%, and the trial authors stated the results "do not indicate that individuals should refrain from the use of sunscreen."[^fda2019][^eu2022][^matta2019]
