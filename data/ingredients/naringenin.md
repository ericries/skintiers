Naringenin is a flavanone antioxidant, a compound described in a 2023 pharmacology review as "a commonly consumed flavonoid found in many plants" and best known from citrus fruit and grapefruit; it appears in skincare marketing (for example [[prequel-skin-utility-gel]]) as a soothing, anti-redness ingredient.[^cai] The evidence for that use is preclinical: cell and mouse studies of UVB-induced inflammation and an atopic-dermatitis mouse model, with no human trial located.

> Naringenin has plausible anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity against UVB damage in cell and mouse models, but a 2023 pharmacology review notes "the absence of adequate clinical data" and that "poor solubility and stability severely restrict its usage as a medicinal agent," and no human skin trial was located for this profile.[^cai][^martinez][^sajeeda]

## The Rubric

**UVB-induced redness and inflammation (topical, animal/cell models).** This is naringenin's best-supported skincare-relevant use, and it is still entirely preclinical. A topical naringenin formulation applied to UVB-irradiated hairless mice inhibited UVB-induced edema, reduced production of the inflammatory cytokines TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, and IL-6, lowered markers of oxidative stress, and preserved antioxidant enzyme activity, compared with untreated irradiated skin.[^martinez] Separately, in UVB-exposed human dermal fibroblasts and in mice, naringenin (isolated from sea buckthorn pulp) reduced UVB-triggered inflammatory signaling (NF-kB activation) and mitochondrial damage, and topical application in mice reduced neutrophil infiltration and epidermal thickening after UVB exposure.[^sajeeda] Both studies are from cell and mouse models, not people, and neither measures a clinical redness or erythema endpoint in human skin.

**Atopic dermatitis and allergic skin inflammation (systemic, animal model).** In NC/Nga mice with dermatitis induced by repeated DNFB skin challenge, naringenin given by intraperitoneal injection (not applied to the skin) reduced ear swelling and skin lesion severity, lowered interferon-gamma production by CD4+ T cells and serum IgE, and reduced eosinophil, mast cell, and T-cell infiltration in the skin.[^kim] This shows systemic anti-inflammatory activity in an allergic-dermatitis mouse model; it does not establish that a topical cosmetic product delivers the same effect, since the dosing route was injection rather than skin application.

**General antioxidant and anti-inflammatory claims.** Naringenin has broad anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity across preclinical models, per a 2023 pharmacology review, but the same review states plainly that clinical data are lacking and that the compound's own poor solubility and stability limit its usefulness as delivered.[^cai] No controlled human trial of a naringenin-containing skincare product was located for this profile; [[prequel-skin-utility-gel]] markets naringenin on a "clinically tested" redness claim that, per that page, rests only on the manufacturer's own unsourced statement, not an independent study.

## The Evidence

**UVB skin inflammation, topical, mouse model.** Martinez and colleagues formulated naringenin into a topical gel and tested it on hairless mice exposed to UVB radiation. The treated formulation was "protected from UVB-induced skin damage by inhibiting edema and cytokine production," specifically TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6, and IL-10 (the last is itself an anti-inflammatory cytokine, so its reduction is not necessarily a marker of harm), while also reducing superoxide anion and lipid hydroperoxide production and preserving catalase activity and glutathione peroxidase 1 and Nrf2 expression, compared with irradiated untreated skin.[^martinez] The authors concluded a naringenin formulation "may be a promising approach to protecting the skin from the deleterious effects of UVB irradiation," a preclinical, mouse-only conclusion.[^martinez]

**UVB skin damage, cell and mouse model.** Sajeeda and colleagues tested naringenin isolated from sea buckthorn pulp in UVB-exposed primary human dermal fibroblasts and in Balb/c mice. In fibroblasts, naringenin "restored the UV-B-induced imbalance in mitochondrial fission and fusion" and "inhibited the phosphorylation of NFkB and reduced the generation of pro-inflammatory cytokines." In mice, topical naringenin "prevented mitochondrial fragmentation and progression of inflammatory responses" and "prevented neutrophil infiltration and epidermal thickening" after UVB exposure.[^sajeeda] This combines a human cell line with an animal model; it is not a human clinical trial.

**Atopic dermatitis, mouse model, systemic dosing.** Kim and colleagues induced dermatitis-like lesions in NC/Nga mice with repeated DNFB skin challenge, then treated with naringenin by intraperitoneal injection for one week. Naringenin "significantly lowered ear swelling and improved back skin lesions," "significantly suppressed production of interferon-gamma (IFN-gamma) by activated CD4+ T cells and serum IgE level," and "reduced DNFB-induced infiltration of eosinophils, mast cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells in skin lesions."[^kim] The dosing route was injection, not topical application, which limits how directly this supports a skincare product.

**Clinical-data gap and delivery limits.** A 2023 review of naringenin's anti-inflammatory and anti-infective pharmacology concluded that "the absence of adequate clinical data and naringenin's poor solubility and stability severely restrict its usage as a medicinal agent," and framed most of the supporting literature as preclinical work aimed at improving delivery.[^cai] Consistent with that, other located naringenin-and-skin papers were formulation studies (liposomes, nanoemulsions, microemulsions) built specifically to work around its poor skin penetration, rather than efficacy trials in people.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Clinically tested to reduce redness and dehydration."** [[prequel-skin-utility-gel]] uses this language for its naringenin-containing formula, with no named study, testing facility, or independent corroboration located for that claim. The closest supporting data are the mouse and cell studies above, none of which tested a finished skincare formula on human skin.
- **"Soothing" as a settled, human-tested effect.** The anti-inflammatory activity behind this framing comes from UVB mouse/cell models and a systemic (injected) mouse model of atopic dermatitis, not a human study of topical redness or irritation.[^martinez][^sajeeda][^kim] [[centella-asiatica]] is a topical soothing ingredient with plausible mechanistic and animal evidence and a human trial record still limited to a handful of small studies, a similar evidence pattern to naringenin's.
