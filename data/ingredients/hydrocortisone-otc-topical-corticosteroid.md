Hydrocortisone is a corticosteroid sold without a prescription at 1% (and, in prescription-only strength, 2.5%) as a cream, ointment, lotion, or spray, labeled under the FDA's OTC Drug Facts framework for the "temporary relief of itching associated with minor skin irritations, inflammation, and rashes" from causes such as eczema, psoriasis, poison ivy/oak/sumac, insect bites, and contact with soaps, detergents, jewelry, or cosmetics.[^cortizone][^kroger] It is the weakest of the seven potency classes used to rank US topical corticosteroids, with 1% and 2.5% hydrocortisone both listed as Class VII, the least-potent tier.[^statpearls]

> A randomized trial in children with moderate atopic-dermatitis flare-ups found that starting on a potent prescription steroid (fluticasone propionate 0.05%) produced significantly better eczema-symptom scores over 24 weeks than starting on hydrocortisone acetate 1%, the mild comparator in the trial.[^vanhalewijn] Hydrocortisone is a real, class-representative anti-inflammatory corticosteroid, but at OTC strength it is the mildest option in its own drug class.

## The Rubric

**Mild-to-moderate itch and inflammation (topical, OTC, 1%)**
- **Effect size: real but the weakest of the corticosteroid class.** Hydrocortisone shares the anti-inflammatory mechanism of every topical corticosteroid, working through vasoconstriction, inhibition of phospholipase A2, and suppression of pro-inflammatory gene transcription, but 1% and 2.5% hydrocortisone sit in Class VII, the least-potent of the US Food and Drug Administration's seven-class potency ranking, which runs from Class I (superpotent, e.g. clobetasol propionate 0.05%) to Class VII (least potent).[^statpearls] In a randomized trial of children with moderate atopic-dermatitis flare-ups, starting treatment on hydrocortisone acetate 1% (the trial's mild comparator) produced significantly worse eczema-symptom (POEM) scores at 1, 4, and 24 weeks than starting on the potent steroid fluticasone propionate 0.05% (24-week POEM 9.4 vs 3.3, P=0.023).[^vanhalewijn] "Hydrocortisone works through the same anti-inflammatory mechanism as every topical corticosteroid, but at OTC strength it is the mildest tier of that mechanism, and a randomized trial in children found it controlled a moderate eczema flare-up less well than a stronger prescription steroid."
- **Evidence quality: solid for the drug class, thin for hydrocortisone specifically at OTC strength.** The corticosteroid mechanism and the seven-class US potency ranking rest on the vasoconstrictor assay, a standard pharmacology method.[^statpearls] The one randomized trial identified here that isolates OTC-strength hydrocortisone against an active comparator is small (32 randomized children of a 209-child cohort) and was designed to test whether starting stronger beats starting mild, not to establish hydrocortisone's effect against placebo.[^vanhalewijn]

## The Evidence

**FDA Drug Facts labeling.** Hydrocortisone 1% is labeled, under DailyMed structured product labels for marketed OTC products such as Cortizone-10 and store-brand hydrocortisone creams, with the purpose "Anti-itch" and the use "temporarily relieves itching associated with minor skin irritations, inflammation, and rashes due to: eczema, psoriasis, poison ivy, oak, sumac, insect bites, detergents, jewelry, cosmetics, soaps, seborrheic dermatitis," with directions capping use at 3 to 4 times daily and a warning to stop and consult a doctor if symptoms persist beyond 7 days or recur.[^cortizone][^kroger] Some labeled products also cover external anal and genital itching at the same 1% strength.[^kroger] Hydrocortisone above 1% (for example 2.5% strength Proctozone-HC) is marketed prescription-only.[^proctozone]

**Potency versus a stronger steroid, in a randomized trial.** van Halewijn and colleagues (BMJ Open, 2024) embedded a pragmatic, open-label randomized controlled trial in a Dutch primary-care cohort of 209 children with atopic dermatitis. When a child's eczema flared to a moderate severity, 32 were randomized to either a potent topical corticosteroid (fluticasone propionate 0.05%, Class III potency) or a mild one (hydrocortisone acetate 1%, the trial's Class I/mild comparator). Over 24 weeks, the primary outcome (Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure, POEM) favored the potent steroid: 3.3 versus 9.4 (P=0.023), with the potent steroid also ahead at 1 week (5.5 vs 12.0, P=0.042) and 4 weeks (4.3 vs 12.7, P=0.030), and quality-of-life scores significantly better with the potent steroid at 4 and 24 weeks.[^vanhalewijn] The authors concluded the data "suggests a clinical benefit from starting with a potent TCS rather than a mild TCS when a flare-up of AD is moderate," while cautioning the sample was small.[^vanhalewijn]

## How It Works

Topical corticosteroids, hydrocortisone included, act through combined anti-inflammatory, anti-mitotic, and immunosuppressive mechanisms: vasoconstriction in the upper dermis (reducing delivery of inflammatory mediators), synthesis of lipocortin (which inhibits phospholipase A2 and lowers prostaglandin and leukotriene production), direct upregulation of anti-inflammatory genes and suppression of pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as NF-kB, and broad suppression of immune-cell maturation and proliferation.[^statpearls] Potency across the corticosteroid class is measured by the vasoconstrictor assay and ranked into seven US classes; hydrocortisone at 1% and 2.5% strength sits in Class VII, the bottom of that ranking, versus superpotent Class I agents like clobetasol propionate 0.05%.[^statpearls]

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Fast, effective relief" implying it works as well as any topical steroid.** Hydrocortisone shares the corticosteroid mechanism, but it is specifically the least-potent class of that mechanism, and the one randomized trial identified here that pitted OTC-strength hydrocortisone against a stronger steroid for a moderate flare-up found the stronger steroid controlled symptoms significantly better.[^statpearls][^vanhalewijn]
