Squalane is the fully saturated, hydrogenated form of squalene, a hydrocarbon that makes up about 13% of human sebum.[^kim] Because it is chemically identical in structure to a lipid the skin already produces, minus the carbon-carbon double bonds that squalene carries, it is used almost entirely as an emollient and occlusive moisturizing ingredient rather than a treatment active, and the Cosmetic Ingredient Review's Expert Panel has concluded that "Squalane and Squalene are safe as cosmetic ingredients in the practices of use and concentration as described in this report."[^cir]

> Squalane's evidence base is a formulation-chemistry case, not a clinical-trial one: it is a well-tolerated, skin-identical emollient with a favorable safety review, its saturation gives it a mechanistic edge over squalene against the oxidation that drives comedone formation, and small in-vitro cell studies report an antioxidant signal at very low concentrations, but no controlled trial isolates squalane's effect on human skin.[^kim][^cir][^motoyoshi][^wolosik-apoptosis]

## The Rubric

**Emollience and occlusive moisturizing (topical)**
- **Effect size: not independently quantified, but its category role is well established.** Squalene, the parent lipid, is grouped with petrolatum, mineral oil, and paraffin as an occlusive hydrocarbon that reduces water loss from skin.[^statpearls] Kim and Karadeniz describe "skin hydrating, and emollient activities" reported for squalene and squalane in animal and in-vitro work.[^kim] This is the general moisturizing-ingredient benefit covered on [[moisturizing]]; no trial isolates squalane's water-loss or hydration effect against a comparator emollient.
- **Evidence quality: category-level, not ingredient-specific.** The occlusive/emollient classification comes from an authoritative review and a food-and-nutrition-research review chapter, not a controlled human trial of squalane alone.[^statpearls][^kim]

**Tolerability and comedogenicity (topical)**
- **Effect size: favorable, on indirect evidence.** A classic rabbit-ear comedogenicity study found that squalene itself was "scarcely comedogenic" while its oxidized peroxides were "highly comedogenic," with a close correlation between lipid peroxide levels and comedone size; squalane was tested alongside squalene, oleic acid, and liquid paraffin in the same design.[^motoyoshi] Because squalane is fully saturated, it lacks the double bonds squalene needs to form those peroxides, which is the chemical rationale for using the hydrogenated form. The CIR panel's 2023 safety review, updating its original 1982 assessment, confirmed squalane and squalene safe in current cosmetic use concentrations.[^cir]
- **Evidence quality: indirect and dated for comedogenicity; solid for general safety.** The comedogenicity data point is a single 1983 rabbit-model study rather than a human acne trial;[^motoyoshi] the safety conclusion is a formal expert-panel review of the accumulated toxicology and use data.[^cir]

**Antioxidant/UVA-protective signal in cultured skin cells (topical, preliminary)**
- **Effect size: preliminary, cell-culture only.** Two 2025 papers from the same research group applied plant-derived squalane to human dermal fibroblasts at 0.005% to 0.015% before UVA exposure. One reported squalane "counteracted UVA-induced ROS formation, decreased the level of reduced thiol groups, activated apoptosis, and inhibited DNA biosynthesis," attributing these effects to "a combination of antioxidant and anti-apoptotic mechanisms," and suppressed p53, caspase-3, caspase-9, and PARP expression while restoring pro-survival p-Akt/mTOR signaling.[^wolosik-apoptosis] The companion paper reported squalane counteracted UVA-induced inhibition of collagen biosynthesis and reduced inflammatory markers NF-kB and COX-2 in the same cell model.[^wolosik-collagen] "A real antioxidant/anti-apoptotic signal in cultured fibroblasts, at concentrations far below what a squalane facial oil or serum applies to skin, with no human or whole-skin data yet."
- **Evidence quality: preliminary.** Both papers are in-vitro human dermal fibroblast studies from one laboratory, published the same year in the same concentration range; neither is a clinical or whole-skin trial, so the topical antioxidant marketing claim for squalane rests on mechanism, not demonstrated skin outcome.[^wolosik-apoptosis][^wolosik-collagen]

## The Evidence

**Chemistry and natural presence.** Kim and Karadeniz's review describes squalene as "a polyunsaturated hydrocarbon" found in shark liver oil and some vegetable oils, and notes "human sebum also contains 13% squalene as one of its major constituents. Squalane is a saturated derivative of squalene and also found in these sources."[^kim] The review reports that "emollient activities of these substances have been reported both in animal models and in vitro environments," alongside other claimed bioactivities it lists as still emerging.[^kim] StatPearls' moisturizer review groups squalene among occlusive hydrocarbons alongside petrolatum, mineral oil, and paraffin.[^statpearls]

**Safety review.** The Cosmetic Ingredient Review's Expert Panel for Cosmetic Ingredient Safety re-reviewed squalane and squalene, updating its original 1982 assessment with newer studies and current use concentrations, and "confirmed that Squalane and Squalene are safe as cosmetic ingredients in the practices of use and concentration as described in this report."[^cir]

**Comedogenicity.** Motoyoshi's 1983 rabbit-ear study evaluated squalene, oleic acid, tetradecane, isopropyl myristate, squalane, and liquid paraffin, both irradiated and non-irradiated, for comedo formation and lipid peroxide levels. It found "squalene itself was scarcely comedogenic but squalene peroxides were highly comedogenic," with "a good correlation between the lipid peroxide levels and the size of the comedones," and concluded that sebum lipids "may be less comedogenic than their peroxides."[^motoyoshi] Squalane was included in the tested panel; because it has no double bonds to peroxidize, the mechanism the paper describes for squalene's oxidized comedogenicity does not apply to it in the same way.

**Cell-culture UVA protection.** Wolosik and colleagues applied plant-derived squalane (0.005% to 0.015%) to human dermal fibroblasts before UVA exposure (10 J/cm2). Squalane "counteracted UVA-induced ROS formation, decreased the level of reduced thiol groups, activated apoptosis, and inhibited DNA biosynthesis" relative to UVA-only controls, acting "through a combination of antioxidant and anti-apoptotic mechanisms."[^wolosik-apoptosis] A companion paper from the same group reported squalane protected against UVA-induced inhibition of collagen biosynthesis and reduced fibroblast expression of the inflammatory markers NF-kB and COX-2, and improved fibroblast migration in a wound-healing assay.[^wolosik-collagen] Both are cell-culture studies at concentrations well below typical leave-on squalane products; neither tests intact human skin.

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Squalane is a powerful antioxidant."** Squalane is fully saturated, unlike squalene, so it does not scavenge free radicals through the double-bond chemistry classic antioxidants use. Two 2025 in-vitro fibroblast studies from one lab found an antioxidant/anti-apoptotic signal at low concentrations,[^wolosik-apoptosis][^wolosik-collagen] but that is cell-culture evidence from a single research group, not a controlled trial showing a topical product delivers an antioxidant benefit to human skin.
- **"Squalane clogs pores."** The comedogenicity data point the opposite direction: squalene, squalane's parent lipid, was "scarcely comedogenic" in the rabbit-ear model, and it is oxidized peroxides, not the intact saturated hydrocarbon, that drove comedone formation in that study.[^motoyoshi]
