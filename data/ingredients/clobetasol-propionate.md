Clobetasol propionate 0.05% sits at the top of the topical steroid potency ladder. Its FDA label calls it "super-high potency" and says studies place it "in the super-high range of potency as compared with other topical corticosteroids".[^label] It is prescription-only, and it is the steroid most of the warnings you have heard about are really about. The class mechanism and the general case for topical steroids are on [[topical-corticosteroids]].

> **The warnings are real, and the label attaches them to duration and quantity rather than to use at all.** Three hard limits are printed on it: no more than two consecutive weeks, no more than 50 g in a week, and no occlusive dressings.[^label] Against that, in the label's own controlled trials the most frequent local reactions were "burning sensation, irritation, and itching in 0.5% of treated patients".[^label] Skin atrophy appears on the label as a *less frequent* reaction, not the headline one. Short courses inside the limits are not what produced the horror stories; prolonged use is, and the label names Cushing's syndrome as the endpoint of that.

## The Rubric

**Inflammatory and itchy corticosteroid-responsive skin disease (topical, prescription)**

- **Effect size: strong.** The indication is broad by design, covering "the inflammatory and pruritic manifestations of corticosteroid-responsive dermatoses".[^label] The sharpest demonstration of how strong is in bullous pemphigoid, where a review describes a large randomized trial finding topical clobetasol "more effective than oral prednisone" and associated with "a significant decrease in severe complications and mortality of BP patients".[^review] A topical beating a systemic steroid on mortality is an unusual result and is the reason this molecule is first-line there; the appraisal and its caveats are on [[bullous-pemphigoid]].
- **Evidence quality: good.** FDA approval, a long clinical record and an indication written at class level. The limit on the grade is that the label's own efficacy evidence is not quantified in it, and the strongest specific result cited here comes to this site through a 2003 review rather than the trial itself.

## How It Works

The same way the rest of its class does, which [[topical-corticosteroids]] sets out and this page does not repeat. What distinguishes clobetasol is only the strength of that effect and therefore the size of the trade: the label notes that absorption rises when the skin barrier is broken. Its words: "Inflammation and/or other disease processes in the skin may increase percutaneous absorption."[^label] That is precisely the situation it is prescribed for.

## The Evidence

**What the label commits to.** Twice-daily application, with therapy stopped once control is achieved, and a reassessment of the diagnosis if nothing improves in two weeks.[^label] Not recommended under 12 years of age.[^label] The 50 g weekly ceiling exists for a stated reason: "the potential for the drug to suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis".[^label]

**What the trials reported.** Burning, irritation and itching in 0.5% of treated patients as the most frequent events. Then a longer list of less frequent ones including "stinging, cracking, erythema, folliculitis, numbness of fingers, skin atrophy, and telangiectasia".[^label]

**What prolonged use has caused.** The label is direct: "Cushing's syndrome has been reported in infants and adults as a result of prolonged use of topical clobetasol propionate formulations".[^label] It also lists reactions reported with topical steroids generally that "may occur more frequently with the use of occlusive dressings and higher potency corticosteroids", among them striae, hypopigmentation and perioral dermatitis.[^label]

That is the honest shape of the risk. The common fear is that a strong steroid thins skin on contact; the label's position is that the serious harms track prolonged use, occlusion and quantity, which is why it caps all three rather than warning against the drug.
