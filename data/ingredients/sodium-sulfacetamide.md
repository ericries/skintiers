Sodium sulfacetamide (chemically Acetamide N-[(4-aminophenyl) sulfonyl]-acetamide, monosodium salt, monohydrate, C8H9N2NaO3S H2O, molecular weight 254.24) is an older prescription sulfonamide antibiotic applied topically, most often as a 10% lotion, wash, or cleanser, and frequently paired with 5% [[sulfur]] in combination products.[^wash][^combo] The plain 10% lotion (brand Klaron) is "indicated in the topical treatment of acne vulgaris," a plain 10% wash formulation is instead indicated for "scaling dermatoses: seborrheic dermatitis and seborrhea sicca (dandruff)" rather than acne, and the sulfacetamide-sulfur combinations are "indicated in the topical control of acne vulgaris, acne rosacea and seborrheic dermatitis."[^klaron][^wash][^combo] It works by starving bacteria of folic acid, and it has been in dermatologic use since the mid-1950s, which is both its credential and its problem: the trial base is old, small, and thin by modern standards, and it is contraindicated in anyone with a sulfonamide (sulfa) allergy.[^wash][^delrosso][^klaron]

> Sodium sulfacetamide is a real drug with a real mechanism, but its modern evidence is threadbare. The plain 10% lotion has no retrievable vehicle-controlled trial program behind its acne indication, and the one respectable randomized trial in rosacea tested the 10% sulfacetamide plus 5% sulfur combination, so the benefit cannot be attributed to sulfacetamide alone.[^torok][^delrosso] The 2015 Cochrane review of rosacea treatments reserved its evidence-backed endorsements for [[metronidazole]], [[azelaic-acid]], [[ivermectin-topical]], and [[brimonidine]]; sulfacetamide is not among them.[^cochrane] Treat it as a second-line option behind the modern first-line actives, and avoid it entirely if you have a sulfa allergy.[^klaron]

## The Rubric

**Acne vulgaris (10% lotion, monotherapy)**
- **Effect size: not quantifiable from modern controlled data.** The Klaron label states the indication ("Klaron Lotion is indicated in the topical treatment of acne vulgaris") but its approval rests on an older regulatory pathway; the label reports pharmacokinetics, not efficacy trial results.[^klaron] The most-cited dedicated acne study is a 1993 report on a sulfacetamide/sulfur lotion in women, a small single publication with no indexed abstract, and a 2009 review of the 10%/5% emollient foam could offer only an uncontrolled case series as its clinical data.[^breneman][^delrosso]
- **Evidence quality: minimal and dated.** No randomized vehicle-controlled trials of 10% sodium sulfacetamide monotherapy for acne are retrievable on PubMed. That is a much weaker foundation than [[benzoyl-peroxide]], [[adapalene]], or even [[clindamycin-topical]], each of which has large controlled trial programs.

**Papulopustular rosacea and seborrheic dermatitis (10% sulfacetamide + 5% sulfur combinations)**
- **Effect size: modest, and belongs to the combination, not to sulfacetamide alone.** In a 12-week investigator-blinded, randomized, parallel-group trial at 6 sites, sodium sulfacetamide 10%/sulfur 5% cream with sunscreens (n=75) reduced inflammatory rosacea lesions more than metronidazole 0.75% cream (n=77): 80% versus 72% (P=.04), with more subjects showing improved erythema (69% vs 45%; P=.0007) and global-improvement success (79% vs 59%; P=.01).[^torok] Tolerability ran the other way: good-or-excellent tolerance in 85% versus 97% for metronidazole, with 7 subjects showing poor tolerance "possibly caused by a sulfa drug allergy."[^torok]
- **Evidence quality: one comparator trial, no vehicle arm, no attribution.** The Torok trial had no vehicle control and tested a two-active product, so it cannot isolate what sulfacetamide contributes over sulfur (or over the vehicle and sunscreens). The 2015 Cochrane review, which pooled 106 rosacea trials, found usable pooled efficacy data only for metronidazole, azelaic acid, ivermectin, and brimonidine among topicals.[^cochrane] For seborrheic dermatitis the labeled indication exists, but controlled trial support is similarly historical.[^wash][^delrosso]

**Safety (topical)**
- **Systemic absorption is low but real, and the sulfa-allergy contraindication is absolute.** In the Klaron pharmacokinetic study, "the percentage of the applied dose of Klaron Lotion excreted in the urine as sulfacetamide plus sulfanilamide ranged from 0.08 to 0.33%."[^klaron] Every label carries the same contraindication: known hypersensitivity to sulfonamides (the combinations add sulfur hypersensitivity, and state the product "is not to be used by patients with kidney disease").[^klaron][^combo] The warnings are not theoretical: "Fatalities have occurred, although rarely, due to severe reactions to sulfonamides including Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, fulminant hepatic necrosis, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, and other blood dyscrasias."[^klaron] Stevens-Johnson syndrome and cases of drug-induced systemic lupus erythematosus (one fatal) have also been reported following topical sodium sulfacetamide use specifically.[^wash] Klaron also contains sodium metabisulfite, a sulfite that can trigger allergic-type reactions in susceptible people.[^klaron]

## How It Works

Sodium sulfacetamide is a sulfonamide: it "exerts a bacteriostatic effect against sulfonamide sensitive Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms" and "acts by restricting the synthesis of folic acid required by bacteria for growth, by its competition with para-aminobenzoic acid."[^wash] Bacteria, unlike human cells, must synthesize their own folate, so blocking the para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) step halts their growth; in acne and rosacea the presumed target is Cutibacterium acnes and other skin flora, and in the sulfur combinations the sulfur adds keratolytic and antibacterial activity of its own (covered on the [[sulfur]] page).[^wash][^delrosso] Per its labels it is bacteriostatic (stops growth) rather than bactericidal, and susceptible organisms include Streptococci, Staphylococci, and E. coli among others.[^wash]

## The Evidence

**Combination cream versus metronidazole for rosacea (topical).** Torok et al. compared sodium sulfacetamide 10% and sulfur 5% cream with sunscreens (n=75) against metronidazole 0.75% cream (n=77) in a 12-week investigator-blinded, randomized, parallel-group study at 6 sites. The combination produced a greater reduction in inflammatory lesions (80% vs 72%, P=.04), more subjects with improved erythema (69% vs 45%, P=.0007), and more global-improvement successes (79% vs 59%, P=.01), with no significant difference in investigator global severity; tolerance was good or excellent in 85% versus 97%, and 7 subjects had poor tolerance to the combination, "possibly caused by a sulfa drug allergy."[^torok] *The single solid randomized trial in this drug's file, and it tests the sulfur combination against an active comparator with no vehicle arm, so it establishes the combination as roughly metronidazole-class for rosacea rather than proving anything about sulfacetamide alone.*

**Historical use and the foam case series (topical).** Del Rosso's 2009 review records that sodium sulfacetamide 10%-sulfur 5% "has been used for the topical treatment of seborrheic dermatitis, acne vulgaris, and rosacea since the mid-1950s and is available in a variety of formulations, including lotions, creams, cleansers, and emollient foams," and the new clinical data the review adds for acne is a case report series of foam-treated patients, not a controlled trial.[^delrosso] *Seventy years of use with a case series as the featured evidence is the profile of a legacy drug, not an active research program.*

**The dated acne literature (topical).** The most commonly cited dedicated acne publication is Breneman and Ariano's 1993 report on a sodium sulfacetamide/sulfur lotion in women with acne vulgaris (Int J Dermatol), a small early-1990s study with no abstract indexed on PubMed.[^breneman] *Cited here as a marker of how thin and old the acne trial base is, not as support for efficacy.*

**What the systematic reviewers found (rosacea).** The 2015 Cochrane review of interventions for rosacea included 106 studies (13,631 participants) and could pool usable efficacy data among topicals only for metronidazole and azelaic acid (both better than placebo), plus high-quality single-trial evidence for ivermectin and brimonidine.[^cochrane] *Sodium sulfacetamide does not appear among the topicals the review's main results endorse, which is the fairest one-line summary of where it stands in the modern evidence hierarchy.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Treats acne, rosacea, and seborrheic dermatitis in one product."** The labeled indications do span all three for the sulfur combinations ("the topical control of acne vulgaris, acne rosacea and seborrheic dermatitis"), so the claim is technically accurate.[^combo] But an indication is not an evidence grade: the controlled-trial support behind each of those three uses is a single comparator trial (rosacea) and historical usage (acne, seborrheic dermatitis).[^torok][^delrosso]
- **"A time-tested classic dermatologists have trusted since the 1950s."** True as history and empty as evidence.[^delrosso] The actives that displaced it (benzoyl peroxide, retinoids, azelaic acid for acne; ivermectin, azelaic acid, metronidazole for rosacea) earned first-line status through vehicle-controlled trial programs sodium sulfacetamide never ran.[^cochrane]
