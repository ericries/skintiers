Tea tree oil is the essential oil of the Australian tree *Melaleuca alternifolia*, and it is the one complementary acne ingredient with a real randomised result behind it. **Bottom line:** that result is a single 60-person trial, Cochrane graded it low quality, and almost nothing you can buy contains the 5% gel that was tested. Graded `weak` because the evidence is thin, not because the effect looked small.

> **One trial is the whole evidence base.** A 2015 Cochrane review of complementary therapies for acne included 35 studies and 3227 participants, but could pool only four of them. On tea tree oil it reports that "Data from a single trial showed potential benefit of tea tree oil compared with placebo in improving total skin lesion counts (MD -7.53, 95% CI -10.40 to -4.66, 60 participants, 1 trial, low quality of evidence) and acne severity scores (MD -5.75, 95% CI -9.51 to -1.99, 60 participants, 1 trial)."[^cochrane]

## The Rubric

**Reducing acne lesions**
Graded modest on preliminary evidence. The single trial is Enshaieh 2007, "a randomized double-blind clinical trial performed in 60 patients with mild to moderate acne vulgaris", 30 on 5% tea tree oil gel and 30 on placebo, followed every 15 days for 45 days.[^enshaieh] Its own summary of the size of the effect is "tea tree oil gel was 3.55 times and 5.75 times more effective than placebo" on total lesion count and acne severity index respectively.[^enshaieh] Note that the trial's "5.75 times" is a ratio of improvements while Cochrane's -5.75 is a mean difference on the severity score; they share digits and are not the same quantity, and this page does not try to reconcile them.[^cochrane][^enshaieh]

**Being gentler than benzoyl peroxide**
Graded modest on preliminary evidence. Bassett 1990 ran "a single-blind, randomised clinical trial on 124 patients" of 5% tea tree oil gel against 5% benzoyl peroxide lotion in mild to moderate acne.[^bassett] Both worked, "although the onset of action in the case of tea-tree oil was slower", and "fewer side effects were experienced by patients treated with tea-tree oil".[^bassett] Three limits: it was single-blind, there was no placebo arm, and the abstract reports no lesion counts, percentages or p-values, so the tolerability advantage is a reported direction rather than a measured quantity. [[benzoyl-peroxide]] carries far more evidence for the effect itself.

## The Evidence

**The gap between the trials and the shelf.** Both trials tested a 5% tea tree oil gel.[^enshaieh][^bassett] Products on this site that actually declare *Melaleuca alternifolia* in their ingredient list, including [[banila-co-clean-it-zero-pore-clarifying-cleansing-balm]] and [[cosrx-low-ph-good-morning-gel-cleanser]], carry it as an extract low in the list, in cleansers that are rinsed off within a minute. Nothing about the 60-person result transfers to that. A trial of a leave-on 5% gel does not grade a wash.

**Cochrane could not pool it with anything.** The review's constraint is stated plainly: "Because of the clinical heterogeneity between trials and the incomplete data reporting, we could only include four trials in two meta-analyses, with two trials in each meta-analysis."[^cochrane] Tea tree oil was not among them. Its verdict is deliberately hedged: "There is some low-quality evidence from single trials that LGLD, tea tree oil, and bee venom may reduce total skin lesions in acne vulgaris".[^cochrane] Being grouped with bee venom is a fair summary of where this sits.

**Side effects were reported, not absent.** The same review records that "specific included trials reported mild adverse effects from herbal medicines, wet-cupping therapy, and tea tree oil gel", while noting no severe adverse events across the 26 of 35 studies that reported on harms.[^cochrane] Separately, a 2024 product analysis in *Contact Dermatitis* lists tea tree oil among the most common contact allergens it identified in the chemical hair relaxers it examined, checked against the American Contact Dermatitis Society core allergen series.[^allergen] That is a statement about which allergens appear in those products, not a rate of reaction, and no prevalence figure for allergic contact dermatitis from tea tree oil was found in the sources consulted here.

**Medical note.** This is educational content, not a substitute for a clinician. Essential oils are applied undiluted by some people on the internet; the trials behind this page used a 5% gel,[^enshaieh][^bassett] and acne that is scarring or not responding is worth taking to a dermatologist rather than escalating the concentration. The graded options for acne are on [[acne]].

## Common Marketing Claims

These are the recurring claim patterns for tea tree oil rather than quotations from any one brand, since no brand page was fetched for this ingredient.

**Clinically proven to treat acne.** The common framing, and overstated in the singular. One 60-person placebo-controlled trial and one 124-person single-blind comparator trial exist, and the relevant Cochrane review calls the evidence low quality.[^cochrane][^enshaieh][^bassett]

**A natural alternative to benzoyl peroxide.** The one head-to-head found tea tree oil slower to act than benzoyl peroxide, with fewer side effects, and published no numbers for either.[^bassett] Treating that as equivalence runs past what the trial reported.

**Gentle because it is natural.** The review that provides this ingredient's best evidence also records mild adverse effects from tea tree oil gel in its included trials.[^cochrane]
