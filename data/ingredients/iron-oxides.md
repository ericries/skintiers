Iron oxides (INCI CI 77491 red, CI 77492 yellow, CI 77499 black; chemically red is ferric oxide Fe2O3 and black is Fe3O4) are mineral pigments blended into tinted mineral [[sunscreen-uv-filters]] at roughly 1% to 5% to match skin tone and reduce the white cast of zinc oxide and titanium dioxide.[^pubchem-fe2o3][^pubchem-fe3o4][^lyons] Beyond cosmetics, iron oxides physically absorb visible light (400 to 700 nm), a wavelength band ordinary UV-only sunscreens do not block, and the one randomized trial isolating this in melasma patients found an iron-oxide-containing sunscreen modestly outperformed a UV-only sunscreen as an adjunct to hydroquinone.[^castanedo][^lyons]

> In a double-blind randomized trial in 68 melasma patients, adding an iron-oxide-containing UV plus visible-light sunscreen to 4% hydroquinone gave "15%, 28% and 4% greater improvements than the UV-only group in MASI scores, colorimetric values and melanin assessments, respectively" after 8 weeks.[^castanedo]

## The Rubric

**Adjunct visible-light protection for melasma and pigment-prone skin (tinted sunscreen)**
- **Effect size: modest, and only as an add-on to standard treatment, not a standalone fix.** The one randomized trial isolating iron-oxide-tinted sunscreen in melasma found it added a 15% greater improvement in MASI score, 28% in colorimetry, and 4% in histologic melanin versus a UV-only sunscreen, both arms also using 4% hydroquinone for 8 weeks.[^castanedo] These are incremental gains on top of an active depigmenting agent, not a replacement for one, and the trial did not report p-values for each individual outcome in the abstract.
- **Evidence quality: thin, one clinical trial plus consistent but largely industry-funded mechanistic and photoprotection data.** The melasma outcome rests on a single randomized trial (68 randomized, 61 completing) from one Mexican research group; a 2024 controlled irradiation study from an independent academic photomedicine unit found a titanium-dioxide-11%/iron-oxide-1% tinted product gave the most consistent protection against combined UVA1 plus visible-light-induced pigmentation, but paradoxically the 1%-iron-oxide product outperformed a 4%-iron-oxide product, so the authors concluded "further studies are needed to evaluate whether iron oxide content correlates with better protection."[^castanedo][^ezekwe] A separate industry study in Fitzpatrick IV subjects found iron-oxide-containing foundations "significantly protected against visible light-induced pigmentation compared to untreated skin or mineral SPF 50+ sunscreen," supporting the same direction but from a manufacturer-style product-efficacy test with no independent replication.[^dumbuya] No trial has tested iron oxide as a standalone (non-hydroquinone-adjunct) melasma treatment.

## How It Works

Iron oxides are opaque inorganic pigments; unlike the UV filters zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, which are engineered as nanoparticles to stay UV-active while remaining cosmetically transparent, iron oxide pigment particles are visible on skin, and that visibility is the mechanism: a formulation must have color on the skin to absorb and scatter visible light.[^lyons] Visible light (400 to 700 nm) makes up close to half of solar radiation reaching skin and, unlike UV, is not blocked by standard broad-spectrum sunscreens; in individuals with more melanin (Fitzpatrick III and above), visible light exposure triggers pigment darkening and can worsen melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.[^dumbuya][^lyons] An industry in vitro/in vivo study on a 4.5% yellow iron oxide (CI 77492) formulation measured an in vitro visible-light protection factor of 2.5, closely matched by an in vivo protection factor of 3.0 in 10 volunteers with Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin, and reported that "multiple daily exposures of visible light have shown increase in skin pigmentation and the application of YIO provide less development of pigmentation when compared to unprotected skin."[^ruvolo]

## The Evidence

**Iron-oxide (UV-VL) sunscreen versus UV-only sunscreen, adjunct to hydroquinone, melasma (topical, RCT).** A double-blind randomized trial enrolled 68 melasma patients into two groups, one applying a broad-spectrum SPF 50-plus sunscreen containing iron oxide as a visible-light absorbing pigment (UV-VL) and the other a regular UV-only broad-spectrum sunscreen of the same SPF, both groups also using 4% hydroquinone, for 8 weeks; 61 patients completed. At 8 weeks, "the UV-VL group showed 15%, 28% and 4% greater improvements than the UV-only group in MASI scores, colorimetric values and melanin assessments, respectively," and the authors concluded the findings "suggest a role for VL in melasma pathogenesis."[^castanedo] *A randomized head-to-head trial found iron-oxide-tinted sunscreen gave a real but modest edge over plain UV sunscreen when both groups were also using hydroquinone; it does not show iron oxide works without an active depigmenting agent alongside it.*

**Sunscreen photoprotection against combined UVA1 plus visible light (topical, controlled irradiation study).** An academic photomedicine and photobiology unit irradiated the backs of 12 volunteers with 320 J/cm2 of visible light plus UVA1 (370-700 nm, 3.5% UVA1) under four sunscreen products and an unprotected control, then tracked erythema and pigmentation by investigator grading, colorimetry, and diffuse reflectance spectroscopy through day 14. The tinted product containing 11% titanium dioxide and 1% iron oxides plus antioxidants "provided the highest and most consistent protection," with significantly less pigmentation than control at all time points; a second tinted product with 12% zinc oxide, organic filters, and 4% iron oxides "also showed some efficacy," but "since the product with 1% iron oxides was superior to the product with 4% iron oxides, further studies are needed to evaluate whether iron oxide content correlates with better protection."[^ezekwe] *Tinted sunscreens beat non-tinted sunscreens for protecting against combined UVA1/visible-light pigmentation, but the study's own finding that less iron oxide outperformed more undercuts a simple dose-response story for the ingredient.*

**Iron-oxide-containing foundations versus mineral sunscreen, visible-light-induced pigmentation, skin of color (topical, industry study).** Researchers compared two iron-oxide-containing foundation formulations against a non-tinted mineral SPF 50-plus sunscreen and untreated skin for preventing visible-light-induced pigmentation in Fitzpatrick IV subjects. "Expert grading and colorimetry demonstrated that the iron-oxide containing formulations significantly protected against visible light-induced pigmentation compared to untreated skin or mineral SPF 50+ sunscreen."[^dumbuya] *An industry-funded study found iron-oxide-tinted foundation outperformed even a mineral (zinc oxide/titanium dioxide) sunscreen against visible-light pigmentation, consistent with the mechanism, but funding source and lack of independent replication temper the claim.*

## Common Marketing Claims

- **"Tinted sunscreen protects melasma-prone skin better than regular sunscreen."** Directionally supported: the one RCT isolating this found a real, if modest (15 to 28%), additional improvement over UV-only sunscreen when both were paired with hydroquinone, and two further studies (one academic, one industry) found tinted, iron-oxide-containing products outperformed non-tinted sunscreen against experimentally induced visible-light pigmentation.[^castanedo][^ezekwe][^dumbuya] It is not evidence that iron oxide alone, without an active lightening agent, meaningfully treats existing melasma.
- **"More iron oxide means more visible-light protection."** Not supported by the available data. The one controlled irradiation study that compared iron oxide concentrations found the 1%-iron-oxide product outperformed the 4%-iron-oxide product, and the authors explicitly said the concentration-protection relationship is unresolved.[^ezekwe] Formulation (particle size, other filters, total pigment load) likely matters more than the iron oxide percentage on a label.
