Vitamin C on a skincare label can mean several different molecules. The reference form is L-ascorbic acid, the pure unmodified vitamin and the only form the skin can use without first converting it. Because L-ascorbic acid is unstable in water and oxidizes readily, formulators also use a set of chemically modified derivatives (the ascorbyl phosphates, ascorbyl glucoside, an ethylated form, and lipid-soluble esters) that are more stable but must be converted back to ascorbic acid in or on the skin to do the same work. The forms are not interchangeable: they differ in how many conversion steps separate them from ascorbic acid, and in how much controlled human evidence stands behind each. This hub maps the family by conversion pathway, by form, and by strength of evidence, and links to the full profile for the reference form, [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]].

> The vitamin C forms share one active endpoint, L-ascorbic acid, but the evidence behind them is uneven. L-ascorbic acid is the reference benchmark and the only form biologically active without conversion; Pinnell and colleagues worked out the low-pH, high-concentration formulation it needs to reach the skin. The derivatives were built to solve its instability, but Pullar, Carr and Vissers' review reports that the phosphate forms convert to ascorbic acid slowly and are poorly absorbed, and that conversion of the lipid-soluble esters is in doubt. Most derivatives carry far less controlled human efficacy data than L-ascorbic acid.

## The Conversion Pathway

The skin uses vitamin C as ascorbic acid. Pullar, Carr and Vissers state that "it is only when pH levels are below 4 and vitamin C is present as ascorbic acid that some penetration occurs."[^pullar] In that form the vitamin acts as a cofactor for collagen synthesis and as an antioxidant: the same review describes vitamin C as "a co-factor for the proline and lysine hydroxylases that stabilise the collagen molecule tertiary structure," one that "also promotes collagen gene expression."[^pullar]

Every other form is a modified version that has to shed its modifying group and become ascorbic acid before it can do this. The chain runs:

- derivative (a phosphate salt, a glucoside, an ether, or a lipid ester) -> L-ascorbic acid -> collagen cofactor and antioxidant activity

Each conversion is a place where activity can be lost, and each derivative sits at a different point. Pullar, Carr and Vissers describe the trade for the phosphate salts: "the addition of a phosphate group confers greater stability and these derivatives may be converted to ascorbic acid in vivo, albeit at a slow rate, but they are poorly absorbed through the skin."[^pullar] For ascorbyl glucoside they note it "also exhibits superior stability and can penetrate, but the rate of its in vivo conversion is not known."[^pullar] For the lipid-soluble esters they record more doubt: derivatives with lipid-soluble groups "such as palmitate" have shown increased uptake in animals but "there is some doubt as to whether these derivatives are efficiently converted in vivo."[^pullar] As a rule, a form's benefit depends on how reliably and how completely it converts to ascorbic acid in living skin, and for most derivatives that conversion is slow, poorly absorbed, or unquantified.

## The Forms

Grouped as the reference form versus the derivatives that modify it for stability.

The reference form:

- L-ascorbic acid (LAA), also written ascorbic acid or pure vitamin C: the unmodified vitamin and the only form biologically active without conversion. It is also the least stable, oxidizing readily in water and air, which is the problem the derivatives are built to solve.[^stamford] Pinnell and colleagues established the formulation it needs. In percutaneous absorption studies, "L-ascorbic acid must be formulated at pH levels less than 3.5 to enter the skin," the "maximal concentration for optimal percutaneous absorption was 20%," tissue levels "were saturated after three daily applications," and "the half-life of tissue disappearance was about 4 days."[^pinnell] Its full profile is at [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]].

The stabilized derivatives (each has to convert to ascorbic acid to act):

- Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate (MAP) and sodium ascorbyl phosphate (SAP): phosphate salts of ascorbic acid, more stable than L-ascorbic acid and usable nearer neutral pH, but converted to ascorbic acid slowly and poorly absorbed through skin.[^pullar] MAP is the one derivative here with a published clinical pigment study.[^kameyama]
- Ascorbyl glucoside (AA2G): ascorbic acid bound to a glucose molecule, stable and able to penetrate, with an in-vivo conversion rate the review describes as not known.[^pullar]
- 3-O-ethyl ascorbic acid (ethyl ascorbic acid, EAC): an ether of ascorbic acid carrying an ethyl group at the 3 position. It is one of the "commonly utilized, commercially available derivatives" surveyed in the derivative literature.[^stamford]
- Ascorbyl palmitate and tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate (ascorbyl tetraisopalmitate, THD ascorbate): lipid-soluble esters designed to move through the skin's fatty barrier. Increased uptake has been demonstrated in animals, but the same review flags doubt over whether they are efficiently converted to ascorbic acid in vivo.[^pullar]

## The Rubric

Grades below are relative, name comparators, and rank evidence strength rather than potency alone. L-ascorbic acid is the benchmark, as tretinoin is for the retinoids.

Best-evidenced form:

- L-ascorbic acid: the reference form and the only one biologically active without conversion. It carries the most controlled human topical vitamin C data, and it is the active in the best-known formulation and photoprotection studies (see [[skinceuticals-c-e-ferulic]]). Its own effect grades are set on its profile, [[ascorbic-acid-vitamin-c]].

One derivative with a clinical pigment study:

- Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate: modest depigmenting signal, preliminary evidence. Kameyama and colleagues reported that VC-PMG, "a stable derivative of AsA," produced a lightening effect "significant in 19 of 34 patients with chloasma or senile freckles" against 3 of 25 patients with normal skin.[^kameyama] A single open cohort on pigmentation, not a vehicle-controlled trial.

Stable but thinly evidenced derivatives:

- Sodium ascorbyl phosphate, ascorbyl glucoside, 3-O-ethyl ascorbic acid, ascorbyl palmitate, and tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate: effect versus L-ascorbic acid unresolvable in isolation, evidence preliminary. They are more stable than L-ascorbic acid, but their skin benefit depends on conversion that Pullar, Carr and Vissers describe as slow and poorly absorbed for the phosphates, unknown in rate for the glucoside, and doubtful for the lipid esters.[^pullar] Much of the derivative data is in-vitro stability, penetration, and conversion work rather than controlled human efficacy trials.[^pullar][^stamford]

## The Evidence

The honest differentiator across the family is not which molecule is trendiest but how much controlled human evidence stands behind each form.

L-ascorbic acid has the most. It is the only form active without conversion, and its formulation was worked out in controlled percutaneous absorption studies: Pinnell and colleagues found it "must be formulated at pH levels less than 3.5 to enter the skin," with optimal absorption at 20% and tissue saturation after three daily applications.[^pinnell] It is also the active in the published topical vitamin C photoprotection work, though those studies tested it combined with vitamin E and ferulic acid and were run by the formula's inventors (covered on [[skinceuticals-c-e-ferulic]]). The mechanism behind its use is characterized: Pullar, Carr and Vissers describe vitamin C as a cofactor for the collagen-stabilizing hydroxylases that also promotes collagen gene expression, and as most effective against oxidative skin damage "when it is used in conjunction with vitamin E."[^pullar] Stamford's review lists the cutaneous benefits attributed to vitamin C as "photoprotection from UV A & B, neocollagenesis, inhibition of melanogenesis and improvement of a variety of inflammatory skin disorders."[^stamford]

Among the derivatives, magnesium ascorbyl phosphate has the most direct clinical data, and it is limited. Kameyama and colleagues reported that VC-PMG "suppressed melanin formation by tyrosinase and melanoma cells" in vitro, and that in patients its lightening effect "was significant in 19 of 34 patients with chloasma or senile freckles and in 3 of 25 patients with normal skin."[^kameyama] That is a single open cohort on pigmentation, not a vehicle-controlled aging trial.

For the remaining derivatives the evidence is thinner and mostly upstream of efficacy. The stability advantage is real, but Pullar, Carr and Vissers note that the phosphate forms convert to ascorbic acid "at a slow rate" and are "poorly absorbed through the skin," that ascorbyl glucoside's "in vivo conversion is not known," and that for the lipid-soluble palmitate-type esters "there is some doubt as to whether these derivatives are efficiently converted in vivo."[^pullar] Stamford frames the whole derivative set as commercially motivated by L-ascorbic acid's "instability" and the "difficulties associated with its topical delivery."[^stamford] The derivatives answer the stability problem; whether each delivers ascorbic-acid-level benefit in skin rests largely on penetration and conversion data rather than controlled human outcome trials.[^pullar][^stamford]

## Common Marketing Claims

Treat everything here as unproven or overstated unless it appears in The Evidence above.

- **"Contains vitamin C, so it works like vitamin C."** The form matters. A derivative is not ascorbic acid until the skin converts it, and Pullar, Carr and Vissers record that the phosphate derivatives are "poorly absorbed through the skin," that ascorbyl glucoside's conversion rate "is not known," and that conversion of the lipid esters is in doubt.[^pullar] The words "vitamin C" on a label do not by themselves establish L-ascorbic-acid-level delivery.
- **"More stable, so better."** Stability and skin benefit are separate axes. The derivatives are more stable than L-ascorbic acid by design,[^stamford] but longer shelf life does not demonstrate greater effect in skin, and for most derivatives the controlled human efficacy data is thinner than for L-ascorbic acid.[^pullar]
- **"This derivative brightens or builds collagen like pure vitamin C."** The collagen-cofactor and antioxidant mechanism is characterized for ascorbic acid itself.[^pullar] Among the derivatives, only magnesium ascorbyl phosphate has a published clinical pigment result, from a single open cohort.[^kameyama] Equivalence to L-ascorbic acid is not established for the other derivatives in controlled human trials.
- **"No low-pH formula needed."** For the phosphate salts this is a genuine formulation difference, since they are used nearer neutral pH, but it comes with the same review's finding that they convert slowly and are poorly absorbed.[^pullar] Freedom from the low pH that L-ascorbic acid requires, below 3.5, is a stability convenience rather than evidence of effect.[^pinnell][^pullar]
